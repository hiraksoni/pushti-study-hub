# Chapter 4: Case — Noun and Pronoun

**Subject**: English Grammar | **Module**: Gram_Ch04 | **Curriculum**: CBSE Standard Coursebook | **Version**: 1.0

## Executive Summary
This dossier covers the grammatical Case system for nouns and pronouns in English. It provides exhaustive theoretical principles and exercises for the Nominative (Subjective) Case, Objective (Accusative) Case, Dative Case (Indirect Object), Possessive (Genitive) Case with apostrophe orthography, Vocative Case (direct address), and advanced syntactic case concord in comparisons and compound constructions.

## Core Themes & Competencies
- Identification of Nominative (Subjective) case as the actor, subject of finite verbs, or predicate nominative
- Recognition of Objective (Accusative) case as the direct receiver of transitive verbs or object of prepositions
- Distinguishing the Dative case (indirect object beneficiary) from the Accusative case (direct object entity)
- Mastery of Possessive (Genitive) case formation with apostrophe rules for singular nouns (-'s), regular plural nouns (-s'), irregular plurals (-'s), compound nouns, and joint vs separate ownership
- Application of correct pronoun case forms in compound subjects (Rohan and I) versus compound objects (Rohan and me)
- Resolution of elliptical comparisons with 'than' and 'as' (taller than I [am]), and prepositional object government (between you and me)

## Theoretical Foundations & Grammatical Architecture

### 1. Nominative (Subjective) Case
A noun or pronoun is in the Nominative Case when it functions as the subject of a finite verb—the entity performing the action or being described (e.g., 'The surgeon performed the operation'; 'She solved the puzzle'). To locate the nominative subject, ask 'Who?' or 'What?' before the verb. It also functions as a Predicate Nominative after linking verbs like 'be' (e.g., 'It is I'; 'This was he').

### 2. Objective (Accusative) & Dative Cases
• Objective (Accusative) Case: Governs direct objects receiving the action of a transitive verb (ask 'Whom?' or 'What?' after the verb: 'He rescued the kitten') and nouns/pronouns following prepositions ('across the ocean', 'with him').
• Dative Case (Indirect Object): The person or entity to whom or for whom the action is performed, positioned between the verb and direct object ('Father bought me a book'; 'She gave her brother a gift'). Paraphrasable with preposition 'to' or 'for'.

### 3. Possessive (Genitive) Case & Apostrophe Rules
Denotes ownership, origin, or authorship:
1. Singular Nouns: Add -'s (Anna's pot, Charles's car, child's toy).
2. Regular Plural Nouns ending in -s: Add apostrophe only (teachers' room, boys' school).
3. Irregular Plural Nouns not ending in -s: Add -'s (children's books, women's rights, men's wear).
4. Inanimate Nouns: Standardly express possession using 'of the...' rather than apostrophes ('the leg of the table', 'the roof of the house').
5. Joint vs Separate Possession: Joint ownership uses apostrophe on the last noun ('Ravi and Sita's house'); separate ownership marks each noun ('Ravi's and Sita's cars').

### 4. Complete Pronoun Declension Master Chart
Pronouns shift morphologically across cases:
• 1st Person: Subj = I, we; Obj/Dat = me, us; Poss. Adj = my, our; Poss. Pron = mine, ours.
• 2nd Person: Subj = you; Obj/Dat = you; Poss. Adj = your; Poss. Pron = yours.
• 3rd Person Masc: Subj = he; Obj/Dat = him; Poss. Adj = his; Poss. Pron = his.
• 3rd Person Fem: Subj = she; Obj/Dat = her; Poss. Adj = her; Poss. Pron = hers.
• 3rd Person Neuter: Subj = it; Obj/Dat = it; Poss. Adj = its; Poss. Pron = its (rare).
• 3rd Person Plural: Subj = they; Obj/Dat = them; Poss. Adj = their; Poss. Pron = theirs.
• Relative/Interrogative: Subj = who; Obj = whom; Poss = whose.

### 5. Special Case Rules & Common Pitfalls
• Prepositions Govern Objective Case: Always 'between you and me' (never 'between you and I').
• Compound Subjects vs Objects: Test by isolating the pronoun ('Rohan and I went' because 'I went'; 'Invited Rohan and me' because 'Invited me').
• Comparisons with Than/As: Take nominative pronouns in formal English because a verb is implied ('He is faster than I [am]').
• Vocative Case: Nouns used in direct address, set off by commas ('Listen closely, dear students').

### 76. [eng_gram4_la_001] Provide a comprehensive comparative analysis of the four primary grammatical cases in English: Nominative, Objective (Accusative/Dative), Possessive (Genitive), and Vocative. Discuss their syntactic diagnostics and structural functions with two illustrative examples each.
- **Type**: `long_answer` | **Difficulty**: `hard` | **Marks**: 5 | **Topic**: Case Architecture in English
- **Answer**: The case system in English reveals the syntactic relationship of a noun or pronoun to other elements in a clause:
1. Nominative (Subjective) Case:
• Function: Acts as the subject performing the action of a finite verb, or as a predicate nominative renaming the subject following a copular verb.
• Diagnostic: Answers 'Who?' or 'What?' before the verb.
• Examples: 'The doctor arrived promptly'; 'It was she who called'.

2. Objective Case (Accusative & Dative):
• Accusative Function: Direct object directly receiving verbal action, or object governed by a preposition.
• Dative Function: Indirect object recipient/beneficiary situated between verb and direct object.
• Diagnostic: Accusative answers 'Whom?'/'What?' after the verb; Dative answers 'To whom?'/'For whom?'.
• Examples: 'The detective questioned him' (Accusative); 'Mother baked us a chocolate cake' (Dative).

3. Possessive (Genitive) Case:
• Function: Expresses ownership, attribution, origin, or authorship.
• Diagnostic: Answers 'Whose?'. Marked by -'s / -' in nouns, and possessive determiners/pronouns in pronouns.
• Examples: 'Shakespeare's sonnets'; 'The choice was entirely hers'.

4. Vocative Case (Direct Address):
• Function: Used to address a person or personified entity directly.
• Diagnostic: Independent of sentence syntax and demarcated by commas.
• Examples: 'Listen carefully, my friends'; 'O Death, where is thy sting?'.
- **Explanation / Critical Insight**: Evaluated on clarity of syntactic definitions, diagnostic questioning methods, and illustrative sentence accuracy across all four cases.

### 77. [eng_gram4_la_002] Detail the rules governing apostrophe placement in forming the Possessive Case of nouns across: (a) singular nouns ending in sibilants, (b) regular and irregular plural nouns, (c) compound nouns, and (d) joint versus separate possession. Provide clear examples for each category.
- **Type**: `long_answer` | **Difficulty**: `hard` | **Marks**: 5 | **Topic**: Apostrophe Orthography & Joint Ownership
- **Answer**: Apostrophe orthography in English adheres to systematic morphological rules:
1. Singular Nouns Ending in Sibilants (-s, -x, -z):
• Modern standard English appends -'s to singular nouns even if they end in 's' (e.g., 'Charles's car', 'the boss's office', 'Keats's poetry'), though classical names sometimes accept an apostrophe alone in traditional usage (e.g., 'Moses' law').

2. Regular vs Irregular Plural Nouns:
• Regular plurals ending in '-s' receive only an apostrophe after the 's' (e.g., 'the girls' hostel', 'the teachers' staff room').
• Irregular plurals not ending in '-s' add -'s (e.g., 'children's playground', 'women's rights', 'geese's feathers').

3. Compound Nouns:
• The apostrophe attaches exclusively to the final word of the compound (e.g., 'my mother-in-law's advice', 'the commander-in-chief's order', 'the passer-by's umbrella').

4. Joint vs Separate Possession:
• Joint Possession (shared ownership): Only the final named person receives the apostrophe (e.g., 'Rohan and Mohan's apartment' = one shared apartment).
• Separate Possession (distinct individual ownership): Each participant receives an apostrophe (e.g., 'Rohan's and Mohan's apartments' = two distinct apartments).
- **Explanation / Critical Insight**: Evaluated on systematic presentation of singular sibilant rules, plural distinctions, compound suffixing, and the joint-versus-separate possession contrast.
