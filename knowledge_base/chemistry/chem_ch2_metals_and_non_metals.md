## 2. Core Conceptual Theory & Notes
### [chapter_notes] Physical Properties of Metals vs Non-Metals & The 10 Periodic Anomalies
1. Metals: Typically hard solids, lustrous (metallic shine), malleable (beaten into thin foils, e.g. Aluminium and Silver foils), ductile (drawn into wires, e.g. Copper and Gold), good conductors of heat and electricity, high densities, high melting/boiling points, and sonorous (produce ringing sound on strike). 2. Non-Metals: Exist in all 3 states (solids: Carbon, Sulphur, Phosphorus; liquid: Bromine; gases: Oxygen, Nitrogen, Chlorine), soft, brittle, non-malleable, non-ductile, non-sonorous, poor conductors (insulators). 3. The 10 Famous Periodic Anomalies: (a) Mercury (Hg) is a liquid metal at room temperature; (b) Gallium (Ga) and Cesium (Cs) have very low melting points and melt in human palm; (c) Sodium (Na) and Potassium (K) are soft alkali metals easily sliced with a butter knife; (d) Iodine (I₂) is a lustrous non-metal; (e) Carbon allotrope Diamond is the hardest naturally occurring substance with very high melting point; (f) Carbon allotrope Graphite conducts electricity due to delocalized electrons.

### [chapter_notes] Reactions with Atmospheric Oxygen & Amphoteric Oxides
1. Metal Oxides: Metals react with Oxygen (O₂) to produce basic metal oxides that turn moist red litmus blue: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (Magnesium oxide). Rusting of Iron: 4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (Hydrated iron(III) oxide). Both oxygen and moisture (water vapor) are mandatory for rusting. 2. Amphoteric Oxides: Oxides that exhibit dual behaviour, reacting with both acids and bases to produce salt and water (e.g. Aluminium oxide Al₂O₃, Zinc oxide ZnO): Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O, and Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ (Sodium aluminate) + H₂O. 3. Non-Metal Oxides: Non-metals react with Oxygen to form acidic oxides (acid anhydrides) that dissolve in water to produce acids, turning blue litmus red: C + O₂ → CO₂ (CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ Carbonic acid); S + O₂ → SO₂ (SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ Sulphurous acid). Neutral non-metal oxides include Water (H₂O), Carbon monoxide (CO), and Nitrous oxide (N₂O).

### [chapter_notes] Reactions with Water & The Atmospheric Storage Paradox
1. Violent Reaction (Cold Water): Potassium and Sodium react violently with cold water, producing metal hydroxides and liberating Hydrogen gas (H₂) with immense heat that immediately catches fire with a lilac/yellow flame: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ ↑ + Heat. Calcium reacts less violently; bubbles of H₂ gas stick to its surface, causing it to float. 2. Reaction with Steam: Magnesium reacts slowly with boiling water, while Aluminium, Zinc, and Iron react only with steam to produce metal oxides: 3Fe + 4H₂O (steam) → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ ↑. Copper, Silver, and Gold do not react with water or steam at all. 3. The Atmospheric Storage Paradox: (a) Sodium (Na) and Potassium (K) catch fire in contact with air and moisture, hence are stored immersed under Kerosene oil; (b) White Phosphorus (P₄) is a non-metal that spontaneously ignites in atmospheric air at 35°C, hence is stored safely immersed under Cold Water (it does not react with water).

### [chapter_notes] Reactions with Dilute Acids & The Reactivity Series
1. Reaction with Dilute Acids: Active metals react with dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl) or sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) to liberate Hydrogen gas (H₂), which burns with a characteristic 'pop' sound: Metal + Dilute Acid → Metal Salt + H₂ ↑. Example: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ ↑. Copper, Silver, and Gold do not react with dilute acids because they lie below Hydrogen in the reactivity series. 2. The Metal Reactivity Series: An arrangement of metals in decreasing order of their chemical reactivity: Potassium (K) > Sodium (Na) > Calcium (Ca) > Magnesium (Mg) > Aluminium (Al) > Zinc (Zn) > Iron (Fe) > Lead (Pb) > [Hydrogen (H)] > Copper (Cu) > Mercury (Hg) > Silver (Ag) > Gold (Au) > Platinum (Pt).

### [chapter_notes] Displacement Reactions & Industrial Metallurgy
1. Single Displacement Reaction: A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its aqueous salt solution: A + BC → AC + B. Example: When an iron nail is immersed in a blue Copper Sulphate (CuSO₄) solution, iron displaces copper; the solution turns light green due to Iron(II) Sulphate (FeSO₄) formation, and a reddish-brown coating of Copper metal deposits on the nail: Fe + CuSO₄ (blue) → FeSO₄ (green) + Cu (reddish-brown) ↓. Conversely, copper cannot displace iron from FeSO₄ solution because copper is less reactive than iron (Cu + FeSO₄ → No Reaction). 2. Thermite Welding: The highly exothermic displacement of Iron from Iron(III) oxide by Aluminium powder (Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe + Immense Heat) produces molten iron used to weld broken railway tracks and heavy machinery.

### [chapter_notes] Corrosion Prevention, Alloys & Real-World Applications
1. Corrosion: Slow deterioration of metal surfaces by air, moisture, and chemical action. Prevention methods: (a) Painting and Greasing; (b) Galvanization: Coating iron with a protective layer of molten Zinc (even if the zinc coating is scratched, zinc corrodes preferentially because it is more electropositive than iron); (c) Electroplating (Chromium/Tin plating); (d) Anodizing aluminium. 2. Alloys: Homogeneous mixtures of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal, engineered for superior strength and corrosion resistance: (a) Steel (Iron + Carbon); (b) Stainless Steel (Iron + Chromium + Nickel + Carbon — does not rust); (c) Brass (Copper + Zinc); (d) Bronze (Copper + Tin); (e) Solder (Lead + Tin — low melting point for electrical circuits); (f) 22 Karat Gold (Alloyed with Copper or Silver to provide hardness for jewellery).
