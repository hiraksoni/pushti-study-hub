## Comprehensive Theory & Chapter Architecture

### Chemical Definition, Classification & Properties of Acids (Pages 3-5)

1. Concept of Acids: The term 'acid' is derived from the Latin word 'acere', which means sour. Acids are chemical substances that possess a sour taste, turn blue litmus paper red, are corrosive in nature, and conduct electricity in aqueous solutions.

2. Natural Sources and Organic Acids:
   - Citric acid: Citrus fruits such as lemons and oranges.
   - Acetic acid: Vinegar.
   - Lactic acid: Curd and sour milk.
   - Tartaric acid: Tamarind, grapes, and unripe mangoes.
   - Malic acid / Maleic acid: Apples.
   - Oxalic acid: Spinach and tomatoes.
   - Methanoic acid (Formic acid, HCOOH): Ant stings and nettle stings.

3. Competition Window & Classification:
   - Arrhenius Dissociation: In aqueous solution, an acid dissociates to release hydrogen ions (H⁺) or hydronium ions (H₃O⁺):
     HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
   - Non-acidic Hydrogen Compounds: Substances like glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) and ethanol (C₂H₅OH) contain hydrogen atoms but do not dissociate in water to produce H⁺ ions; hence, they do not exhibit acidic properties.
   - Mineral (Inorganic) vs Organic Acids: Mineral acids are prepared from minerals of the earth (e.g., hydrochloric acid HCl, sulphuric acid H₂SO₄, nitric acid HNO₃, and carbonic acid H₂CO₃). Organic acids are naturally occurring in plants and animals.

4. Strength and Basicity of Acids:
   - Strong Acids: Dissociate completely in aqueous solution to yield a high concentration of H⁺ ions (e.g., HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃).
   - Weak Acids: Dissociate only partially in aqueous solution (e.g., CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃, citric acid, oxalic acid).
   - Concentrated vs Dilute: Concentrated acid contains a minimal amount of water, whereas dilute acid contains a relatively large proportion of water. Dilution is always performed by adding concentrated acid slowly to water with constant stirring, never water to acid, as the process is highly exothermic.
   - Basicity: The number of replaceable hydrogen ions (H⁺) furnished by one molecule of an acid in water:
     * Monobasic: HCl, HNO₃, CH₃COOH (furnishes 1 H⁺ ion)
     * Dibasic: H₂SO₄, H₂CO₃, oxalic acid (furnishes 2 H⁺ ions)
     * Tribasic: Phosphoric acid H₃PO₄ (furnishes 3 H⁺ ions)

5. Uses of Acids:
   - Sulphuric Acid (H₂SO₄): Known as the 'King of Chemicals' because of its extensive industrial use in manufacturing fertilizers (ammonium sulphate, superphosphate), paints, dyes, synthetic fibres, plastics, detergents, and lead storage batteries.
   - Nitric Acid (HNO₃): Used in making fertilizers (ammonium nitrate), explosives like TNT (trinitrotoluene) and nitroglycerine, and in the purification of noble metals like gold and silver.
   - Hydrochloric Acid (HCl): Used for cleaning sanitary ware and tiles (descaling boilers), pickling of steel, leather tanning, and textile dyeing.

6. 'Do You Know?' Callout Facts:
   - Fatty acids (stearic acid, oleic acid, palmitic acid) are organic acids present in animal fats and plant oils.
   - DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) in human cells stores genetic information and heredity traits.
   - Amino acids are the fundamental building blocks of proteins in living tissues.
   - Human gastric juice contains approximately 0.5% hydrochloric acid, essential for digestion and eliminating bacteria.
   - The dense, opaque atmosphere of Venus is dominated by thick clouds of sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄).

### Chemical Definition, Classification & Properties of Bases and Alkalies (Pages 5-7)

1. Concept of Bases: Bases are chemical substances that have a bitter taste, feel slippery or soapy to touch, and turn red litmus paper blue. In aqueous solutions, bases release hydroxide ions (OH⁻):
   NaOH(s) --Water--> Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

2. Alkalies: Water-soluble bases are designated as alkalies. All alkalies are bases, but all bases are not alkalies (e.g., copper(II) hydroxide Cu(OH)₂ and ferric hydroxide Fe(OH)₃ are bases but insoluble in water, hence not alkalies).

3. Common and Chemical Names of Bases:
   - Caustic soda: Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)
   - Caustic potash: Potassium hydroxide (KOH)
   - Milk of magnesia: Magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)₂)
   - Slaked lime: Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂)
   - Quicklime: Calcium oxide (CaO)
   - Ammonium hydroxide: NH₄OH

4. Strength and Acidity of Bases:
   - Strong Bases: Ionize completely in water to yield a high concentration of OH⁻ ions (e.g., NaOH, KOH).
   - Weak Bases: Ionize only partially in aqueous solution (e.g., NH₄OH, Mg(OH)₂, Ca(OH)₂).
   - Acidity of a Base: The number of replaceable hydroxide ions (OH⁻) furnished by one molecule of a base:
     * Monoacidic: NaOH, KOH (furnishes 1 OH⁻ ion)
     * Diacidic: Ca(OH)₂, Mg(OH)₂ (furnishes 2 OH⁻ ions)
     * Triacidic: Aluminium hydroxide Al(OH)₃, Ferric hydroxide Fe(OH)₃ (furnishes 3 OH⁻ ions)

5. Uses of Bases:
   - Calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂]: Used for whitewashing buildings, neutralizing acidic agricultural soil, and manufacturing bleaching powder.
   - Sodium hydroxide [NaOH]: Used in the manufacture of soaps, detergents, rayon, paper, and refining petroleum.
   - Ammonium hydroxide [NH₄OH]: Used in window cleaners, household cleaning solutions for dissolving grease, and in dyes and fertilizers.
   - Magnesium hydroxide [Mg(OH)₂]: Used as an antacid (Milk of Magnesia) to neutralize excess gastric acidity.

6. 'Do You Know?' Callout Facts:
   - Lime water is a clear solution of calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂], whereas lime juice comes from the citrus fruit (Citrus aurantifolia) containing citric acid.

### Indicators: Natural, Olfactory, and Synthetic (Pages 7-11)

1. Definition: Indicators are special chemical substances that display characteristic color or odor changes in acidic, basic, or neutral media.

2. Natural Indicators:
   - Litmus: Extracted from lichens (a symbiotic composite organism of fungus and algae). In distilled water (neutral), it shows a mauve/purple color. In acidic solution, it turns RED; in basic solution, it turns BLUE.
   - Red Rose Indicator: Prepared by boiling red rose petals in warm water. Turns RED in acidic solutions, GREEN in basic solutions, and shows NO CHANGE in neutral solutions.
   - China Rose (Gudhal) Indicator: Warm water extract of petals forms a light pink solution. Turns DARK PINK / MAGENTA in acidic solutions, and GREEN in basic solutions.
   - Turmeric (Haldi): Contains the natural yellow pigment curcumin. Remains YELLOW in acidic and neutral solutions, but turns RED / REDDISH-BROWN in basic solutions.
   - Red Cabbage Indicator: Extracted from purple-red cabbage leaves. Turns RED in acidic solutions and GREEN in basic solutions.

3. Olfactory Indicators:
   - Substances whose characteristic odor changes when exposed to acidic or basic media.
   - Examples: Onion extract, vanilla essence, and clove oil. In basic media, their characteristic smell is destroyed/undetectable, whereas in acidic media their aroma persists.

4. Synthetic Indicators:
   - Phenolphthalein: Remains COLOURLESS in acidic and neutral solutions; turns PINK in basic solutions.
   - Methyl Orange: Orange in neutral solution; turns RED in acidic solutions; turns YELLOW in basic solutions.

5. Summary Colour Response Table:
   | Indicator | Acidic Medium | Basic Medium | Neutral Medium |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Blue litmus paper | Turns red | Remains blue | Remains blue |
   | Red litmus paper | Remains red | Turns blue | Remains red |
   | Turmeric paper / solution | Yellow | Red / Reddish-brown | Yellow |
   | China rose extract | Dark pink (Magenta) | Green | Light pink (No change) |
   | Red rose extract | Red | Green | No change |
   | Red cabbage extract | Red | Green | Purple |
   | Phenolphthalein | Colourless | Pink | Colourless |
   | Methyl orange | Red | Yellow | Orange |

6. 'Do You Know?' Callout Facts:
   - Hydrangea macrophylla flowers act as natural pH indicators: they bloom blue in acidic soils and pink/red in basic/alkaline soils.

### Neutralisation Reactions & Practical Everyday Applications (Pages 12-14)

1. Chemical Mechanism of Neutralisation:
   - Neutralisation is a chemical reaction between an acid and a base that results in the formation of a salt and water, accompanied by the evolution of heat:
     Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat
     HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + Heat
     H₂SO₄(aq) + 2KOH(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l) + Heat
   - Net Ionic Reaction: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

2. Neutralisation in Everyday Life:
   a) Acidity and Indigestion:
      - Overeating or stress causes the stomach to secrete excessive hydrochloric acid (HCl), causing burning sensation, pain, and acid indigestion.
      - Treatment: Administration of mild bases called antacids (such as Milk of Magnesia [Mg(OH)₂], magnesium carbonate [MgCO₃], or sodium bicarbonate [NaHCO₃]) to neutralize excess acid:
        Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
   b) Soil Treatment for Agriculture:
      - Excessive use of chemical fertilizers often makes the soil excessively acidic, stunting plant growth. To neutralize acidic soil, farmers treat it with basic compounds such as quicklime (calcium oxide, CaO) or slaked lime (calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂).
      - If soil becomes excessively alkaline/basic, decaying organic matter (manure/compost) is incorporated; the acids released by decomposition neutralize the soil alkalinity.
   c) Insect Bites and Stings:
      - Ant / Honeybee Sting: Injects acidic formic acid (methanoic acid, HCOOH) into the dermal tissue, causing burning pain and inflammation. Treatment requires applying a mild base such as moist baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO₃) or calamine lotion (containing zinc carbonate, ZnCO₃).
      - Wasp Sting: Injects an alkaline liquid. Treatment requires applying a mild acid, such as household vinegar (acetic acid) or lemon juice, to neutralize the alkali.
   d) Industrial and Factory Waste Treatment:
      - Industrial effluents and liquid chemical wastes frequently contain toxic mineral acids. If discharged directly into rivers, lakes, or ponds, the water becomes acidic, killing fish and aquatic flora/fauna. Factory wastes are neutralised by adding basic substances (slaked lime) prior to environmental discharge.
   e) Prevention of Tooth Decay:
      - Oral bacteria decompose residual dietary sugars and food particles stuck between teeth, releasing lactic and other organic acids. These acids attack tooth enamel (hydroxyapatite). Brushing teeth regularly with alkaline toothpaste neutralizes the mouth acids, preventing cavity formation and decay.

### Salts, pH Scale, Acid Rain & Water of Crystallisation (Pages 14-17)

1. Nature and Nomenclature of Salts:
   - Salts are ionic compounds formed by the neutralisation reaction between an acid and a base.
   - Salt naming rule: The first part of the salt's name is derived from the base (the cation), and the second part is derived from the acid (the anion):
     * Sodium chloride (NaCl) is derived from sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and hydrochloric acid (HCl).
     * Potassium nitrate (KNO₃) is derived from potassium hydroxide (KOH) and nitric acid (HNO₃).

2. Classification of Salts:
   - Neutral Salts: Formed by the reaction of a strong acid and a strong base. Aqueous solutions are neutral with pH = 7.
     Examples: Sodium chloride (NaCl), potassium chloride (KCl), sodium sulphate (Na₂SO₄), potassium nitrate (KNO₃).
   - Acidic Salts: Formed by the neutralisation of a strong acid and a weak base. Aqueous solutions are acidic with pH < 7.
     Examples: Ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl), copper sulphate (CuSO₄), ammonium sulphate ((NH₄)₂SO₄), ferric chloride (FeCl₃).
   - Basic Salts: Formed by the neutralisation of a weak acid and a strong base. Aqueous solutions are basic/alkaline with pH > 7.
     Examples: Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃), sodium acetate (CH₃COONa), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃).

3. pH Scale and Biochemical Significance:
   - The pH scale measures the relative concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in an aqueous medium.
   - Scale spans 0 to 14: pH < 7 indicates acidic nature (lower pH = higher acidity); pH = 7 indicates neutral; pH > 7 indicates basic nature (higher pH = higher basicity).
   - Biological Significance: Human blood must be maintained within a tight physiological buffer range of pH 7.35 to 7.45. Any significant deviation disrupts cellular enzymes and induces severe illness.

4. Acid Rain:
   - Combustion of fossil fuels releases acidic gaseous pollutants into the atmosphere: carbon dioxide (CO₂), sulphur dioxide (SO₂), and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂).
   - These react with atmospheric moisture to form dilute acids:
     CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (Carbonic acid)
     SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (Sulphurous acid); 2SO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 2H₂SO₄ (Sulphuric acid)
     4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃ (Nitric acid)
   - Effects: Acid rain corrodes limestone and marble monuments (CaCO₃), leaches essential nutrients from soil, and acidifies freshwater bodies, destroying aquatic ecosystems.

5. Properties and Uses of Salts:
   - Reaction with Acids: NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl; 2NaCl + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2HCl
   - Reaction with Bases: (NH₄)₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O + 2NH₃↑
   - Reaction with Metals: Fe + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
   - Uses of Common Salts:
     * Sodium chloride (NaCl, common salt): Culinary seasoning, food preservative, tanning, manufacturing soap (salting out), chlor-alkali industry.
     * Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃, washing soda): Manufacture of glass, soap, paper, and industrial water softening.
     * Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃, baking soda): Baking powder formulation, antacid tablets, soda-acid fire extinguishers.
     * Copper sulphate (CuSO₄·5H₂O, blue vitriol): Agricultural fungicide (Bordeaux mixture) and copper electroplating.

6. 'Do You Know?' Callout Facts:
   - Salting Out of Soap: In soap manufacturing, common salt (NaCl) is added to the boiled mixture of fats and alkali; NaCl decreases soap solubility, forcing it to precipitate and solidify at the surface.
   - Water of Crystallisation: The fixed stoichiometric number of water molecules chemically bonded in a salt crystal structure, imparting geometric crystal form and color:
     * Ferrous sulphate heptahydrate: FeSO₄·7H₂O (green vitriol)
     * Copper sulphate pentahydrate: CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue vitriol)
     * Sodium carbonate decahydrate: Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (washing soda)
   - Hydrated vs Anhydrous Salts: Crystalline salts containing water of crystallisation are hydrated; when heated strongly, they lose water and turn into amorphous, colourless powders termed anhydrous salts.
   - Deliquescent Salts: Salts that absorb moisture from the air and dissolve in it to form a liquid solution (e.g., anhydrous CaCl₂, MgCl₂, solid NaOH).
   - Efflorescent Salts: Hydrated crystalline salts that lose their water of crystallisation when exposed to air, crumbling into powder (e.g., Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, FeSO₄·7H₂O).

### Textbook Illustrations (Illustrations 1 to 10) (Pages 12-16)

Illustration 1: Why does curd taste sour whereas baking soda tastes bitter?
Ans: Curd contains lactic acid which imparts a sour taste, whereas baking soda contains sodium hydrogen carbonate (a base) which imparts a bitter taste.

Illustration 2: Why does a yellow curry stain on a white shirt turn red when washed with soap?
Ans: The curry stain contains turmeric (a natural indicator). Soap is alkaline/basic in nature; turmeric indicator turns red in a basic medium.

Illustration 3: A drop of lemon juice is put on red litmus paper, and a drop of lime water is put on blue litmus paper. What are the observations?
Ans: Lemon juice is acidic, so red litmus paper shows no change. Lime water is basic, so blue litmus paper shows no change.

Illustration 4: How will phenolphthalein and methyl orange behave when added to vinegar and caustic soda?
Ans: Vinegar (acid): Phenolphthalein remains colourless; methyl orange turns red. Caustic soda (base): Phenolphthalein turns pink; methyl orange turns yellow.

Illustration 5: Classify the following acids on the basis of their basicity: HCl, H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄, HNO₃, H₂CO₃.
Ans: Monobasic: HCl, HNO₃. Dibasic: H₂SO₄, H₂CO₃. Tribasic: H₃PO₄.

Illustration 6: What happens when dilute sulphuric acid and lime water are mixed together?
Ans: Neutralisation reaction occurs. Lime water [Ca(OH)₂] reacts with sulphuric acid [H₂SO₄] to form calcium sulphate [CaSO₄] salt, water, and heat:
Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CaSO₄(s) + 2H₂O(l) + Heat

Illustration 7: Sodium hydroxide is taken in a test tube and a few drops of phenolphthalein are added to it. Then dilute hydrochloric acid is added dropwise. What will you observe and why? What will happen if an excess of acid is added?
Ans: The solution initially turns pink upon adding phenolphthalein due to basic NaOH. When dilute HCl is added dropwise, the pink colour gradually fades and eventually disappears as neutralisation occurs. If excess acid is added, the solution remains colourless because phenolphthalein is colourless in acidic medium.

Illustration 8: Give the chemical name and formula of any two salts.
Ans: (i) Sodium chloride, NaCl; (ii) Copper sulphate, CuSO₄.

Illustration 9: Potassium nitrate is a salt. Give the names of the acid and base used to form this salt.
Ans: Potassium nitrate (KNO₃) is formed from potassium hydroxide (KOH, base) and nitric acid (HNO₃, acid):
KOH(aq) + HNO₃(aq) → KNO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)

Illustration 10: Explain why an aqueous solution of sodium chloride is neutral.
Ans: Sodium chloride is formed by the reaction between a strong acid (HCl) and a strong base (NaOH). Both ions dissociate completely without hydrolyzing, producing equal concentrations of H⁺ and OH⁻ ions; hence the solution is neutral with pH = 7.
