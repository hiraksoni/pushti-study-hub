🔋 Electric Charges & Current

⚡ What is Electric Current?

The rate of flow of electric charges through a conductor is called electric current. It is denoted by I.

I = Q / t  |  SI Unit: Ampere (A)

Electric charges are of two types: positive (+) and negative (−). Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.

💡 Charge of one electron = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ Coulomb. SI unit of charge is Coulomb (C).
🔌 Electric Circuit & Symbols

🔁 What is an Electric Circuit?

A closed, continuous path through which an electric current flows is called an electric circuit.

Essential components: Electric cell/battery, bulb, switch (key), and connecting wires.

⚡ Electric Potential & Ohm's Law

🔺 Potential Difference (V)

Work done in moving a unit positive charge from one point to another.

V = W / Q  |  SI Unit: Volt (V)
Ohm's Law: The current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends (at constant temperature).
V = IR — where R is resistance (SI unit: Ohm, Ω)
💡 Resistance is the property of a conductor that opposes the flow of electric current.
🌡️ Heating Effect of Electric Current

♨️ Joule's Law of Heating

When electric current passes through a wire, it produces heat. This is the heating effect of current (also called Joule heating).

H = I²Rt  |  Heat ∝ I² and Heat ∝ R and Heat ∝ t
ApplianceFilament Material
Electric BulbTungsten (melting point: 3422°C)
Electric Iron, Heater, ToasterNichrome
⚠️ Heat produced in a wire depends on its material, length, and thickness.

🔌 Electric Fuse

A safety device made of an alloy of 63% tin and 37% lead with a low melting point. When excess current flows, the fuse wire melts and breaks the circuit — protecting appliances.

⚠️ MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker) is a modern alternative to fuses. It automatically switches off when excess current flows.
💡 Fuse works on the principle of the heating effect of electric current.
🧲 Magnetic Effect of Electric Current

🧲 Oersted's Discovery

In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted first showed that a current-carrying wire behaves like a magnet and deflects a compass needle. This is the magnetic effect of electric current.

Electromagnet: A coil of insulated copper wire wound around an iron rod. When current flows, the iron behaves like a magnet. When current is off, magnetism disappears — hence a temporary magnet.

Strength of electromagnet increases with:

  • More current through the coil
  • More turns of wire in the coil
  • Shorter length of the electromagnet
🔔 Electric bell works on the principle of the magnetic effect of electric current.

📡 Claude's Corner
🌟 Bonus Knowledge — Beyond the Exam! Real-world connections to deepen understanding.

⚡ Why is Lightning So Powerful?

Bonus
Lightning is gigantic electric current! A single bolt carries about 1 billion volts and around 20,000 Amperes — roughly 20,000 times more current than a household circuit! It heats the surrounding air to 30,000°C — 5 times hotter than the surface of the Sun. That's why you see the flash before you hear the thunder!
Franklin's lightning rod (1752): Benjamin Franklin proved lightning is electricity using a kite. His invention — the lightning rod — is just a conductor that safely directs lightning current to the ground. Every tall building in India uses one today. It's the world's first practical application of the magnetic/electrical effect!
Oersted's accident (1820): Hans Christian Oersted accidentally discovered the magnetic effect of current during a lecture — he noticed a compass needle deflect when placed near a current-carrying wire. This single observation launched the entire field of electromagnetism!

🔋 Why Do Batteries Die?

Bonus
Chemical reactions power cells: Inside a battery, a chemical reaction converts stored chemical energy into electrical energy. When all the reactive chemicals are used up, the reaction stops — and the battery is "dead." Rechargeable batteries reverse the reaction using electrical energy to restore the chemicals. That's why your phone can charge!
Lemon battery experiment: You can make a real working battery using lemons! Insert a zinc strip and a copper strip into a lemon — the citric acid inside acts as the electrolyte, enabling a chemical reaction between the two metals to produce a small voltage. Connect 4-5 lemons in series and you can light an LED! 🍋⚡
India's biggest battery storage project: In 2024, India commissioned one of Asia's largest battery energy storage systems in Rajasthan, capable of storing 1000+ MWh of solar energy — essentially a giant battery the size of a football field that stores solar electricity for use at night!

🌡️ Why Does Wire Heat Up?

Bonus
Electrons collide with atoms: When electrons (current) flow through a wire, they constantly collide with the metal atoms in the wire. Each collision transfers kinetic energy to the atom, making it vibrate faster — which we experience as heat! This is why resistance literally means "resistance to electron flow" — it causes these collisions.
Tungsten — the toughest metal: Tungsten melts at 3,422°C — the highest melting point of any metal! That's why bulb filaments are made of it. When electricity flows through the thin tungsten wire, it heats to about 2,500°C and glows white-hot — producing light. Modern LED bulbs skip this entirely, converting electricity directly to light using semiconductors (90% less heat wasted!).
Nichrome in your toaster: The reddish-orange glow you see in electric toasters and heaters is nichrome wire at work! Nichrome (80% nickel + 20% chromium) has high resistance and high melting point, making it perfect for heating elements. Interestingly, nichrome doesn't rust or oxidize at high temperatures — copper would burn away immediately!

🧲 Electromagnets Change the World

Bonus
MRI machines use electromagnets: The human body produces tiny electric currents from moving charged particles (ions). A very strong electromagnet in an MRI scanner interacts with these currents to create detailed images of inside your body — no X-rays, no cutting! The electromagnet in an MRI is 60,000 times stronger than Earth's magnetic field. 🏥
Maglev trains: The world's fastest trains (like Japan's SCMaglev at 603 km/h) use powerful electromagnets to levitate the train above the track — eliminating all friction! With no wheels touching rails, the only speed limit is air resistance. India is actively researching maglev trains for routes between major cities. 🚄
Electric bells in your school: The school bell you hear every day uses exactly the electromagnet circuit you study in Class 7! The electromagnet attracts the iron strip, the hammer strikes the gong, then the circuit breaks — electromagnet switches off, strip springs back, circuit reconnects, and the cycle repeats dozens of times per second to create a continuous ring.

🔌 Why Is Copper Used for Wires?

Bonus
Silver conducts better, but copper wins: Silver is actually a better conductor than copper, but copper is used in almost all electrical wiring because: (1) Copper costs about 100× less than silver, (2) Copper is stronger and doesn't break easily, (3) Copper is non-magnetic. The few exceptions — like in satellite components — use silver for its slightly better conductivity when every bit matters. 💡
India is a copper powerhouse: India has some of the world's largest copper reserves, particularly in Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, and Jharkhand. The Khetri copper complex in Rajasthan is one of India's most important copper mining sites — making India partially self-sufficient in this critical electrical material.
Why are wires covered in plastic? Plastic/rubber is an insulator — it prevents electricity from jumping from one wire to another (short circuit) and from reaching you if you touch the wire. If all household wires were bare metal, touching any wire could be fatal. The thin plastic covering is literally what makes electrical devices safe to use! ⚡→🛡️
