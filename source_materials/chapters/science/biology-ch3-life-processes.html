Chapter 03 Biology

Life Processes in Animals

Understanding Nutrition, Digestion, and Respiration in animals

📖 Chapter at a Glance

Life processes (like nutrition, respiration, and excretion) are essential for animal survival. This chapter focuses on nutrition and respiration. You will learn that animals are heterotrophic — they depend on other organisms for food. Digestion breaks complex food into simpler forms in the alimentary canal, while respiration provides energy by breaking down food using oxygen.

🎯 Learning Outcomes

  • Understand what life processes are and why they're essential
  • Learn about nutrition in animals and different modes of feeding
  • Know the parts of the human digestive system and their functions
  • Understand how digestion works in ruminants and birds
  • Learn about aerobic and anaerobic respiration
  • Know the human respiratory system and mechanism of breathing
  • Understand how respiration occurs in fish (gills), frogs (skin + lungs), and other animals
  • Know the difference between breathing and respiration

🌟 Introduction to Life Processes

What are Life Processes?

Life processes are the basic activities essential for an organism's survival on Earth. These include nutrition, respiration, excretion, and reproduction.

Heterotrophic vs Autotrophic Nutrition

  • Animals are heterotrophic, meaning they depend on other organisms (plants or other animals) for their food
  • Plants are autotrophic because they can make their own food through photosynthesis

Stages of Obtaining Nutrition

There are three main stages involved in obtaining nutrition:

  1. Intake of food: Taking in food into the body (ingestion)
  2. Digestion: Breaking down complex food particles into simple, absorbable forms
  3. Assimilation: Using the absorbed nutrients to get energy, grow, and repair the body

Food provides the essential energy needed to move, grow, repair tissues, and carry out all activities!

💡
Did You Know?

Different organisms have unique ways to intake food! A Paramecium uses tiny hair-like structures called cilia to sweep food toward its mouth. A Hydra uses tentacles covered with stinging cells to catch its prey!

🧬 Human Digestive System

The human digestive system consists of two main parts:

  • Alimentary Canal (gut or digestive tract)
  • Digestive Glands (salivary glands, liver, pancreas, etc.)

1. Mouth & Buccal Cavity 👄

  • Food enters through the mouth opening. The inner space is the buccal cavity.
  • Contains teeth and the tongue.
  • Tongue: A fleshy muscular organ attached at the back. It tastes food, moves it for chewing, mixes saliva, and helps swallow.
  • Mastication: The process of chewing food with jaws and teeth (mechanical digestion).

Types of Teeth

An adult has 32 teeth (16 per jaw). Enamel is the outer covering of teeth and is the hardest substance in the human body!

Type of Teeth Function Total in Mouth
Incisors (Front) Biting and cutting 8
Canines (Pointy) Piercing and tearing 4
Premolars (Flat) Crushing and grinding 8
Molars (Back) Crushing and grinding 12

Saliva and Digestion

Three pairs of salivary glands secrete saliva, which moistens the food. Saliva contains an enzyme called salivary amylase which breaks down starch into simple sugars (chemical digestion).

💧
Did You Know?

Your mouth produces about 1000-1500 mL (1 to 1.5 litres) of saliva every single day!

2. Oesophagus (Food Pipe) 🔽

  • A ~25 cm long muscular tube connecting the pharynx to the stomach.
  • The pharynx is a common passage for both food and air.
  • Chewed food forms a moist ball called a bolus.
  • Peristalsis: Wave-like contractions and relaxations of the oesophagus walls that push food downwards.
  • Epiglottis: A flap-like structure that closes the windpipe when we swallow to prevent food from entering it.
⚠️
Safety First!

Don't talk or laugh while eating — food particles may accidentally enter the windpipe causing choking and coughing fits!

3. Stomach 🍲

  • A J-shaped, thick-walled bag. It is the widest organ of the alimentary canal.
  • Its inner lining secretes gastric juice which contains:
    1. Hydrochloric Acid (HCl): Kills bacteria and makes the medium acidic for enzymes to work.
    2. Mucus: Protects the stomach lining from the strong acid.
    3. Pepsin (Enzyme): Breaks down proteins into simpler substances (amino acids).
  • The stomach churns food, partially digests it, and stores it for a few minutes to a few hours.
👨‍⚕️
A Fortunate Accident!

In 1822, a man named Alexis St. Martin was accidentally shot in the stomach. His doctor, William Beaumont, treated him but a hole (fistula) remained. Through this hole, Dr. Beaumont observed digestion in action and discovered that stomach digestion is primarily chemical!

4. Liver, Gall Bladder & Pancreas 🧪

  • Liver: The largest gland in the body, reddish-brown, located on the right side of the abdomen. It secretes bile juice.
  • Gall Bladder: A small sac that stores the bile juice.
  • Bile Juice: Mildly basic, it breaks down large fat globules into tiny droplets. This is called emulsification. (Note: Bile is NOT an enzyme!)
  • Pancreas: A long, pale-pink gland just behind the stomach. It secretes pancreatic juice which acts on carbohydrates, fats, and proteins.

5. Small Intestine 🌀

  • The longest portion of the alimentary canal (about 7.5 metres long!), highly coiled.
  • It receives bile juice, pancreatic juice, and its own intestinal juice.
  • Complete digestion occurs here:
    • Proteins → Amino acids
    • Carbohydrates → Simple sugars (Glucose)
    • Fats → Fatty acids + Glycerol
  • Absorption: About 90% of nutrient absorption occurs here. The inner walls have tiny finger-like projections called villi which increase the surface area for absorption.
  • Villi have thin walls containing blood capillaries and lymph vessels (lacteals). Digested food is absorbed directly into the blood!

Note: Celiac disease is an immune reaction to gluten (found in wheat/barley) which damages the villi, making it hard to absorb nutrients.

6. Large Intestine & Assimilation 🗑️

  • About 1.5 metres long. It is wider and shorter than the small intestine.
  • Function: Absorbs water and mineral salts from the remaining undigested food.
  • It houses about 100 trillion bacteria in a symbiotic relationship!
  • The semi-solid waste (faeces) is stored in the rectum and expelled through the anus. This process is called egestion.

Assimilation

Absorbed substances are transported via blood to different organs:

  • Carbohydrates (Glucose) → provides energy in cells or stored as glycogen in liver.
  • Proteins (Amino acids) → used to build body tissues, muscles, and skin.
  • Fats (Fatty acids/Glycerol) → stored in fat depots for future use.

🐄 Digestion in Other Animals

Digestion in Ruminants

Ruminants are hooved, plant-eating mammals that chew cud. Examples include cow, buffalo, goat, sheep, deer, and yak.

They have a special 4-chambered stomach:

  1. Rumen: The largest sac.
  2. Reticulum: Closely associated with the rumen; contents mix freely.
  3. Omasum: Absorbs water.
  4. Abomasum: The "true stomach" where digestive enzymes act.

The Process of Rumination:

  • The animal quickly swallows grass which goes into the rumen.
  • In the rumen, anaerobic bacteria produce an enzyme called cellulase which ferments and partially digests the grass. This partially digested food is called cud.
  • When resting, the cud is brought back to the mouth in small lumps for re-chewing. This process is called rumination.
  • After complete chewing, it passes to the omasum, then abomasum, and finally to the intestine for complete digestion.

Digestion in Birds 🦅

Birds don't have teeth! They use their beaks to pick up food and swallow it whole or in small pieces.

  • Crop: A sac-like structure where food is stored and softened.
  • Stomach: Has two parts:
    • Proventriculus: Where digestive juices are added.
    • Gizzard: A strong muscular part that grinds food, often using small stones or grit the bird has swallowed!
  • Intestines & Cloaca: Nutrients are absorbed in the intestines, and waste is expelled through the cloaca.

🌬️ Respiration in Humans

Breathing vs Respiration

They are NOT the same thing!

  • Breathing is mechanical — just taking air in and out.
  • Cellular Respiration is biochemical — the oxidation (breakdown) of glucose inside cells to release energy!

Types of Respiration

1. Aerobic Respiration

  • Takes place in the presence of oxygen.
  • Produces a large amount of energy.
  • Occurs in most organisms (aerobes).
  • Equation: Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy
  • (C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy)

2. Anaerobic Respiration

  • Takes place without oxygen.
  • In yeast and some bacteria: Glucose → Ethyl alcohol + CO₂ + Energy (small amount)
  • In muscle cells during heavy exercise: Glucose → Lactic acid + Energy
🏃
Muscle Cramps!

Have you ever had aching muscles after running fast? This is because your muscles didn't get enough oxygen, so they switched to anaerobic respiration, producing lactic acid which causes cramps!

  • Nasal Cavity: Hair and mucus trap dust and germs. Air is moistened and warmed. (Always breathe through your nose, not mouth!)
  • Lungs: Located in the chest cavity (thoracic cavity) and protected by the rib cage.
  • Diaphragm: A large, dome-shaped muscle at the floor of the thoracic cavity that helps in breathing.
  • Alveoli: Tiny balloon-like air sacs at the end of bronchioles. They are surrounded by blood capillaries. Gas exchange happens here!

Oxygen enters the blood, binds to a red pigment called haemoglobin (forming oxyhaemoglobin), and is carried to all cells. Carbon dioxide from cells is brought back to the alveoli to be breathed out.

Breathing Rate ⏱️

  • Normal Adult: 16-18 breaths per minute at rest.
  • During heavy exercise, it can increase to up to 25 breaths per minute to supply extra oxygen!
  • Infants have a much higher breathing rate than adults.
🥱
Why do we yawn?

When you are tired or bored, your breathing rate slows down. A yawn is your body's way of taking a deep breath to bring in extra oxygen to the lungs!

🫁 Mechanism of Breathing

Breathing involves two steps: Inhalation (taking air in) and Exhalation (pushing air out).

Feature Inhalation (Inspiration) 📥 Exhalation (Expiration) 📤
Ribs Move upward and outward Move downward and inward
Diaphragm Moves down (becomes flat) Moves up (becomes dome-shaped)
Thoracic Cavity Volume Increases Decreases
Air Pressure in Lungs Decreases Increases
Air Movement Rushes INTO lungs (high to low pressure) Pushed OUT of lungs

Composition of Air 💨

The air we breathe in and out has different amounts of oxygen and carbon dioxide:

  • Inhaled Air: 21% Oxygen, 0.04% Carbon dioxide
  • Exhaled Air: 16-17% Oxygen, 4-5% Carbon dioxide

We exhale less oxygen because our body uses some of it, and we exhale more carbon dioxide because it is produced as a waste product during respiration!

🚭 Warning against Smoking

Smoking damages the surface of the alveoli, drastically reducing the area available for gas exchange. It increases the risk of lung cancer and severe respiratory illnesses. Say NO to tobacco!

🐟 Respiration in Other Animals

Different animals have evolved different organs for breathing:

  • Reptiles, birds, mammals: Lungs
  • Fish: Gills
  • Earthworms: Moist skin
  • Insects (cockroach, grasshopper): Spiracles (tiny holes) and tracheae (air tubes)

Respiration through Gills (Fish) 🐠

  • Gills are special projections of the skin with many thin filaments and a rich supply of blood capillaries.
  • Water enters the fish's mouth and flows over the gills.
  • Dissolved oxygen from the water diffuses into the blood capillaries, and carbon dioxide from the blood diffuses out into the water.
🐋
Whales and Dolphins

Even though they live in water, whales and dolphins are mammals! They do not have gills. They have lungs and must come to the surface of the water to breathe air through blowholes on top of their heads!

Respiration in Frogs 🐸

Frogs are amphibians and have a fascinating respiratory system. They can breathe in three ways:

  1. Cutaneous Respiration (Skin): A frog's skin is moist and thin, allowing direct gas exchange. This works both on land and in water!
  2. Lung Respiration: Frogs have lungs, but unlike humans, they don't have ribs or a diaphragm. They use their throat to gulp and pump air into their lungs.
  3. Buccopharyngeal Cavity: Gas exchange can also happen through the lining of their mouth cavity.

🔬 Deep Dive: Beyond the Textbook

🥪 The Amazing Journey of a Sandwich

Imagine eating a delicious sandwich. The moment you take a bite, your teeth act like scissors and grinders, while saliva acts like a watery mixer. It slides down the oesophagus on a muscular wave (peristalsis). In the stomach, it takes an acid bath (strong enough to dissolve metal, but your stomach is protected by mucus!). In the small intestine, it gets showered with juices from the pancreas and liver, breaking it down to microscopic molecules that enter your blood. What's left takes a slow ride through the large intestine where water is sucked out, before being flushed away!

💨 Why Do We Burp and Fart?

Burping is just your body's way of getting rid of swallowed air. When you eat fast, talk while eating, or drink fizzy sodas, you swallow air. Farting (flatulence) is science in action! The 100 trillion bacteria in your large intestine feast on undigested food (like beans or cabbage). As they "eat," they produce gases like methane, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide. This gas builds up and has to escape somewhere!

🦠 Gut Microbiome: Your Tiny Helpers

Your large intestine is like a bustling city for over 100 trillion bacteria! This is called the gut microbiome. It's a perfect symbiotic relationship: we give them a warm place to live and food to eat, and in return, they help us digest tough plant fibres, produce vitamins (like Vitamin K), and fight off bad germs. Eating fermented foods like curd and buttermilk keeps these helpful bacteria healthy. The ancient Indian medical text Charaka Samhita also highlights the importance of gut health for overall immunity.

🐟 How Fish Breathe Underwater

Fish gills are a marvel of engineering! They use a "countercurrent exchange system." Blood in the gills flows in the opposite direction to the water flowing over them. This ensures that the blood always meets water that has a higher oxygen concentration, allowing fish to extract up to 80% of the oxygen dissolved in water! If they flowed in the same direction, they could only get about 50%.

🧘 Pranayama and Breathing Exercises

Long before modern science, ancient Indian traditions understood the power of breath control, known as Pranayama. By consciously controlling inhalation, exhalation, and retention of breath, you can increase lung capacity and calm the mind. In cold regions like Ladakh, monks practice Tummo breathing, a technique that generates enough body heat to dry wet sheets on their backs in freezing temperatures, just by controlling their breath!

🏆 Fun Digestive System Records

Longest Gut: Herbivores have much longer intestines than carnivores because plants are hard to digest.
Most Stomach Chambers: A Baird's Beaked Whale has a stomach with up to 13 chambers!
Fastest Digestion: A fruit fly digests its food in about 45 minutes, while a sloth can take up to a month to digest a single meal!

💡 Quick Recall Flashcards

Test your memory! Click on a card to reveal the answer.

What is digestion?

The process of breaking down complex food substances into simpler, absorbable forms that the body can use for energy, growth, and repair.

What is the alimentary canal?

The long tube from mouth to anus through which food travels. Also called the digestive tract or gut. Includes: buccal cavity, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, and anus.

What is peristalsis?

Wave-like muscle contractions and relaxations in the walls of the alimentary canal that push food forward. Occurs throughout the digestive tract.

Name the 4 types of teeth

1. Incisors — biting & cutting (8)
2. Canines — piercing & tearing (4)
3. Premolars — crushing (8)
4. Molars — grinding (12)
Total: 32 teeth in adults

What does saliva do?

Contains salivary amylase enzyme that breaks starch into simple sugars (chemical digestion). Also moistens food to form a bolus for easy swallowing. About 1-1.5 litres produced daily!

What is the role of HCl in the stomach?

(i) Kills bacteria in food
(ii) Makes the medium acidic for enzymes to work
(iii) Activates pepsin enzyme.
(Mucus protects the stomach wall from acid.)

What are villi?

Tiny finger-like projections on the inner walls of the small intestine. They increase the surface area for absorption of digested food. Each villus has blood vessels and a lacteal (lymph vessel).

What is a ruminant?

A cud-chewing mammal (cow, buffalo, goat, sheep) with a 4-chambered stomach: Rumen, Reticulum, Omasum, Abomasum. They quickly swallow grass and later re-chew the cud.

Name the 4 chambers of a ruminant's stomach

1. Rumen — largest, bacteria ferment food
2. Reticulum — closely linked to rumen
3. Omasum — absorbs water
4. Abomasum — true stomach, digestion with enzymes

Aerobic vs Anaerobic Respiration?

Aerobic: With O₂, produces CO₂ + H₂O + lots of energy.
Anaerobic: Without O₂, produces ethanol/lactic acid + little energy. (Happens in yeast and in muscles during heavy exercise).

What is the equation for aerobic respiration?

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy
(Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy)

What are alveoli?

Tiny balloon-like air sacs at the end of bronchioles in the lungs. Surrounded by blood capillaries. This is where gas exchange happens — O₂ enters blood, CO₂ leaves blood.

What happens during inhalation?

Ribs move UP and OUT. Diaphragm moves DOWN (flattens). Thoracic cavity volume INCREASES. Air pressure DECREASES. Air rushes INTO lungs.

How do fish breathe?

Through gills — projections of skin with filaments and blood capillaries. Water enters mouth, flows over gills. O₂ from water enters blood. CO₂ from blood exits into water.

Breathing vs Respiration?

Breathing is a MECHANICAL process (air in/out of lungs).
Respiration is a BIOCHEMICAL process (breakdown of glucose in cells to release energy). Breathing is part of respiration.
