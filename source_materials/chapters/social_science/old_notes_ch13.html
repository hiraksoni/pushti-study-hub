13.1 Meaning of Gender Social Construct

Sex is the biological difference between males and females that we are born with[cite: 5]. Gender, on the other hand, refers to the social and cultural roles, expectations, and behaviors that society expects from boys, girls, men, and women[cite: 5]. Gender is a social construct—it is learned and changes over time and across different societies[cite: 5].

Case Studies: Growing Up in Different Societies Meghalaya vs Haryana

🍃 Khasi Tribe (Meghalaya)

  • Matrilineal society where lineage and family name pass through the mother[cite: 5].
  • Daughters inherit a larger share of parental property[cite: 5].
  • Youngest daughter takes care of aging parents[cite: 5].

🌾 Traditional Haryana Household

  • Patrilineal structure where the father is the primary breadwinner[cite: 5].
  • Sons traditionally receive property inheritance to care for parents in old age[cite: 5].

💡 Takeaway

  • These contrasting examples prove that gender roles are not natural or fixed; they are created by human societies[cite: 5]!
13.2 & 13.3 Stereotypes & Devaluing Women's Work Impact on Society

🔒 What is a Stereotype?

  • A fixed, oversimplified, or biased belief about a group of people[cite: 5].
  • Examples: "Boys don't cry," "Girls are good at cooking," "Boys are better at sports/maths"[cite: 5].

🏠 Devaluing Unpaid Care Work

  • Women spend long hours cooking, cleaning, and caring for children/elders[cite: 5].
  • Society often views this as "natural duty" rather than actual work, leaving women with no monetary pay or leisure time[cite: 5].

⚖️ Double Burden

  • Working women face a double burden—earning money outside while still managing all household chores at home[cite: 5].
13.5 & 13.6 Social Reformers & Government Initiatives Fighting for Rights

📜 Key Social Reformers

  • Raja Rammohan Roy: Led movement to abolish Sati in 1829[cite: 5].
  • Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar: Fought for the Hindu Widow Remarriage Act (1856)[cite: 5].
  • Savitribai & Jyotirao Phule: Started the first Indian school for girls in Pune (1848)[cite: 5].
  • Pandita Ramabai: Founded Sarada Sadan in Mumbai for widows[cite: 5].
  • Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain: Championed Muslim girls' education[cite: 5].

🏛️ Constitutional & Legal Provisions

  • Right to Equality: Prohibits discrimination based on sex; guarantees equal pay for equal work[cite: 5].
  • Right to Education (RTE): Free, compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14[cite: 5].
  • 33% Reservation: 33% of seats in Panchayati Raj local bodies reserved for women[cite: 5].
  • Anti-Dowry & Domestic Violence Laws: Strict protection against exploitation[cite: 5].
Master Multiple Choice Questions

2. Who led the movement that successfully abolished Sati in 1829?[cite: 5]
Explanation: Raja Rammohan Roy campaigned against Sati, leading to its legal abolition in 1829[cite: 5].
3. What percentage of seats are reserved for women in Panchayati Raj local bodies?[cite: 5]

Exercise E: Answer the following questions in brief (All 5 Questions)[cite: 5]
1. What do you understand by the term gender equality? Answer in about 20–30 words.[cite: 5]
Gender equality means providing equal rights, opportunities, responsibilities, and respect to all human beings regardless of whether they are born male or female[cite: 5].

2. What are gender stereotypes? How are gender stereotypes strengthened?[cite: 5]
Gender stereotypes are fixed, biased beliefs about how men and women should behave[cite: 5]. They are strengthened when families, schools, and media reinforce rigid roles (e.g., buying toy cars for boys and dolls for girls)[cite: 5].

3. Explain why gender equality is important for us.[cite: 5]
Gender equality allows every individual to fulfill their full potential without discrimination, leading to a just, progressive, and prosperous society[cite: 5].

4. Do you think it is only the government's responsibility to ensure gender equality? Why/Why not?[cite: 5]
No, it is a shared responsibility of both the government and individuals[cite: 5]. While the government passes laws, individuals and families must break everyday biases at home and in public[cite: 5].

5. List any three ways in which we can play a positive role in reducing gender inequality.[cite: 5]
• Sharing household chores equally between boys and girls[cite: 5].
• Encouraging girls to pursue higher education and careers of their choice[cite: 5].
• Speaking up against gender discrimination and stereotypes in our daily lives[cite: 5].
Exercise F: Answer the following questions in detail (All 5 Questions)[cite: 5]
1. Using an example, explain how gender stereotypes lead to gender inequality.[cite: 5]
Stereotypes set fixed expectations[cite: 5]. For instance, if a family believes "men are breadwinners and women should only cook," they may invest money in a son's higher education while denying the daughter school access[cite: 5]. This leads to gender inequality in literacy, jobs, and financial independence[cite: 5].

2. Describe any two factors that lead to gender inequality in not more than 80 words.[cite: 5]
• Stereotypes & Biases: Deep-rooted societal beliefs that men are superior or more capable than women limit opportunities for girls[cite: 5].
• Devaluing Household Work: Expecting women to perform unpaid domestic care work deprives them of financial independence, rest, and career growth[cite: 5].

3. How do women get affected by the unpaid work they do?[cite: 5]
Because household cooking, cleaning, and caregiving are uncounted in national income, women's work is devalued[cite: 5]. Women face a double burden (working outside and managing home) and suffer from lack of leisure time, financial independence, and social recognition[cite: 5].

4. Explain the historical evolution of the women's movement in India in about 100 words.[cite: 5]
In 19th-century India, social reformers like Raja Rammohan Roy and Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar fought against social evils like Sati and child marriage[cite: 5]. Women leaders like Pandita Ramabai and Savitribai Phule championed girls' education and widow empowerment[cite: 5]. During the freedom struggle, women actively participated alongside men[cite: 5]. After Independence, the Indian Constitution guaranteed equal rights, equal pay, and educational access for women[cite: 5].

5. Describe any two laws that the government has passed to promote gender equality.[cite: 5]
• Right to Education Act (RTE): Guarantees free and compulsory education for all children aged 6 to 14, ensuring girls get equal schooling[cite: 5].
• 33% Panchayat Reservation: Reserves one-third of local governance seats for women, empowering female leadership[cite: 5].
