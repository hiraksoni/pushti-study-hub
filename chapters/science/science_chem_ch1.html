1. The Three Building Blocks: Atoms, Molecules & Formulas

Concept 1

What is an Atom?

An Atom is the smallest single unit of an element. Just like one single red LEGO brick.

Examples: H (Hydrogen), O (Oxygen), Na (Sodium), Cl (Chlorine).
Concept 2

What is a Molecule?

When two or more atoms click together chemically, they form a Molecule or compound.

Examples: H₂O (Water = 2 H + 1 O), CO₂ (1 C + 2 O), NaCl (1 Na + 1 Cl).
Concept 3

Chemical Formulas

A Chemical Formula is simply the recipe card showing exactly how many atoms of each kind are in that substance!

HCl = 1 Hydrogen + 1 Chlorine
NaOH = 1 Sodium + 1 Oxygen + 1 Hydrogen

3. Ions Decoded: The Secret Behind Every Acid & Base

The Acid Hero H⁺

Hydrogen Ion (H⁺)

A normal Hydrogen atom has 1 proton and 1 electron. When it enters water, it gives away its electron and becomes a positively charged H⁺ ion!

The Golden Law of Acids:
"Every acid produces H⁺ ions when dissolved in water. The H⁺ ion is what gives acids their sharp sour taste, reacts with metals, and turns blue litmus red!"
The Base Hero OH⁻

Hydroxide Ion (OH⁻)

An Oxygen atom and a Hydrogen atom hold hands and grab an extra electron, becoming a negatively charged OH⁻ ion!

The Golden Law of Bases:
"Every base produces OH⁻ ions when dissolved in water. The OH⁻ ion is what makes bases feel slippery/soapy, taste bitter, and turns red litmus blue!"
💃

4. The Atomic Dance of Neutralisation: When + Meets -

Now watch what happens when you pour an Acid into a Base! Opposite electrical charges attract each other with unstoppable force:

H⁺ (from Acid) + OH⁻ (from Base) → H₂O (Neutral Water!) + Heat (Warm!)

The fierce acidic H⁺ cancels out the corrosive basic OH⁻. They merge into gentle, pure Water (H₂O). The remaining spectator ions (like Na⁺ and Cl⁻) join hands to form a Salt!

HCl (Acid) + NaOH (Base) → NaCl (Common Salt) + H₂O (Water) + Heat

🍋 Theory Module · Acids

Acids: Chemical Nature, Classification & The Golden Dilution Rule

The word acid comes from the Latin word acidus, meaning sour. In chemistry, an acid is defined as a chemical substance that produces Hydrogen ions (H⁺) or Hydronium ions (H₃O⁺) when dissolved in water.

🌿

Organic (Natural) Acids

Acids naturally present in plants and animals. They contain Carbon, are generally weak acids, and are safe and edible in dilute forms.

  • Citric Acid: Citrus fruits (lemons, oranges, amla).
  • Acetic Acid (Ethanoic Acid): Vinegar (sirka) — 4-8% solution.
  • Lactic Acid: Sour milk, curd (dahi).
  • Tartaric Acid: Tamarind (imli), unripe grapes, raw mangoes.
  • Oxalic Acid: Spinach, tomatoes.
  • Formic / Methanoic Acid: Ant sting, nettle leaf hairs.
  • Malic Acid: Apples, cherries.
  • Ascorbic Acid (Vitamin C): Amla, citrus fruits.
🏭

Mineral (Inorganic / Lab) Acids

Acids synthesized from minerals of the Earth. Most are strong acids, highly corrosive, and attack skin, cloth, and metals violently.

  • Hydrochloric Acid (HCl): Also called Muriatic acid. Present in stomach gastric juice (pH ~1.5 to 2.0) to digest proteins and kill bacteria.
  • Sulphuric Acid (H₂SO₄): Known as the "King of Chemicals" and "Oil of Vitriol". Used in car batteries, paints, and fertiliser manufacturing.
  • Nitric Acid (HNO₃): Historically known as Aqua Fortis (strong water). Used in manufacturing explosives (TNT) and fertilisers.
  • Carbonic Acid (H₂CO₃): Formed by dissolving CO₂ in water (CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃). A unique weak mineral acid found in soda and fizzy soft drinks.
⚠️
Mandatory Safety Protocol · CBSE High-Yield Exam Question

The Golden Rule of Acid Dilution: "Acid to Water, NEVER Water to Acid!"

When diluting a concentrated acid, ALWAYS add concentrated acid slowly to water along the inner sides of the beaker with continuous, gentle stirring using a glass rod.

Why must we NEVER pour water into concentrated acid? Dissolution of acid in water is an intensely exothermic reaction (releases tremendous heat). If water is poured into concentrated acid, the small amount of water instantly boils into superheated steam. This causes the concentrated acid to violently spurt and splash out onto your face, hands, and eyes, and the sudden localized heat can crack or shatter the glass beaker!

⚡ Core Chemical Properties of Acids

Taste & Feel

Sour taste. Dilute solutions feel like water; concentrated acids are intensely corrosive and destroy organic tissue.

Litmus Action

Turn Blue Litmus paper Red. Red litmus remains red (no change).

Electrical Conduction

Aqueous acid solutions contain free mobile H⁺ and anion ions, making them good conductors of electricity (electrolytes).

Corrosion

React with active metals (Zn, Fe, Mg) to liberate Hydrogen gas (H₂). That is why curd/pickles are never kept in copper or brass!

🧼 Theory Module · Bases

Bases & Alkalies: The Hydroxide (OH⁻) Donors

A Base is a chemical substance that tastes bitter, feels slippery or soapy to touch, and reacts with acids to form salt and water. Chemically, bases dissociate in water to yield Hydroxide ions (OH⁻).

🧪
Fundamental Chemistry Theorem

"All Alkalies are Bases, but All Bases are NOT Alkalies!"

An Alkali is simply a base that is soluble in water. Most metallic oxides and hydroxides are insoluble in water, but a select few dissolve readily.

Water-Soluble Bases (Alkalies):
  • Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH) — Caustic Soda
  • Potassium Hydroxide (KOH) — Caustic Potash
  • Calcium Hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂] — Slaked lime / Lime water
  • Ammonium Hydroxide (NH₄OH) — Window cleaner
Water-Insoluble Bases (NOT Alkalies):
  • Copper Hydroxide [Cu(OH)₂] — Blue precipitate
  • Ferric Hydroxide [Fe(OH)₃] — Rust-brown solid
  • Aluminium Hydroxide [Al(OH)₃] — Gelatinous white solid
  • Zinc Hydroxide [Zn(OH)₂] — Amphoteric white base

Why Curry Stains Turn Red When Washed with Soap

Curry contains turmeric, whose active pigment is curcumin. Laundry soap and detergents are alkaline (containing basic sodium salts of fatty acids). When soap is applied to the yellow stain, the hydroxide ions (OH⁻) alter the molecular electron structure of curcumin, instantly transforming its yellow chromophore into a deep reddish-brown color! When rinsed thoroughly with plenty of water, the basic soap is washed away, and the yellow color reappears.

🌿

Litmus: Chemistry's 700-Year-Old Gift from Lichens

Litmus is not an artificial synthetic factory chemical! It is a water-soluble natural dye extracted from Lichens (chiefly Roccella tinctoria). Lichens are miraculous symbiotic partnerships between a fungus and photosynthetic algae. In distilled water, the pure extract is neutral mauve/purple. It was first used as a chemical indicator around 1300 AD by the Spanish physician Arnaldus de Villa Nova.

⚡ Reaction Dynamics · Neutralisation

Neutralisation Reactions & 6 Vital Everyday Life Applications

When an acid reacts with an equimolar amount of a base, both destroy each other's chemical properties. The resulting products are a Salt and Water, accompanied by the evolution of heat.

Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat (Exothermic Reaction)
💊 1. Indigestion (Acidity)

Our stomach secretes hydrochloric acid (HCl) to digest food. Overeating or stress causes excess acid production (hyperacidity), creating painful burning sensations in the chest (heartburn).

Medical Remedy: Antacids containing mild bases such as Milk of Magnesia [Mg(OH)₂] or Baking soda (NaHCO₃) to neutralise the excess HCl: Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
🐜 2. Ant & Nettle Stings

When a red ant or bee stings, it injects an acidic venom called Formic Acid (Methanoic acid, HCOOH) into the skin, causing severe burning pain, redness, and swelling.

Home Remedy: Rubbing with moist Baking Soda (NaHCO₃) paste or applying Calamine Lotion (which contains basic Zinc Carbonate, ZnCO₃) immediately neutralises the acid.
🐝 3. Wasp vs Bee Sting

A classic exam trap! Bee venom is acidic, but Wasp venom is alkaline (basic)!

Exam Distinction: • Bee Sting (Acidic): Treat with mild base (Baking Soda).
• Wasp Sting (Basic): Treat with mild acid like Vinegar (acetic acid) or Lemon juice! Never apply baking soda to a wasp sting!
🌾 4. Agricultural Soil Treatment

Plants cannot grow well in soil that is either too acidic or too basic. Excessive use of chemical nitrogen fertilizers makes soil overly acidic.

Farmer's Solution: • Too Acidic: Add basic Quicklime (CaO) or Slaked lime [Ca(OH)₂].
• Too Basic: Add decaying Organic Matter (compost/manure) which releases natural acids.
🏭 5. Factory Waste Effluents

Chemical and electroplating factories produce highly acidic liquid wastes. If allowed to discharge directly into rivers, lakes, and ponds, the acid kills fish and aquatic plants.

Ecological Neutralisation: Factory wastes are mandatorily treated with basic substances like Slaked lime [Ca(OH)₂] in treatment plants to neutralise acidity before safe disposal.
🪥 6. Prevention of Tooth Decay

Oral bacteria feed on sweet food debris lodged between teeth, producing corrosive lactic and organic acids that dissolve hard calcium phosphate enamel (tooth decay).

Dental Hygiene: Toothpastes are formulated to be mildly alkaline. Brushing teeth neutralises the bacterial oral acids, restoring pH and protecting tooth enamel.
🧂 Theory Module · Salts

Salts, The pH Spectrum & Acid Rain "Marble Cancer"

A Salt is an ionic compound formed by the neutralisation reaction between an acid and a base. It consists of a positive metallic cation (from the base) and a negative non-metallic anion (from the acid).

pH = 7 · Neutral

Neutral Salts

Formed by the reaction of a Strong Acid + Strong Base.

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
Examples: NaCl, KNO₃, Na₂SO₄.
pH < 7 · Acidic

Acidic Salts

Formed by the reaction of a Strong Acid + Weak Base.

HCl + NH₄OH → NH₄Cl + H₂O
Examples: NH₄Cl, CuSO₄, AlCl₃.
pH > 7 · Basic

Basic Salts

Formed by the reaction of a Weak Acid + Strong Base.

CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
Examples: CH₃COONa, Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃.
💎

Water of Crystallisation & Hydrated Salts

Many salts have a fixed number of water molecules chemically bonded within their crystalline lattice structure. This is known as Water of Crystallisation. These salts are called hydrated salts.

Copper Sulphate (Blue Vitriol / Neelathotha): Formula: CuSO₄·5H₂O (Copper sulphate pentahydrate).
Contains 5 water molecules per formula unit, giving it a vibrant blue crystal appearance. When gently heated in a dry test tube, the water evaporates: CuSO₄·5H₂O (Blue Crystals) —(Heat)→ CuSO₄ (White Powder) + 5H₂O↑ Adding a few drops of water to the white anhydrous powder immediately turns it blue again!
Other Important Hydrated Salts:
  • • Washing Soda: Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (Sodium carbonate decahydrate — 10 water molecules).
  • • Gypsum: CaSO₄·2H₂O (Calcium sulphate dihydrate — 2 water molecules).
  • • Plaster of Paris (POP): CaSO₄·½H₂O (Calcium sulphate hemihydrate).

Acid Rain & The "Marble Cancer" of Historic Monuments

Industrial factories, refineries, and vehicular traffic release massive quantities of Sulphur Dioxide (SO₂) and Nitrogen Oxides (NO₂) into the atmosphere. These pollutant gases dissolve in atmospheric water droplets within rainclouds to produce mineral acids:

SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (Sulphurous acid)
2SO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 2H₂SO₄ (Sulphuric acid)
4NO₂ + 2H₂O + O₂ → 4HNO₃ (Nitric acid)
What is Marble Cancer? (Taj Mahal Degradation) Normal rain has a slightly acidic pH of ~5.6 due to dissolved CO₂. Rain with pH below 5.6 is termed Acid Rain. When acid rain falls on historical monuments made of white marble (Calcium Carbonate, CaCO₃) such as the Taj Mahal in Agra, sulphuric acid chemically attacks the marble: CaCO₃ (Marble) + H₂SO₄ (Acid Rain) → CaSO₄ (Calcium Sulphate) + H₂O + CO₂↑ Calcium sulphate creates an insoluble yellow crust that flakes away, corroding, pitting, and discoloring the monument. This phenomenon is termed Marble Cancer.

Activity 2 · Turmeric Art Book Page 9

Preparing Turmeric Indicator Paper & Drawing a Greeting Card

Aim: Create a natural indicator paper from kitchen turmeric and observe its reaction with soap solution.
Procedure: Mix a spoonful of turmeric powder with a little water to form a smooth paste. Apply the paste evenly onto a sheet of white blotting/filter paper and allow it to dry completely. Cut into thin strips. Using an ear-bud dipped in soap solution, draw a flower or greeting message on the yellow paper.
Observation: The yellow turmeric paper instantly turns into a vibrant, deep brick-red line wherever the soap solution touches it!
Conclusion: Soap solution is basic in nature. Turmeric serves as a natural indicator that turns red in basic medium and remains yellow in acidic/neutral medium.

Activity 3 · China Rose Indicator Book Page 10

Preparation and Testing with China Rose (Gudhal) Petal Indicator

Aim: Extract anthocyanin pigment from China rose petals using warm water and test sample solutions.
Procedure: Collect some China rose (Hibiscus) petals and place them in a beaker. Add warm water and allow the mixture to stand until the water turns light pink. Filter the colored water to use as an indicator. Add 5 drops of indicator to test tubes containing shampoo (dilute), lemon juice, soda water, sodium hydrogencarbonate solution, vinegar, and sugar solution.
Observation & Conclusion:
• In acidic solutions (lemon juice, vinegar): Turns Dark Pink / Magenta.
• In basic solutions (shampoo, baking soda): Turns Deep Green.
• In neutral solutions (sugar, salt): No change (retains light pink).

Activity 4 · Lime Water Preparation Book Page 11

Preparing Clear Lime Water [Calcium Hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂] in the Lab

Aim: Prepare a standard calcium hydroxide alkaline solution.
Procedure: Dissolve some quick lime (Chuna, Calcium Oxide, CaO) in water in a beaker. Stir well and let it stand undisturbed for a few hours. The beaker feels distinctly warm to touch because slaking of lime is an exothermic reaction: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + Heat. Carefully decant the top clear liquid into a clean bottle. This clear supernatant liquid is Lime Water. Testing with red litmus turns it blue, proving it is a strong soluble base.

Activity 5 · Exothermic Neutralisation Titration Book Page 12

Demonstrating Neutralisation with Phenolphthalein & Measuring Temperature Rise

Aim: Demonstrate acid-base neutralisation using phenolphthalein indicator and measure temperature change.
Procedure: Take about 5 mL of dilute HCl in a test tube. Note its initial temperature using a laboratory thermometer (e.g. 24°C). Add 2-3 drops of phenolphthalein; the solution remains completely colorless. Now, using a dropper, add dilute NaOH drop-by-drop with constant swirling. The moment a faint pink color persists, the acid is completely neutralised. Immediately check the thermometer.
Observation: The temperature rises significantly (e.g. from 24°C to 31°C)! If one more drop of dilute HCl is added, the pink color disappears. If another drop of NaOH is added, pink returns!
Conclusion: Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat. Neutralisation is an exothermic process, and phenolphthalein is a reversible indicator.

AR #1 · Ant Sting Treatment
Assertion: The pain caused due to ant sting can be neutralised by baking soda. Reason: Ant sting is acidic in nature.
Answer: (a) Both assertion and reason are true and reason is the correct explanation of assertion.

Ant venom contains formic acid. Baking soda is a mild base; applying it neutralises the acid, providing relief.

AR #2 · Nature of Salts
Assertion: A salt is produced when an acid is neutralised by a base. Reason: A salt can be acidic, basic or neutral.
Answer: (b) Both assertion and reason are true but reason is not the correct explanation of assertion.

Both statements are factually correct. However, the fact that salts can be acidic, basic, or neutral does not explain why a salt is produced during neutralisation.

AR #3 · Milk of Magnesia Mechanism
Assertion: To neutralise the excess acid formed in the stomach, milk of magnesia is taken. Reason: Milk of magnesia contains a base called magnesium hydroxide.
Answer: (a) Both assertion and reason are true and reason is the correct explanation of assertion.

Milk of magnesia contains the weak base magnesium hydroxide [Mg(OH)₂], which neutralises the hyperacidic gastric juice.

AR #4 · Turmeric Indicator Response
Assertion: With some samples turmeric paper turns red. Reason: Such samples are acidic in nature.
Answer: (c) Assertion is true but reason is false.

Assertion is true because turmeric turns red in alkaline solutions. The reason is false because samples that turn turmeric paper red are basic, not acidic.

AR #5 · Synthetic Indicators
Assertion: Methyl orange and phenolphthalein are natural indicators. Reason: Methyl orange is yellow in colour while phenolphthalein is pink.
Answer: (d) Both assertion and reason are false.

Both statements are false. Methyl orange and phenolphthalein are synthetic indicators, not natural ones. Methyl orange is orange in neutral solution (yellow only in base), and phenolphthalein is naturally colourless (pink only in base).

AR #6 · Red Rose on Lime Water
Assertion: Lime water turns red rose indicator into green. Reason: Lime water is basic in nature.
Answer: (a) Both assertion and reason are true and reason is the correct explanation of assertion.

Red rose indicator turns green in basic media. Since lime water is calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂] which is basic, it turns the red rose indicator green.

AR #7 · pH of Acetic Acid
Assertion: pH of acetic acid is less than 7. Reason: Acetic acid is an organic acid.
Answer: (b) Both assertion and reason are true but reason is not the correct explanation of assertion.

Acetic acid has pH < 7 because it dissociates in water to release H⁺ ions. Being an organic acid is a true statement, but being organic does not explain why its pH is less than 7 (its acidic ionisation does).

AR #8 · Neutral Salt Formation
Assertion: NaCl is neutral salt. Reason: It is combination of strong acid and strong base.
Answer: (a) Both assertion and reason are true and reason is the correct explanation of assertion.

Salts formed from the neutralisation of a strong acid (HCl) and a strong base (NaOH) yield aqueous solutions with pH = 7 (neutral salts).

AR #9 · Exothermic Neutralisation
Assertion: Salt and water are formed in neutralisation reaction. Reason: Heat is released in a neutralisation reaction.
Answer: (b) Both assertion and reason are true but reason is not the correct explanation of assertion.

Both statements are true. Neutralisation produces salt and water, and it is an exothermic reaction that evolves heat. However, the release of heat is not the reason salt and water are formed.

AR #10 · Olfactory Indicators
Assertion: Odour of olfactory indicator change in an acidic or basic medium. Reason: Clove oil is an olfactory indicator.
Answer: (b) Both assertion and reason are true but reason is not the correct explanation of assertion.

⚠️ Exam Mastery · HOTS

Classic Exam Traps, Common Misconceptions & Olympiad HOTS

The difference between 90% and 100% in science is understanding where examiner traps lie. Here are the 6 most common pitfalls and 6 Olympiad-grade Higher Order Thinking questions.

🪤 Trap 1: "All Hydrogen Compounds are Acids" — FALSE!

The Trap: Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) and Alcohol (C₂H₅OH) contain plenty of Hydrogen atoms. Students often mistakenly mark them as acids.

The Scientific Truth: To be an acid, the substance must ionize in water to release free H⁺ ions. The Hydrogen atoms in glucose and ethanol are tightly bound by covalent bonds and do NOT ionize. Hence, they do not conduct electricity and do not turn blue litmus red.
🪤 Trap 2: Dry HCl Gas vs Moist Blue Litmus

The Trap: "Dry HCl gas turns dry blue litmus paper red." (True/False?) Students write True and lose 1 mark!

The Scientific Truth: Acids show acidic behavior ONLY in the presence of water. Without moisture, dry HCl molecules cannot dissociate into H⁺ ions. Only when the litmus paper is moist does dry HCl dissolve, form H⁺ ions, and turn red!
🪤 Trap 3: Dilution — Water to Acid vs Acid to Water

The Trap: Remembering which is poured into which during laboratory dilution.

The Scientific Truth: Always add Acid to Water slowly. If water is poured into concentrated acid, localized boiling causes the acid to violently splash onto skin and shatters glass!
🪤 Trap 4: Wasp Sting Remedy Confusion

The Trap: Applying baking soda to a wasp sting.

The Scientific Truth: Wasp venom is alkaline / basic! Applying baking soda (another base) will worsen the irritation. Apply mild acid like vinegar or lemon juice. Bee stings are acidic and require baking soda!
🪤 Trap 5: Curd & Sour Food in Copper Vessels

The Trap: Why should curd and pickles never be kept in copper, brass, or bronze vessels?

The Scientific Truth: Curd contains lactic acid. Acids react with copper and brass to form toxic copper salts (e.g. copper lactate) which cause food poisoning. Always use stainless steel, glass, or earthenware!
🪤 Trap 6: Does Distilled Water Conduct Electricity?

The Trap: "Water conducts electricity, so distilled water must conduct too."

The Scientific Truth: Pure distilled water has no dissolved ions and is a poor conductor (insulator). Only when an acid, base, or salt is dissolved in it (or tap water with minerals) does it conduct electricity!
