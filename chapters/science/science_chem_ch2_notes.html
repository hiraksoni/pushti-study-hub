CONCEPT PILLAR 1.1

The Elemental Taxonomy & The Electron Sea Model

Classification of 118 elements, valence configurations, and the quantum mechanics of metallic bonding.

Out of the 118 known elements in the Modern Periodic Table, approximately 93 are metals, 18 are non-metals, and 7 are metalloids (elements possessing intermediate properties, such as Silicon, Germanium, Arsenic, and Antimony).

The fundamental chemical distinction between metals and non-metals lies in their valence electronic configuration and their thermodynamic drive to attain a stable octet noble gas configuration ($ns^2np^6$):

Classification Criterion Metals (Electropositive Elements) Non-Metals (Electronegative Elements)
Valence Electrons Typically possess 1, 2, or 3 electrons in outermost shell (e.g., $Na: 2, 8, 1$; $Mg: 2, 8, 2$; $Al: 2, 8, 3$). Typically possess 4, 5, 6, or 7 electrons in outermost shell (e.g., $C: 2, 4$; $N: 2, 5$; $O: 2, 6$; $Cl: 2, 8, 7$).
Ion Formation Tendency Electropositive: Readily donate electrons to form positively charged cations:
$$M \longrightarrow M^{n+} + ne^- \quad (Na \to Na^+ + e^-)$$ 		Electronegative: Readily accept electrons to form negatively charged anions:
$$X + me^- \longrightarrow X^{m-} \quad (Cl + e^- \to Cl^-)$$
Nature of Bonding Form ionic bonds with non-metals (electron transfer) and metallic bonds with other metals. Form ionic bonds with metals and covalent bonds by sharing electrons with other non-metals.
The "Electron Sea Model" of Metallic Bonding (Drude-Lorentz Theory)

Why do metals exhibit unique macroscopic properties like luster, high electrical conductivity, and malleability? In a metallic crystal, the valence electrons detach from individual parent atoms, forming a delocalized, freely moving "sea of mobile electrons" surrounding fixed positive atomic cores (kernels or cations):

$$\text{Kernel}^{n+} \quad \sim \quad e^- \quad \sim \quad \text{Kernel}^{n+} \quad \sim \quad e^- \quad \sim \quad \text{Kernel}^{n+}$$

Because metallic bonding is non-directional, when an external shear stress is applied (e.g., hammering), planes of metal kernels can smoothly slide past one another without generating catastrophic electrostatic repulsion, explaining why metals flatten instead of shattering.

Textbook Foundations: Elements, Minerals, Ores & Metallurgy
  • Metals: Electropositive elements forming cations ($1, 2, 3$ valence electrons).
  • Noble Metals: Silver ($\text{Ag}$), Gold ($\text{Au}$), Platinum ($\text{Pt}$) occurring in native (free) state due to unreactivity with air, water, and common acids.
  • Minerals: Naturally occurring inorganic chemical compounds of metals in Earth's crust.
  • Ores: Minerals from which metals can be extracted conveniently and profitably. ("All ores are minerals, but not all minerals are ores.")
  • Metallurgy: The complete industrial technological process of isolating pure metals from ores.

CONCEPT PILLAR 1.2

Physical Properties & The 10 Famous Periodic Anomalies

Comprehensive 10-parameter comparative breakdown and exact physical rationales for every textbook exception.

Physical Property Metals Non-Metals
1. Physical State (STP) Solids at room temperature (Exception: Mercury is liquid; Gallium and Cesium melt slightly above room temperature). Exist in all three states: 11 gases ($H_2, N_2, O_2, F_2, Cl_2$, noble gases), 1 liquid (Bromine), remainder are brittle solids ($C, S, P, I_2$).
2. Metallic Luster Lustrous surface when freshly cut. Mobile electrons absorb and instantly re-emit incident light photons. Dull surfaces; do not reflect light coherently (Exception: Iodine crystals and Graphite are lustrous).
3. Hardness & Strength Generally hard with high tensile strength (Exception: Alkali metals $Li, Na, K$ are soft enough to slice with a butter knife). Generally soft and mechanically weak (Exception: Diamond is the hardest known natural substance).
4. Malleability Highly malleable (can be beaten into thin foils). Gold and Silver are the most malleable elements. Non-malleable; strictly brittle (crumble into powder when struck with a hammer).
5. Ductility Highly ductile (drawn into fine microscopic wires). $1\text{ g}$ of Gold can be drawn into a $2\text{ km}$ wire! Completely non-ductile; shatter under tensile elongation stress.
6. Thermal Conductivity Excellent conductors (Silver and Copper are best; Lead and Bismuth are poorest). Poor thermal conductors / insulators (Exception: Diamond has higher thermal conductivity than Copper via phonon vibrations!).
7. Electrical Conductivity Excellent electrical conductors ($Ag > Cu > Au > Al > W > Fe$). Delocalized electron drift. Electrical insulators (Exception: Graphite conducts electricity along planar sheets via delocalized $\pi$-electrons).
8. Density Generally high density ($Osmium = 22.59\text{ g/cm}^3$). (Exceptions: $Li, Na, K$ float on water; $Na = 0.97\text{ g/cm}^3$). Generally low densities ($< 2.5\text{ g/cm}^3$).
9. Melting & Boiling Points Generally very high (Tungsten melts at $3422^\circ\text{C}$). (Exceptions: Gallium melts at $29.8^\circ\text{C}$, Cesium at $28.4^\circ\text{C}$). Generally low melting and boiling points (Exceptions: Carbon in Diamond form melts at $> 3550^\circ\text{C}$).
10. Sonority Sonorous: Produce a deep, resonant ringing sound when struck hard (used in temple bells and gongs). Non-sonorous: Emit a dull, dead thud when struck.
The 10 Famous Periodic Exceptions (High-Yield Roster)
1. Liquid Metal: Mercury ($Hg$)
Relativistic contraction of its $6s^2$ electrons shields valence electrons, weakening metallic bonds so much that it is liquid at $-38.8^\circ\text{C}$.
2. Palm-Melting Metals: $Ga$ & $Cs$
Gallium ($29.76^\circ\text{C}$) and Cesium ($28.44^\circ\text{C}$) have unusually weak crystal lattices and melt from human body heat ($37^\circ\text{C}$).
3. Knife-Cuttable Metals: $Li, Na, K$
Alkali metals have large atomic radii, single valence electrons, and loose Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) packing, yielding very low densities.
4. Lustrous Non-Metal: Iodine ($I_2$)
Large polarizable electron cloud of $I_2$ allows delocalized surface electron transitions that reflect light with metallic purple sheen.
5. Super-Hard Non-Metal: Diamond
Giant 3D network of covalent bonds ($sp^3$ tetrahedral carbon) makes it the hardest natural substance known.
6. Conducting Non-Metal: Graphite
$sp^2$ hybridized hexagonal planar carbon sheets leave one free unhybridized $p$-electron per atom delocalized across the sheet.
7. Liquid Non-Metal: Bromine ($Br_2$)
The only non-metal liquid at STP ($25^\circ\text{C}, 1\text{ atm}$); reddish-brown fuming liquid.
8. Highest Melting Point: Tungsten ($W$)
Maximum half-filled $5d^46s^2$ valence bonding electrons generate an astronomical melting point of $3422^\circ\text{C}$.
9. Poor Conductor Metals: Lead ($Pb$) & Bismuth ($Bi$)
Heavy nuclear charges and lattice scattering reduce the mean free path of valence electrons, making them poor thermal/electrical conductors.
10. High Tensile Non-Metal: Carbon Fiber
Aligned covalent carbon backbones yield higher tensile strength per unit weight than high-grade structural steel!

ACTIVITY CORNER 2.1 • NCERT TEXTBOOK LAB
To Show That Metals Are Malleable in Nature While Non-Metals Are Brittle
Materials Required: An iron nail, a piece of coal, pencil lead (graphite), a piece of thick aluminium wire, and a hammer.
Procedure: Place the iron nail on a hard surface and strike it repeatedly with the hammer. Repeat the exact same hammering process on the coal piece, the pencil lead, and the aluminium wire.
Observations: The iron nail and aluminium wire do not break; they flatten out into thin sheets. In contrast, the coal piece and pencil lead shatter into small pieces and powder.
Scientific Conclusion: Metals possess malleability (their atomic layers can slip past each other under stress without fracturing due to delocalized metallic bonding). Non-metals are brittle and fracture under mechanical force.
ACTIVITY CORNER 2.2 • NCERT TEXTBOOK LAB
To Demonstrate Electrical Conductivity in Metals vs. Non-Metals
Materials Required: Dry cell (battery), torch bulb with holder, connecting copper wires, switch, crocodile clips A and B, test samples (iron nail, copper wire, coal piece, sulphur block).
Procedure: Set up an electrical testing circuit with a gap between clips A and B. Insert each sample between the clips to complete the circuit and observe whether the bulb illuminates.
Observations: When the iron nail or copper wire is clamped between clips A and B, the circuit is completed and the bulb glows brightly. When coal or sulphur is clamped, the bulb remains completely dark.
Scientific Conclusion: Metals are excellent electrical conductors because their delocalized valence electrons move freely across the lattice under an electric potential difference. Non-metals (with localized valence electrons) act as electrical insulators.

CONCEPT PILLAR 2.1

Reactions with Atmospheric Oxygen ($O_2$) & Amphoteric Oxides

Basic metal oxide formation, combustion energetics, and the dual acidic/basic behavior of Aluminium and Zinc oxides.

Almost all metals combine with oxygen upon heating to synthesize metal oxides. Because metal oxides ionize in water to yield hydroxide ($OH^-$) ions, they are conventionally classified as basic oxides:

$$\text{Metal} + \text{Oxygen} \longrightarrow \text{Metal Oxide (Basic)}$$
Metal Combustion Behavior & Observations Balanced Chemical Equation
Sodium ($\text{Na}$) / Potassium ($\text{K}$) Combine violently with oxygen even at room temperature ($25^\circ\text{C}$). Catch fire spontaneously in open air; stored strictly under kerosene oil. $$4\text{Na}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{Na}_2\text{O}(s)$$
$$\text{Na}_2\text{O}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{NaOH}(aq) \quad (\text{Basic Solution})$$
Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$) Does not burn at room temperature. On ignition, burns with a dazzling, blinding white flame to form white magnesium oxide powder. $$2\text{Mg}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{MgO}(s)$$
Aluminium ($\text{Al}$) & Zinc ($\text{Zn}$) Form a protective self-healing nanometer-thin oxide film that passivates the metal against further atmospheric oxidation. $$4\text{Al}(s) + 3\text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s)$$
$$2\text{Zn}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{ZnO}(s)$$
Iron ($\text{Fe}$) Iron rods do not burn on heating; however, fine iron filings sprinkled into a Bunsen burner flame burn vigorously with brilliant golden sparks. $$3\text{Fe}(s) + 2\text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4(s) \quad (\text{Magnetic Iron Oxide})$$
Copper ($\text{Cu}$) Does not burn with a flame even on strong prolonged heating. Instead, its surface is coated with a thin black layer of Copper(II) Oxide (cupric oxide). $$2\text{Cu}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\text{Prolonged heat}} 2\text{CuO}(s) \quad (\text{Black})$$
Silver ($\text{Ag}$) & Gold ($\text{Au}$) Completely unreactive with oxygen even at volcanic temperatures ($> 1000^\circ\text{C}$). Native noble metals. $$\text{Ag} / \text{Au} + \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow \text{No Reaction}$$
Amphoteric Oxides: Dual Acidic & Basic Mechanisms ($Al_2O_3$ & $ZnO$)

Most metal oxides are purely basic. However, oxides of aluminium ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) and zinc ($\text{ZnO}$) react with both acids and bases to yield salt and water, earning the designation Amphoteric Oxides (Greek amphoteroi = "both"):

1. Aluminium Oxide ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$):
• Acting as a Base (Reacting with Acid):
$$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 6\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{AlCl}_3(aq) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \quad (\text{Aluminium Chloride})$$
• Acting as an Acid (Reacting with Base):
$$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{NaAlO}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \quad (\text{Sodium Aluminate})$$
2. Zinc Oxide ($\text{ZnO}$):
• Acting as a Base (Reacting with Acid):
$$\text{ZnO}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \quad (\text{Zinc Chloride})$$
• Acting as an Acid (Reacting with Base):
$$\text{ZnO}(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{ZnO}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \quad (\text{Sodium Zincate})$$
CONCEPT PILLAR 2.2

Reactions of Metals with Water ($H_2O$)

Reaction kinetics across physical states of water: cold water, hot water, and superheated steam.

When metals react with water, they produce a metal oxide (or hydroxide) and liberate combustible hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$). The reaction intensity drops steeply down the electrochemical series:

Category Metals Kinetics & Chemical Dynamics Balanced Equation
Cold Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_{(l)}$ at $20^\circ\text{C}$) Potassium ($\text{K}$), Sodium ($\text{Na}$), Calcium ($\text{Ca}$) Violent, instantaneous, and highly exothermic. The heat evolved with $\text{K}$ and $\text{Na}$ immediately ignites the hydrogen gas with lilac and yellow flames respectively. With $\text{Ca}$, reaction is less violent; bubbles of $\text{H}_2$ stick to calcium metal, making it float. $$2\text{K}(s) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{KOH}(aq) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow + \text{Heat}$$
$$2\text{Na}(s) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{NaOH}(aq) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow + \text{Heat}$$
$$\text{Ca}(s) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{Ca(OH)}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$
Hot Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_{(l)}$ at $80\text{--}100^\circ\text{C}$) Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$) Completely unreactive in cold water. In boiling water, reacts steadily forming magnesium hydroxide. Bubbles of hydrogen gas cling to its surface, causing magnesium strips to float to the water surface! $$\text{Mg}(s) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(\text{hot}) \longrightarrow \text{Mg(OH)}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$
Steam ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_{(g)}$ at $>100^\circ\text{C}$) Aluminium ($\text{Al}$), Zinc ($\text{Zn}$), Iron ($\text{Fe}$) Do not react with liquid water at all. When superheated steam is passed over red-hot metal in an ignition tube, they form insoluble metal oxides (NOT hydroxides!) and liberate $\text{H}_2$ gas. $$2\text{Al}(s) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) \longrightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$
$$\text{Zn}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(g) \longrightarrow \text{ZnO}(s) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$
$$3\text{Fe}(s) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) \longrightarrow \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4(s) + 4\text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$
Completely Inert Lead ($\text{Pb}$), Copper ($\text{Cu}$), Silver ($\text{Ag}$), Gold ($\text{Au}$) Zero reaction with water under any conditions—cold, boiling, or red-hot superheated steam. $$\text{Metal} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{No Reaction}$$
Critical Board Exam Memory Rule: Hydroxides vs. Oxides
Metals reacting with liquid water ($\text{Na, K, Ca, Mg}$) produce soluble hydroxides ($\text{NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)}_2, \text{Mg(OH)}_2$). Metals reacting strictly with steam ($\text{Al, Zn, Fe}$) produce solid metal oxides ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \text{ZnO}, \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$), because metal hydroxides thermally decompose into oxides at superheated steam temperatures!
CONCEPT PILLAR 2.3

Reactions with Dilute Acids & The Nitric Acid Paradox

Acid-metal displacement kinetics and why nitric acid fails to produce hydrogen gas with common metals.

Metals lying above hydrogen in the electrochemical series displace hydrogen from dilute mineral acids (such as $\text{HCl}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), producing metal salts and hydrogen gas:

$$\text{Metal} + \text{Dilute Acid} \longrightarrow \text{Metal Salt} + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$
  • $$\text{Mg}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{MgCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow \quad (\text{Most vigorous effervescence})$$
  • $$2\text{Al}(s) + 6\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{AlCl}_3(aq) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$
  • $$\text{Zn}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow \quad (\text{Standard laboratory preparation of } \text{H}_2)$$
  • $$\text{Fe}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{FeCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow \quad (\text{Slow effervescence, pale green ferrous chloride})$$
  • $$\text{Cu}(s) + \text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{No Reaction} \quad (\text{Copper lies below hydrogen})$$
The Nitric Acid Paradox: Why $HNO_3$ Does NOT Evolve $H_2$ Gas

When metals react with dilute nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$), hydrogen gas is NOT evolved. Why?

Nitric acid is a powerful, aggressive oxidizing agent. As rapidly as nascent hydrogen ($\text{H}$) is produced at the metal surface, $\text{HNO}_3$ oxidizes it into water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), while the nitrogen in $\text{HNO}_3$ is itself reduced to oxides of nitrogen ($\text{NO}_2, \text{NO},$ or $\text{N}_2\text{O}$):

$$3\text{Cu} + 8\text{HNO}_3(\text{dilute}) \longrightarrow 3\text{Cu(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{NO} \uparrow + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$$ $$\text{Cu} + 4\text{HNO}_3(\text{concentrated}) \longrightarrow \text{Cu(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{NO}_2 \uparrow + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The Only Two Exceptions: Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$) & Manganese ($\text{Mn}$)
Only Magnesium and Manganese are sufficiently electropositive to liberate $\text{H}_2$ gas with nitric acid, and ONLY when the acid is extremely dilute ($1\text{--}2\%$) and ice-cold:
$$\text{Mg}(s) + 2\text{HNO}_3(\text{very dilute } 1\%) \longrightarrow \text{Mg(NO}_3)_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$
$$\text{Mn}(s) + 2\text{HNO}_3(\text{very dilute } 1\%) \longrightarrow \text{Mn(NO}_3)_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$
CONCEPT PILLAR 2.4

The Reactivity Series, Single Displacement & Thermite Welding

Electrochemical hierarchy, standard reduction potentials, and high-temperature aluminothermic engineering.

Master Mnemonic for the Electrochemical Reactivity Series

"Please Stop Calling Me A Careless Zebra, Instead Try Learning How Copper Minerals Save Gold!"

$\text{K} > \text{Na} > \text{Ca} > \text{Mg} > \text{Al} > \text{C} > \text{Zn} > \text{Fe} > \text{Sn} > \text{Pb} > [\mathbf{H}] > \text{Cu} > \text{Hg} > \text{Ag} > \text{Au}$

Any metal higher in the reactivity series possesses a lower (more negative) reduction potential, allowing it to donate electrons spontaneously to displace a lower metal from its aqueous salt solution:

$$A + BC \longrightarrow AC + B \quad (\text{Occurs only if } A \text{ is more reactive than } B)$$
Industrial Railway Welding: The Goldschmidt Thermite Process

Displacement reactions involving highly electropositive metals like Aluminium are intensely exothermic. In the Thermite Process, a mixture of powdered Iron(III) Oxide ($\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$) and Aluminium powder ($\text{Al}$) in a $3:1$ stoichiometric ratio is ignited with a magnesium ribbon fuse and barium peroxide:

$$\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{Al}(s) \xrightarrow{\text{Ignition}} 2\text{Fe}(l) + \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + \text{Enormous Heat} \quad (\Delta H = -851.5\text{ kJ/mol})$$

The reaction generates blistering temperatures exceeding $2500^\circ\text{C}$ to $3000^\circ\text{C}$. Because the melting point of iron is $1538^\circ\text{C}$, the displaced iron is produced in the white-hot molten liquid state ($\text{Fe}_{(l)}$), which pours directly into cracks between severed railway tracks to weld them seamlessly on site!

COMPETITION WINDOW • DISPLACEMENT OF SILVER

When a copper wire is suspended in a colourless aqueous solution of Silver Nitrate ($\text{AgNO}_3$), the solution gradually turns bright blue due to the formation of cupric nitrate, while glittering dendritic crystals of pure silver metal deposit along the copper wire:

$$\text{Cu}(s) + 2\text{AgNO}_3(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Cu(NO}_3)_2(aq) + 2\text{Ag}(s) \downarrow \quad (\text{Colourless } \to \text{ Brilliant Blue})$$

ACTIVITY CORNER 2.3 • NCERT TEXTBOOK LAB
To Check the Basic Chemical Nature of Magnesium Oxide
Materials Required: Magnesium ribbon ($3\text{ cm}$), pair of tongs, Bunsen burner, watch glass, red and blue litmus papers, distilled water.
Procedure: Clean magnesium ribbon with sandpaper to remove protective oxide film. Hold with tongs and ignite in the flame. Collect the ash in a watch glass, dissolve in $5\text{ mL}$ warm distilled water, and test with litmus papers.
Observations: The ribbon burns with a dazzling, blinding white flame, forming white powdered ash ($\text{MgO}$). The aqueous solution turns moist red litmus paper blue, while blue litmus paper shows no color change.
Chemical Equations & Conclusion:
$$2\text{Mg}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{MgO}(s) \quad (\text{White Ash})$$
$$\text{MgO}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{Mg(OH)}_2(aq) \quad (\text{Magnesium Hydroxide, Basic})$$
This proves conclusively that metal oxides are basic in nature.
ACTIVITY CORNER 2.4 • NCERT TEXTBOOK LAB
To Compare the Reactivity of Different Metals with Water (5-Tube Hierarchy)
Materials Required: 5 glass test tubes, water, burner, small samples of Sodium ($\text{Na}$), Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$), Zinc ($\text{Zn}$), Iron filings ($\text{Fe}$), and Copper turnings ($\text{Cu}$).
Procedure: Add $5\text{ mL}$ cold water to each tube and drop the metal. Observe rate of reaction. If no reaction, heat to boiling or pass steam.
Observations:
  • Tube 1 ($\text{Na}$): Violent reaction in cold water, darts on surface, hissing sound, evolved $\text{H}_2$ gas ignites with pop sound: $2\text{Na} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2 \uparrow$.
  • Tube 2 ($\text{Mg}$): No reaction with cold water. Reacts with hot boiling water; bubbles of $\text{H}_2$ stick to its surface, causing magnesium to float: $\text{Mg} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{Mg(OH)}_2 + \text{H}_2 \uparrow$. With steam, it yields white $\text{MgO}$.
  • Tube 3 ($\text{Zn}$): Does not react with cold or hot water; reacts only when heated in steam: $\text{Zn} + \text{H}_2\text{O}(g) \to \text{ZnO} + \text{H}_2 \uparrow$.
  • Tube 4 ($\text{Fe}$): Reacts very sluggishly only with red-hot steam: $3\text{Fe} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) \to \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + 4\text{H}_2 \uparrow$.
  • Tube 5 ($\text{Cu}$): No reaction whatsoever, even with red-hot steam.
Reactivity Series with Water: $\text{Na} > \text{Mg} > \text{Zn} > \text{Fe} > \text{Cu}$. Calcium and Magnesium float in water because evolved hydrogen gas bubbles adhere to their surfaces.
ACTIVITY CORNER 2.5 • NCERT TEXTBOOK LAB
To Show the Reaction of Metals with Dilute Acids and the Hydrogen Pop Test
Materials Required: Zinc granules, test tube, dilute hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$), wooden splinter, matchbox.
Procedure: Place granulated zinc in a test tube and add $5\text{ mL}$ of dilute $\text{HCl}$. Rapid bubbling commences. Bring a burning splinter near the mouth of the tube.
Observations: Rapid effervescence occurs. The burning splinter is extinguished with a distinct, sharp 'pop' sound accompanied by a pale blue flame.
Chemical Reaction & Conclusion:
$$\text{Zn}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$
Metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series displace hydrogen from dilute mineral acids. The 'pop' sound is the definitive confirmatory test for hydrogen gas.
ACTIVITY CORNER 2.6 • NCERT TEXTBOOK LAB
To Show That More Reactive Metals Displace Less Reactive Metals from Salt Solutions
Materials Required: Two test tubes (I & II), copper sulphate solution ($\text{CuSO}_4$, bright blue), ferrous sulphate solution ($\text{FeSO}_4$, pale green), clean iron nail, zinc strip.
Procedure: Place clean iron nail in Tube I ($\text{CuSO}_4$) and zinc strip in Tube II ($\text{FeSO}_4$). Allow both to stand undisturbed for 25–30 minutes.
Observations:
  • In Tube I: The deep blue color of copper sulphate fades and turns into a light pale green solution ($\text{FeSO}_4$). A prominent reddish-brown coating of metallic copper deposits on the iron nail.
  • In Tube II: The pale green color of ferrous sulphate disappears completely, becoming crystal clear / colourless ($\text{ZnSO}_4$). Grey spongy iron precipitates onto the zinc strip.
Displacement Proof:
$$\text{Tube I: } \text{Fe}(s) + \text{CuSO}_4(aq) \longrightarrow \text{FeSO}_4(aq) + \text{Cu}(s) \downarrow \quad (\text{Blue } \to \text{ Light Green})$$
$$\text{Tube II: } \text{Zn}(s) + \text{FeSO}_4(aq) \longrightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4(aq) + \text{Fe}(s) \downarrow \quad (\text{Light Green } \to \text{ Colourless})$$
This demonstrates that Zinc is more reactive than Iron, and Iron is more reactive than Copper ($\text{Zn} > \text{Fe} > \text{Cu}$).

CONCEPT PILLAR 3.1

Chemical Properties of Non-Metals & Reaction Pathways

Electronegativity, non-metallic covalent synthesis, and reactions with Oxygen, Chlorine, and Hydrogen.

Non-metals are electronegative elements that possess 4, 5, 6, or 7 valence electrons. They achieve stable noble gas electron configurations by accepting electrons from metals (forming ionic compounds) or by sharing electron pairs with other non-metals (forming covalent bonds):

Reaction Type Reactants & Conditions Chemical Equations & Product Description
Reaction with Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$) Non-metals burn in air/oxygen to form acidic covalent oxides (acid anhydrides). $$\text{C}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{CO}_2(g) \quad (\text{Carbon Dioxide})$$
$$\text{S}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{SO}_2(g) \quad (\text{Sulphur Dioxide})$$
$$\text{P}_4(s) + 5\text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}(s) \quad (\text{Phosphorus Pentoxide})$$
Reaction with Chlorine ($\text{Cl}_2$) Form covalent liquid or gaseous chlorides by mutual sharing of electrons. $$\text{H}_2(g) + \text{Cl}_2(g) \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2\text{HCl}(g) \quad (\text{Hydrogen Chloride})$$
$$\text{P}_4(s) + 6\text{Cl}_2(g) \longrightarrow 4\text{PCl}_3(l) \quad (\text{Phosphorus Trichloride})$$
$$\text{C}(s) + 2\text{Cl}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{CCl}_4(l) \quad (\text{Carbon Tetrachloride})$$
Reaction with Hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$) React at elevated temperatures/pressures in the presence of catalysts to synthesize stable covalent hydrides. $$\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \xrightleftharpoons[\text{Fe / Mo}]{450^\circ\text{C}, 200\text{ atm}} 2\text{NH}_3(g) \quad (\text{Ammonia})$$
$$\text{H}_2(g) + \text{S}(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{H}_2\text{S}(g) \quad (\text{Hydrogen Sulphide, rotten egg smell})$$
Reaction with Dilute Acids Non-metals DO NOT displace hydrogen gas from dilute mineral acids ($\text{HCl}, \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$). $$\text{Non-Metal} + \text{Dilute Acid} \longrightarrow \text{No Reaction}$$
Why Non-Metals Cannot Displace Hydrogen from Acids
To liberate hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$) from an acidic solution, hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$) must accept electrons to undergo reduction: $$2\text{H}^+(aq) + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{H}_2(g) \uparrow$$ Metals are electropositive electron donors ($M \to M^{n+} + ne^-$) and supply these electrons effortlessly. In sharp contrast, non-metals are electronegative electron acceptors (oxidizing agents); they cannot donate electrons to hydrogen ions. Therefore, non-metals cannot reduce $\text{H}^+$ and show zero displacement reaction with dilute acids.

Acidic Anhydrides vs. Neutral Non-Metal Oxides

Aqueous hydrolysis of non-metal oxides into oxyacids and the chemistry of neutral oxides.

Non-metal oxides dissolve in water to form acidic solutions that turn moist blue litmus paper red:

$$\text{CO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3(aq) \quad (\text{Carbonic Acid})$$
$$\text{SO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3(aq) \quad (\text{Sulphurous Acid})$$
$$2\text{NO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{HNO}_2(aq) + \text{HNO}_3(aq) \quad (\text{Mixed Acid Anhydride})$$
$$\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}(s) + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 4\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4(aq) \quad (\text{Orthophosphoric Acid})$$
High-Yield Exam Concept: The Neutral Non-Metal Oxides

Not all non-metal oxides are acidic! A select group of non-metal oxides do NOT react with water to form acids, nor do they react with bases or acids. They have zero effect on both red and blue litmus papers and are known as Neutral Oxides:

1. Carbon Monoxide ($\text{CO}$)
Colorless, odorless, highly toxic neutral gas.
2. Nitric Oxide ($\text{NO}$)
Neutral radical gas with an odd number of electrons ($15e^-$).
3. Nitrous Oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$)
"Laughing gas", neutral anaesthetic oxide.
4. Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)
Amphiprotic / neutral oxide of hydrogen ($pH = 7$).
The Fluorine-Water Anomaly
While non-metals generally do not react with water, Fluorine ($\text{F}_2$) is the ultimate electronegative halogen. It reacts violently and spontaneously with liquid water, oxidising oxygen out of water to liberate oxygen and ozone gases:
$$2\text{F}_2(g) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 4\text{HF}(aq) + \text{O}_2(g)$$
CONCEPT PILLAR 3.3

The Storage Paradox & Human Biology Nitrogen

Thermodynamic storage paradox of white phosphorus vs. sodium and physiological manifestations of nitrogen.

The Great Storage Paradox: Sodium vs. White Phosphorus
Sodium Metal ($\text{Na}$)
Stored Under: Paraffin / Kerosene Oil.
Scientific Reason: Sodium reacts explosively with both liquid water and atmospheric water vapor ($2\text{Na} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2$), generating sufficient heat to ignite the evolved hydrogen gas. Kerosene is a hydrocarbon solvent that contains zero oxygen or moisture and does not react with sodium, keeping it completely inert.
White Phosphorus ($\text{P}_4$)
Stored Under: Cold Distilled Water.
Scientific Reason: White phosphorus has an exceptionally low ignition temperature (just $30^\circ\text{C}$ / $303\text{ K}$). Exposed to air at room temperature, its strained tetrahedral cage ($60^\circ$ bond angles) undergoes spontaneous combustion, forming dense toxic clouds of $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$. Because phosphorus is non-polar and completely insoluble and unreactive in polar water, water acts as an airtight blanket preventing atmospheric contact.
DO YOU KNOW? • TEXTBOOK CURIOSITY
Scuba Diving & "The Bends":
Ascending too rapidly to the surface during deep-sea diving causes dissolved nitrogen gas to suddenly bubble out of solution in the bloodstream due to pressure release (Henry's Law), painfully blocking blood capillaries—a life-threatening condition known as "the bends" (decompression sickness).
Knuckle Cracking Sound:
The distinct popping sound you hear when you crack your finger knuckles is not bones clicking—it is actually microscopic bubbles of dissolved nitrogen gas bursting and cavitating within the synovial fluid lubricating your joint capsules!

CONCEPT PILLAR 4.1

The Electrochemical Mechanism of Iron Rusting & Tarnish Profiles

Galvanic micro-cell reactions, hydrated ferric oxide formation, and corrosion kinetics of Copper and Silver.

Corrosion is an electrochemical oxidation process wherein microscopic galvanic cells spontaneously form on a metal surface exposed to atmospheric moisture, dissolved oxygen, and carbon dioxide:

Galvanic Micro-Cell Electrochemistry of Iron Rusting
1. Anodic Oxidation (Pit Formation):
Iron atoms at stressed regions of the lattice lose electrons and dissolve as ferrous ions:
$$\text{Fe}(s) \longrightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \quad (E^\circ = -0.44\text{ V})$$
2. Cathodic Reduction (Oxygen Depolarization):
Electrons travel through the metallic iron to the droplet perimeter where dissolved atmospheric oxygen is reduced in acidic water ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$):
$$\text{O}_2(g) + 4\text{H}^+(aq) + 4e^- \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \quad (E^\circ = +1.23\text{ V})$$
3. Overall Rust Precipitation:
Ferrous ions ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$) react with hydroxide ions and are further oxidized by atmospheric oxygen to yield brown hydrated ferric oxide: $$4\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + \text{O}_2(g) + (4 + 2x)\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2[\mathbf{\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O}}](s) \downarrow + 8\text{H}^+(aq)$$ Because rust is porous and crumbly, fresh iron beneath is continuously exposed until the structural object is completely eaten away.
Copper Patina Formation
Copper objects exposed to humid atmospheric air react slowly with carbon dioxide, water vapor, and oxygen to form an insoluble pale-green protective surface coating known as Basic Copper Carbonate (Verdigris/Patina):
$$2\text{Cu}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \mathbf{[\text{CuCO}_3 \cdot \text{Cu(OH)}_2]}(s) \quad (\text{Green})$$
Notable Example: The Statue of Liberty in New York Harbor is covered by this protective green patina.
Silver Tarnishing
Silver is a noble metal unreactive towards oxygen. However, it reacts slowly with airborne trace hydrogen sulphide gas ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$) present in industrial air, forming an unsightly black surface tarnish of Silver Sulphide ($\text{Ag}_2\text{S}$):
$$2\text{Ag}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{S}(g) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{Ag}_2\text{S}}(s) \quad (\text{Black Tarnish}) + \text{H}_2(g)$$

To Show That Rusting of Iron Requires Both Air (Oxygen) and Water Simultaneously
Materials Required: Three clean test tubes (marked A, B, and C), clean rust-free iron nails of identical size, tap water, boiled distilled water, turpentine/kerosene oil layer, anhydrous calcium chloride ($\text{CaCl}_2$) or silica gel, rubber corks.
Procedure:
  • Tube A: Place iron nails and pour tap water such that nails are half submerged in water and exposed to air. Leave open/loosely corked.
  • Tube B: Place iron nails in boiled distilled water (boiled for 10 min to purge all dissolved $\text{O}_2$) and pour $1\text{ mL}$ of turpentine oil on top to form an airtight seal. Cork tightly.
  • Tube C: Place iron nails in a completely dry tube containing anhydrous $\text{CaCl}_2$ (desiccant to absorb every trace of moisture). Seal with a tight rubber cork.
Leave all three tubes completely undisturbed for 4 to 6 days.
Observations:
  • In Tube A: The iron nails show extensive reddish-brown flaky rust ($\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O}$) accumulated along their surface and settled at the bottom.
  • In Tube B: The iron nails remain completely bright and rust-free (water is present, but atmospheric air/oxygen is excluded by the oil barrier).
  • In Tube C: The iron nails remain completely clean and rust-free (air/oxygen is present, but water moisture is eliminated by anhydrous $\text{CaCl}_2$).
Definitive Scientific Conclusion: Rusting of iron is an electrochemical process that requires the simultaneous presence of both water (moisture) and oxygen (air). Neither air alone nor moisture alone can induce rusting.

Modern Corrosion Prevention Technologies & Electroplating

Sacrificial galvanic protection, galvanization, anodizing, and industrial electroplating bath chemistry.

Industrial Corrosion Prevention Technologies
  • 1. Barrier Protection: Applying paint, varnish, enamel, grease, or oil to physically prevent atmospheric air and moisture from touching the underlying metal.
  • 2. Galvanization: Coating iron or steel with a thin protective layer of Zinc ($\text{Zn}$) by hot-dip immersion or electrodeposition. Zinc has a more negative reduction potential ($E^\circ = -0.76\text{ V}$) than iron ($E^\circ = -0.44\text{ V}$). Even if scratched, zinc acts as a sacrificial anode, corroding preferentially and supplying electrons to the exposed iron cathode!
  • 3. Anodizing: An electrochemical process used to thicken the natural protective oxide film on Aluminium ($\text{Al}$) articles. The clean aluminium object is made the anode in an electrolytic cell containing dilute sulphuric acid. Oxygen gas evolved at the anode reacts with aluminium to form an impervious, scratch-resistant $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ coat that easily dyes into brilliant colors.
  • 4. Electroplating: Electrolytic deposition of a superior, non-corrosive metal (Chromium, Nickel, Silver, Gold, Tin) onto a base metal article.

Principles of Industrial Metallurgy & Refining

The multi-stage chemical isolation of pure metals from ore bodies in the Earth's crust.

Four Universal Stages of Extractive Metallurgy
  1. Crushing & Grinding: Massive ore boulders are pulverized in jaw crushers and ball mills into fine powder.
  2. Concentration (Beneficiation / Dressing): Removal of unwanted rocky, siliceous impurities known as gangue or matrix using hydraulic washing, magnetic separation, or froth flotation (for sulphide ores).
  3. Conversion of Ore to Metal Oxide: Oxides are thermodynamically easier to reduce to elemental metal than carbonates or sulphides. Done via Calcination or Roasting.
  4. Reduction of Oxide to Metal: Removing oxygen from the metal oxide using Carbon (coke), Carbon Monoxide, Aluminium, or direct Electrolysis.
  5. Refining (Purification): Removing residual trace impurities to achieve $> 99.9\%$ purity (e.g., electrolytic refining).
Distinguishing Feature Calcination Roasting
Target Ores Used predominantly for Carbonate and Hydrated Ores (e.g., Calamine, Limestone, Bauxite). Used strictly for Sulphide Ores (e.g., Zinc Blende, Galena, Copper Pyrites).
Air / Oxygen Supply Ore is heated strongly in the absence or very limited supply of air below its melting point. Ore is heated strongly in a regular, excess supply of air below its melting point.
Chemical Transformations Volatile moisture and $\text{CO}_2$ gas are driven off, leaving a porous oxide:
$$\text{ZnCO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{ZnO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g) \uparrow \quad (\text{Calamine})$$
$$\text{CaCO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CaO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g) \uparrow \quad (\text{Limestone})$$ 		Sulphide is oxidized to metal oxide while volatile $\text{SO}_2$ gas escapes:
$$2\text{ZnS}(s) + 3\text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{ZnO}(s) + 2\text{SO}_2(g) \uparrow \quad (\text{Zinc Blende})$$
$$2\text{PbS}(s) + 3\text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{PbO}(s) + 2\text{SO}_2(g) \uparrow \quad (\text{Galena})$$
Electrolytic Refining of Blister Copper & Anode Mud

Crude copper extracted from smelters ($98\text{--}99\%$ pure) is refined to $99.99\%$ electrical grade via electrolysis:

  • Anode (+ve): A thick slab of impure crude blister copper.
  • Cathode (-ve): A thin sheet of pure electrolytic copper.
  • Electrolyte Bath: Acidified aqueous copper sulphate solution ($\text{CuSO}_4 + \text{dilute } \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$).

When an electric current passes through the bath, pure copper dissolves from the impure anode as $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions, travels across the electrolyte, and deposits electrochemically onto the pure cathode sheet:
$$\text{At Anode: } \text{Cu}(\text{impure}) \longrightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-$$
$$\text{At Cathode: } \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Cu}(\text{pure}, 99.99\%) \downarrow$$

Anode Mud: Insoluble noble metal impurities present in crude copper (such as Gold, Platinum, and Silver) do not ionize at this potential; they drop to the bottom below the anode as a rich sludge called Anode Mud, which is recovered industrially to offset the entire operational cost of refining!

Model Solution: Noble metals are metallic elements—specifically Gold ($\text{Au}$), Platinum ($\text{Pt}$), and Silver ($\text{Ag}$)—that occupy the lowest positions in the electrochemical reactivity series. They are termed "noble" because of their extreme chemical inertness: they do not react with atmospheric oxygen, moisture, carbon dioxide, or common dilute acids. Consequently, they retain their metallic luster over centuries and occur in the Earth's crust in their native (uncombined) metallic state.
Illustration 5 • Comparative Contrast
State the four principal physical and chemical differences between metals and non-metals.
Model Solution:
1. Ion Formation: Metals are electropositive and lose valence electrons to form cations ($\text{M} \to \text{M}^{n+} + ne^-$); non-metals are electronegative and gain electrons to form anions ($\text{X} + ne^- \to \text{X}^{n-}$).
2. Oxide Nature: Metal oxides are basic (or amphoteric) and turn red litmus blue ($\text{Na}_2\text{O} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{NaOH}$); non-metal oxides are acidic anhydrides that turn blue litmus red ($\text{SO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3$).
3. Malleability & Ductility: Metals can be hammered into thin foils and drawn into wires; non-metals are brittle solids or gases.
4. Electrical Conductivity: Metals conduct electricity via delocalized sea of electrons; non-metals (except graphite) lack mobile charge carriers and act as electrical insulators.

Sulphur ($\text{S}$):
(i) Manufacturing sulphuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, the "King of Chemicals" via Contact Process).
(ii) Vulcanization of Natural Rubber: Cross-linking polyisoprene chains with sulphur bridges to make rubber tough and heat-resistant.
(iii) Antiseptic skin ointments, dermatological soaps, and agricultural fungicides.
Illustration 7 • Corrosion Prevention Strategies
Describe five distinct methods utilized industrially to prevent the corrosion of metals.
Model Solution:
1. Barrier Painting & Greasing: Applying a protective coat of paint, lacquer, grease, or oil to physically prevent atmospheric oxygen and moisture from contacting the metal surface.
2. Galvanization: Depositing a thin protective zinc layer over iron/steel by hot-dip immersion. Zinc preferentially oxidizes and provides sacrificial cathodic protection even if scratched.
3. Electroplating: Electrolytically coating iron objects with a superior, non-corrosive metal such as Chromium ($\text{Cr}$), Nickel ($\text{Ni}$), or Tin ($\text{Sn}$).
4. Alloying: Homogeneously melting iron with chromium ($18\%$) and nickel ($8\%$) to formulate Stainless Steel, which forms an impervious self-healing chromium oxide nanolayer.
5. Anodizing: Electrolytically thickening the natural protective aluminium oxide ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) film on aluminium articles to render them completely unreactive.

Checkpoint 1.1: Why do metals conduct electricity even in the solid state, whereas ionic compounds like $NaCl$ only conduct when molten or aqueous?
Answer: Metals possess a delocalized sea of free, mobile electrons that move rapidly under an electric potential gradient in the solid crystal lattice. In contrast, solid $NaCl$ has its ions locked tightly into fixed positions by rigid electrostatic ionic lattice forces. In solid state, $NaCl$ has no mobile ions or free electrons; ions only become free to migrate when the lattice melts or dissolves in water.
Checkpoint 2.1: Why is Aluminium foil used for wrapping food items rather than copper foil or iron sheet?
Answer: Aluminium is exceptionally malleable, allowing it to be rolled into ultra-thin ($0.016\text{ mm}$), lightweight, flexible sheets at low cost. Furthermore, Aluminium rapidly forms an invisible, impermeable, non-toxic passivating oxide film ($Al_2O_3$) on its surface that prevents the metal from reacting with food moisture or acids under neutral conditions, while copper can form toxic verdigris salts.
Checkpoint 3.1: How do you confirm that the gas evolved when Zinc reacts with dilute sulfuric acid is indeed Hydrogen?
Answer: Pass the evolved gas through a soap solution so that gas-filled bubbles rise into the air. Bring a burning candle near a soap bubble. The bubble bursts with a characteristic sharp "POP" sound! This occurs because hydrogen burns explosively in atmospheric oxygen: $2H_2\text{ (g)} + O_2\text{ (g)} \longrightarrow 2H_2O\text{ (g)} + \text{Energy}$.
Checkpoint 4.1: If a student places a clean strip of Lead ($Pb$) into a beaker of Zinc Sulfate ($ZnSO_4$), will any reaction occur? Explain why or why not.
Answer: NO reaction will occur! In the reactivity series, Zinc ($Zn$) lies well above Lead ($Pb$). Zinc has a much higher oxidation potential and stronger electropositive character than Lead. Therefore, Lead is thermodynamically incapable of donating electrons to $Zn^{2+}$ ions, so $ZnSO_4$ remains completely unaffected.
Checkpoint 5.1: If sulfur powder is burned in a deflagrating spoon and the gas collected in a jar with a little water, what will happen when red and blue litmus papers are introduced?
Answer: Burning sulfur produces sulfur dioxide gas ($S + O_2 \longrightarrow SO_2$). When shaken with water, it forms sulfurous acid ($SO_2 + H_2O \longrightarrow H_2SO_3$).
• The blue litmus paper will turn RED, confirming the acidic nature of the non-metallic oxide.
• The red litmus paper will remain RED (no change).
Checkpoint 6.1: Why does an iron nail rust rapidly in ordinary tap water, but does NOT rust at all in water that has been boiled and sealed with a layer of oil?
Answer: Rusting strictly requires the simultaneous presence of BOTH oxygen and water. Boiling the water completely expels all dissolved oxygen gas ($O_2$). The layer of poured oil forms an airtight barrier that prevents atmospheric oxygen from re-dissolving. Without dissolved oxygen to act as the cathodic electron acceptor, the electrochemical rusting reaction cannot occur, so the iron nail remains 100% rust-free!
1. Why does an iron knife dipped in a blue copper sulfate solution acquire a reddish-brown coating, but a silver spoon dipped in copper sulfate shows no change?
Answer: Iron is higher than Copper in the reactivity series ($Fe > Cu$), so Iron donates electrons to reduce $Cu^{2+}$ to reddish-brown Copper metal ($Fe + CuSO_4 \rightarrow FeSO_4 + Cu\downarrow$). In contrast, Silver is lower than Copper ($Ag < Cu$), so Silver cannot displace copper from solution ($Ag + CuSO_4 \rightarrow \text{No Reaction}$).
2. What is the composition of the green patina that forms on old copper monuments like the Statue of Liberty? Write its balanced chemical equation.
Answer: The green patina is Basic Copper Carbonate, an equimolar complex of copper carbonate and copper hydroxide: $\mathbf{CuCO_3 \cdot Cu(OH)_2}$.
Balanced Equation: $2Cu\text{ (s)} + H_2O\text{ (l)} + CO_2\text{ (g)} + O_2\text{ (g)} \longrightarrow CuCO_3 \cdot Cu(OH)_2\text{ (s)}$.
3. Why does pure 24-carat gold not tarnish or rust over thousands of years, whereas 22-carat gold may show slight surface dulling in industrial cities?
Answer: Pure 24K gold is a chemically inert noble metal with an extremely high positive reduction potential ($+1.50\text{ V}$), making it completely unreactive with oxygen, moisture, and $H_2S$. However, 22K gold is an alloy containing $8.4\%$ Copper ($Cu$) or Silver ($Ag$). In polluted city air containing traces of $H_2S$ and moisture, the constituent copper/silver atoms at the surface slowly form black $Ag_2S$ or $CuO$, causing slight surface dulling!
4. Why is Galvanized iron preferred over Tinned iron for building water pipes and roofing sheets? What happens if both are scratched?
Answer: Zinc is more reactive than iron ($Zn > Fe$), while Tin is less reactive than iron ($Sn < Fe$).
• If Galvanized Iron is scratched: Zinc continues to protect iron sacrificially because Zinc has a more negative reduction potential and corrodes preferentially.
• If Tinned Iron is scratched: Iron is more reactive than Tin. At the scratch site, Iron becomes the anode and corrodes MUCH FASTER than if the tin wasn't there at all (intense galvanic pitting corrosion)!
