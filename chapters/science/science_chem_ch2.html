Section 4: Reactivity Series & Displacement Reactions (7 Questions)

The reactivity hierarchy, displacement of less reactive metals from aqueous salt solutions, and non-feasible reactions.

Q15 Very Short Answer
Name the reaction in which more reactive metal displaces less reactive metal from its salt solution.
Model Textbook Answer:
Displacement reaction.
Q16 Very Short Answer
Arrange Ca, Zn, Fe, Au, Ag and Cu in order of decreasing reactivity.
Model Textbook Answer:
Ca > Zn > Fe > Cu > Ag > Au.
Q17 Very Short Answer
What is the most common property of the metals lying at the bottom of the reactivity series?
Model Textbook Answer:
They are highly unreactive (noble metals) and do not easily form compounds.
Q18 Short Answer
What will happen if you drop (a) some pieces of magnesium ribbon into blue copper sulphate solution? (b) some tin pieces into green ferrous sulphate solution?
Model Textbook Answer:
(a) Magnesium displaces copper, turning the solution colourless (magnesium sulphate) and depositing brown copper. (b) No reaction occurs because tin is less reactive than iron.
Q19 Short Answer
Arrange the metals Na, Ca and Mg in decreasing order of reactivity on the basis of reaction with water.
Model Textbook Answer:
Na > Ca > Mg.
Q20 Short Answer
Explain why aluminium metal can displace copper from copper sulphate solution but copper cannot displace aluminium from aluminium sulphate solution?
Model Textbook Answer:
Aluminium is more reactive than copper, so it can displace copper. Copper, being less reactive, cannot displace aluminium.
Q21 Long Answer
Define displacement reaction. An iron knife kept in blue copper sulphate solution turns the blue solution into light green. Explain.

A displacement reaction is a reaction in which a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution. Iron is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from copper sulphate, forming light green iron(II) sulphate and brown solid copper.

4 Core Concept Pillars

Visual synthesis of core principles connecting physical and chemical behavior of metals and non-metals.

Pillar 1: Physical Spectrum (Metals vs Non-Metals)
Malleability & Ductility

Metals can be beaten into thin sheets (e.g. Gold, Silver, Aluminium foils) and drawn into fine wires (Copper, Gold). Non-metals shatter upon impact (brittle).

Electrical & Thermal Conductivity

Metals have sea of delocalized electrons facilitating high conductivity (Silver > Copper > Aluminium). Non-metals are insulators, except Graphite.

Key Anomalies & Exceptions

Mercury (Hg) is liquid at room temp; Gallium (Ga) & Caesium (Cs) melt on human palm (m.p. < 30°C); Diamond is the hardest natural substance; Iodine crystals are lustrous.

Pillar 2: Chemical Reactivity (The Elemental Ladder)
Reaction with Oxygen

Metals produce basic oxides (2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO). Amphoteric oxides (Al2O3, ZnO) react with both acids and bases. Non-metals form acidic oxides (S + O2 -> SO2).

Reaction with Water

Na and K react violently with cold water, evolving H2 which catches fire (stored in kerosene). Mg reacts with hot water. Fe and Zn react only with steam.

Reaction with Acids

Metal + Acid -> Metal Salt + H2 gas (burns with pop sound). Metals below hydrogen in the reactivity series (Cu, Ag, Au) do not displace H2 from dilute acids.

Pillar 3: Displacement Dynamics & Salt Solutions
The Reactivity Hierarchy

K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au. Highly electropositive metals readily lose valence electrons to form cations.

Single Displacement Rule

A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its aqueous salt solution: Fe + CuSO4 (blue) -> FeSO4 (pale green) + Cu (reddish brown deposit).

Equilibrium & Non-Reactivity

A less reactive metal cannot displace a more reactive metal: Cu + FeSO4 -> No Reaction; Cu + ZnSO4 -> No Reaction.

Pillar 4: Corrosion Prevention & Metallurgy (Alloys)
Corrosion Chemistry

Rusting requires both oxygen and moisture: 4Fe + 3O2 + 2xH2O -> 2Fe2O3·xH2O. Copper tarnishes to green basic carbonate Cu(OH)2·CuCO3; Silver blackens to Ag2S.

Barrier & Sacrificial Protection

Painting, oiling/greasing, electroplating, and galvanization (coating iron with sacrificial zinc which oxidizes preferentially).

Engineering Alloys

Brass (Cu+Zn), Bronze (Cu+Sn), Stainless Steel (Fe+Cr+Ni+C), Duralumin (Al+Cu+Mg+Mn), Solder (Pb+Sn), and Amalgams (alloy with Mercury).

Ancient Metallurgy
The Rustless Iron Pillar of Delhi
Standing over 1,600 years near Qutub Minar without significant corrosion!
Forged during the Gupta dynasty (around 400 CE), the 7.2-meter-tall iron pillar contains a remarkably high phosphorus content (approx. 0.28%) and low sulfur. This unique ancient metallurgy caused the spontaneous formation of a protective passive film of crystalline iron hydrogen phosphate hydrate (FePO4·H3PO4·4H2O), scientifically known as 'misawite', shielding the iron from atmospheric moisture for over sixteen centuries.
Elemental Marvels
Liquid Metals & Palm-Melting Elements
Gallium and Caesium melt right in your warm palm!
While Mercury (Hg) remains liquid down to -38.8°C, Gallium (Ga) has a melting point of just 29.76°C (85.6°F) and Caesium (Cs) melts at 28.4°C. Because normal human core and surface skin temperature is roughly 34-37°C, holding a solid solid piece of pure Gallium in your hand causes it to absorb body heat and liquefy into an iridescent metallic puddle before your eyes.
Chemical Safety
Why Sodium in Kerosene, but Phosphorus in Water?
Contrasting extremes of alkali metals vs reactive non-metals.
Sodium (Na) is an alkali metal that reacts explosively with water to liberate combustible hydrogen gas (2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2 + Heat); thus it must be isolated in inert liquid hydrocarbons like kerosene oil. Conversely, White Phosphorus (P4) is a non-metal with an auto-ignition temperature of just 35°C that violently catches fire in air, yet does not react with water—so it is stored safely submerged under water.
Smart Materials
Shape-Memory Metals: The Wonder of Nitinol
Metals that remember their original shape when heated.
Nitinol is an equiatomic alloy of Nickel (Ni) and Titanium (Ti). At low temperatures, it can be twisted, crumpled, or stretched into arbitrary shapes. Upon gentle exposure to hot water or electrical resistance heating, Nitinol undergoes a reversible, diffusionless solid-state phase transformation (from Martensite to Austenite), instantly snapping back to its original pre-programmed structure. It powers heart stents, flexible eyeglasses, and space rover actuators.
