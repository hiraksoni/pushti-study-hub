The First Indian Empires (The Mauryas)

Rise of India's first unified subcontinental empire under Chandragupta Maurya and Chanakya, the administrative hierarchy detailed in Arthashastra and Indica, Ashoka's military conquests, the pivotal Kalinga War transformation, and propagation of Dhamma via rock and pillar edicts.

1. Periodisation of History

  • Historians organise history into distinct periods based on major political, religious, and socio-economic changes to make the study of the past structured and meaningful.
  • Ancient Period: Extends from prehistoric times up to around 700 CE (covering the Indus Valley, Vedic Era, Janapadas, and the Mauryan & Gupta empires).
  • Medieval Period: Extends from around 700 CE to around 1700 CE (covering the Sultanates, regional kingdoms, and the Mughal Empire).
  • Modern Period: Extends from 1700 CE to the present day (covering colonial expansion, freedom struggle, and post-independence India).

2. Rise of the Mauryan Empire

Kingdom

A smaller territorial unit ruled by a king or queen. Governed with smaller regional forces and relatively simple revenue collection.

Empire

A vast multi-province realm ruled by an emperor. Commands large standing armies, a vast administrative bureaucracy, and centralized tax revenues.

  • Magadha was a prosperous and powerful kingdom, but its last Nanda ruler, Dhana Nanda, was widely unpopular and oppressive.
  • A brilliant political philosopher and teacher from Taxila, Chanakya (Kautilya), resolved to overthrow Dhana Nanda. He identified and trained a talented young warrior named Chandragupta Maurya in warfare and statecraft.
  • In 321 BCE, Chandragupta defeated Dhana Nanda, established control over Magadha from Pataliputra, and founded the Mauryan Empire.
  • Chandragupta expanded the empire across northern India and defeated Seleucus Nicator (Alexander's general), securing Afghanistan and Baluchistan.
  • Dynastic Lineage: Chandragupta Maurya was succeeded by his son Bindusara (~297 BCE), who further expanded into central India, followed by Bindusara's son Ashoka (~268 BCE).

3. Ashoka the Great, Kalinga War & Dhamma

  • Ashoka ascended the throne in 268 BCE and is acclaimed as one of the greatest emperors in world history.
  • In 261 BCE, he fought a fierce military campaign to conquer the independent coastal kingdom of Kalinga (modern Odisha).
  • Although Ashoka emerged victorious, the sight of over 100,000 deaths, 150,000 captives, and widespread devastation filled him with overwhelming remorse and sorrow.
  • Ashoka made the historic pledge to give up warfare forever. He remains the only emperor in human history to renounce military conquest after a decisive victory.
  • From Bherighosha to Dhammaghosha: The sound of war drums (*Bherighosha*) was replaced by the proclamation of righteousness (*Dhammaghosha*).
  • Ashoka's Dhamma: Inspired by Buddhism, Dhamma was a universal moral and ethical code:
    • Compassion and non-violence (*ahimsa*) towards all living beings (humans and animals).
    • Obedience and respect towards parents, teachers, and elders.
    • Kind and humane treatment of servants, slaves, and laborers.
    • Religious tolerance and mutual respect among all sects and beliefs.
    • Discouraged meaningless rituals, animal sacrifices, and extravagant social ceremonies.
  • Propagation: Ashoka appointed dedicated civil officers called Dhamma Mahamattas to teach moral principles. He had his edicts inscribed on rocks and polished monolithic pillars in Prakrit (Brahmi script), Greek, and Aramaic.
  • He built hospitals for humans and animals, dug wells along highways, planted shade trees, and constructed rest-houses (*sarais*).

4. Primary Historical Sources of the Mauryas

  • Arthashastra (Book): Authored in Sanskrit by Chanakya (Kautilya). It is a master treatise detailing governance, ministerial duties, legal codes, municipal administration, military tactics, and taxation policies.
  • Indica (Traveller's Account): Written by Megasthenes, the Greek ambassador sent by Seleucus Nicator to Chandragupta's court. It provides an eyewitness record of Pataliputra's wooden palaces, municipal administration, and daily life.
  • Ashokan Inscriptions (Edicts): Engraved on rocks and towering polished stone pillars across India, Pakistan, and Afghanistan, providing direct imperial records in Brahmi and Kharosthi scripts.
  • Monuments & Archaeology: Buddhist monuments such as the Sanchi Stupa (Madhya Pradesh) and the monolithic polished Lion Capital at Sarnath.
  • Coins: Punch-marked silver and copper coins bearing imperial symbols used for trade and commerce.

5. Imperial Administration & Socio-Economic Life

  • Vast Geographical Extent: Stretched from present-day Afghanistan and the Hindu Kush in the northwest to Karnataka in the south, and from Bengal in the east to Kathiawar in the west.
  • Capital Administration: The emperor directly administered the capital city of Pataliputra and surrounding heartland, aided by the Mantrin Parishad (Council of Ministers).
  • Provincial Administration: The vast empire was divided into major provinces centered around strategic cities:
    • Taxila: Gateway to the northwest and Central Asian trade routes.
    • Ujjain: Vital trade junction connecting northern and southern India.
    • Provinces were governed by royal princes designated as Kumaras (Governors).
  • Economic Life & Agriculture: Agriculture was the principal livelihood. The state cleared forests, built irrigation canals, tanks, and dams (*Sudarshana Lake*).
  • Craft Guilds (Shrenis): Artisans, weavers, and metalworkers organized into autonomous guilds called Shrenis, which set product standards, trained apprentices, and provided banking services.
  • Taxation: Systematic taxes were collected in cash and kind from farmers (*bhaga* - usually 1/6th of produce), herders, artisans, and merchants, funding public works and the army.
  • Society & Position of Women: The four-fold Vedic Varna system was followed. Women enjoyed respect and were legally allowed to own personal property (*stridhana*), though society was patriarchal.

6. Causes of the Decline of the Mauryan Empire

  • Weak Successor Rulers: After Ashoka's demise in 232 BCE, subsequent monarchs lacked the administrative vigor and military authority required to hold the vast realm together.
  • Excessive Territorial Size: The enormous geographical expanse made centralized administration, rapid communication, and tax enforcement from Pataliputra nearly impossible.
  • External Invasions: Weakened northwestern borders allowed foreign powers (Bactrian Greeks and Sakas) to breach the frontiers.
  • Military Coup in 185 BCE: In 185 BCE, the last Mauryan ruler, Brihadratha, was assassinated during an army review by his own commander-in-chief, Pushyamitra Shunga, who founded the Shunga Dynasty.

7. Master Exam Glossary (Quick-Audit Reference)

Use this authoritative vocabulary table to rapidly cross-verify terms appearing in any school exam question paper:

Term / Name Authoritative Historical Definition & Context
Empire A massive territory comprising diverse regions and cultures, ruled by an emperor, with standing armies and centralized revenue.
Dynasty A succession of rulers belonging to the same royal family ruling one after another (e.g., Chandragupta → Bindusara → Ashoka).
Dhamma Prakrit word for Dharma; an ethical code of conduct introduced by Ashoka emphasizing non-violence, truth, and respect.
Dhamma Mahamattas Special imperial officers appointed by Ashoka to travel across cities and villages preaching moral living and checking injustice.
Shrenis Autonomous guilds or associations formed by craftspeople and merchants to protect trade interests, regulate prices, and provide credit.
Mantrin Parishad The high-level advisory Council of Ministers assisting the Mauryan Emperor in policy-making and day-to-day administration.
Kumaras Royal princes appointed as provincial governors in strategic centers like Taxila and Ujjain.
Arthashastra The Sanskrit treatise on political statecraft, diplomacy, and economic administration authored by Chanakya (Kautilya).
Indica The Greek descriptive account of Indian society, geography, and Pataliputra's municipal government authored by ambassador Megasthenes.
Edicts Official royal orders, decrees, and teachings engraved on rocks and monolithic stone pillars throughout the empire.
Pushyamitra Shunga The Mauryan army general who assassinated the last ruler Brihadratha in 185 BCE, founding the Shunga dynasty.
