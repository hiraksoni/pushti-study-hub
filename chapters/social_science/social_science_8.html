B. Nalanda Mahavihara (Studied by Xuanzang / Hiuen Tsang)

Gatekeeper Admission Test

According to Xuanzang, the university gatekeeper (*Dwarapala*) personally interrogated aspiring scholars with tough philosophical riddles. Only 20% to 30% passed the entrance gate to gain admission.

Comprehensive Curriculum

Students studied Mahayana and Hinayana Buddhist philosophy, the Vedas, Hetu-vidya (logic), Shabda-vidya (Sanskrit grammar), Chikitsa-vidya (medicine), and astronomy under Chancellor Shilabhadra.

Royal Endowments

Harshavardhana and Gupta kings granted the revenues of over 100 villages to Nalanda, providing 10,000 resident monks and scholars with free food, clothing, bedding, and medicines.

The 7 Classical Pillars of the Guptas, Harsha & Southern Dynasties

Pillar 1 · Imperial Foundation c. 320 CE Genesis

Foundation of the Gupta Empire: Chandragupta I & Maharajadhiraja

Founded by Sri Gupta and Ghatotkacha (who bore the modest title *Maharaja*), the dynasty rose to imperial sovereignty under **Chandragupta I (~320 CE)**. By marrying Princess Kumaradevi of the influential Licchavi clan, Chandragupta I consolidated his northern territory and assumed the grand title **Maharajadhiraja** (King of Kings). He inaugurated the Gupta Era (Gupta Samvat) in 319–320 CE.

Why It Mattered: The Licchavi alliance provided military prestige and fertile Gangetic lands, elevating the Guptas above rival regional feudatories.
Exam Trap: Do NOT confuse Chandragupta I (Gupta, 320 CE) with Chandragupta Maurya (Maurya, 321 BCE, 600 years earlier!).
Pillar 2 · Military Genius Prayag Prashasti

Samudragupta & Harishena's Prayag Prashasti (The Napoleon of India)

Son of Chandragupta I, **Samudragupta (~335–380 CE)** was an invincible military commander, poet, and musician (depicted playing the veena on his gold coins). His court poet **Harishena** engraved a 33-line panegyric poem (*prashasti*) in ornate classical Sanskrit (*champu* style) on the Ashokan monolithic pillar at Prayag (Allahabad). Harishena outlined four distinct imperial conquest policies based on geographic proximity and strategic logistics.

Pillar 3 · The Golden Age Zenith Navratna Court

Chandragupta II Vikramaditya: Shaka Defeat & The Nine Gems

Son of Samudragupta, **Chandragupta II (~380–415 CE)** took the title **Vikramaditya** after crushing the Western Shakas (Kshatrapas) of Gujarat and Malwa. This conquest gave the empire direct access to western Arabian Sea ports (Bharuch, Sopara), causing international maritime trade with Rome and the Near East to surge. His royal court was adorned by the **Navratnas (Nine Gems)**, including India's greatest Sanskrit playwright **Kalidasa** (*Shakuntala*, *Meghaduta*) and astronomer **Varahamihira**.

Pillar 4 · Scientific Renaissance Aryabhata & Iron Pillar

Scientific Masterworks: Aryabhata & The Rustless Iron Pillar of Delhi

Gupta intellectuals pioneered pathbreaking discoveries:
• **Aryabhata**: In his astronomical treatise *Aryabhatiyam*, he proved that the Earth rotates on its own axis, accurately calculated the solar year (365.258 days), formulated the value of π (3.1416), and explained that eclipses are caused by planetary shadows rather than mythical demons.
• **Iron Pillar of Delhi (Mehrauli)**: 7.2 meters high and weighing 6 tonnes, this wrought-iron pillar with an inscription to King 'Chandra' has stood exposed to monsoon rains and extremes for 1,600 years without rusting—testimony to advanced metallurgical oxidation resistance.

Pillar 5 · 7th Century Re-Unification 606 – 647 CE

Harshavardhana: Kannauj Empire & The Battle of Narmada

Following the White Hun (Huna) invasions that collapsed the Gupta core, **Harshavardhana (606–647 CE)** ascended the throne of Thaneshwar and united Northern India, shifting his capital to the strategic trade citadel of **Kannauj**. His court poet **Banabhatta** authored the biography *Harshacharita*. When Harsha attempted to march into the Deccan plateau, his army was decisively halted on the banks of the Narmada River by the Chalukya sovereign **Pulakeshin II** (recorded by court poet Ravikirti in the **Aihole Inscription**).

Pillar 6 · Primary Chinese Chronicles Fa-Hien & Xuanzang

Eyewitness Chronicles: Fa-Hien (Gupta Era) & Xuanzang (Harsha Era)

• **Fa-Hien (~400 CE)**: Visited during Chandragupta II's reign to collect authentic Buddhist Vinaya texts. He observed peaceful social conditions: low crime, no capital punishment, lenient judicial fines, vegetarianism among Buddhist laypeople, but noted the harsh social exclusion of 'untouchables' (Chandalas).
• **Xuanzang (~630–645 CE)**: Spent over 15 years in India during Harsha's reign, studying at Nalanda University under Chancellor Shilabhadra and attending Harsha's grand assemblies at Kannauj and Prayag.

Pillar 7 · Peninsular Powers Southern Dynasties

Southern Powers: Pallavas of Kanchi & Chalukyas of Badami

• **Pallavas of Kanchipuram**: Ruled northern Tamil Nadu. Under Mahendravarman I and Narasimhavarman I (Mamalla), they pioneered monolithic rock-cut shrines (Pancha Rathas at Mahabalipuram) and the structural Shore Temple, defeating Pulakeshin II and sacking Vatapi.
• **Chalukyas of Badami (Vatapi)**: Dominated Karnataka and the Raichur Doab between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers. Their commercial and religious center was **Aihole** ('Cradle of Temple Architecture', home to the 6th-century Durga Temple).

Section 8: Primary Epigraphy & Artifact Matrix

1. Prayag Prashasti (Allahabad Pillar)

c. 375 CE

Composed by court poet Harishena in Classical Sanskrit kavya style on a polished sandstone pillar originally erected by Emperor Ashoka. It records Samudragupta's genealogical descent, his battles, his 4 conquest policies, and his musical talents as a maestro of the veena.

2. Iron Pillar of Delhi (Mehrauli)

c. 400 CE

A 7.2-meter-high monolithic wrought-iron pillar weighing over 6,000 kg, situated in the Qutb Complex. It commemorates King 'Chandra' (Chandragupta II) and his conquest of Vanga (Bengal) and Balkh (Bactria). Its advanced phosphorus-rich passive iron-oxide film has protected it from corrosion for over 1,600 years.

3. Aihole Inscription of Pulakeshin II

634 CE

Written by Jain court poet Ravikirti at the Meguti Jain temple in Aihole (Karnataka). Composed in high Sanskrit poetry, it recounts Pulakeshin II's military triumphs, specifically describing how Harsha lost his 'harsha' (joy) after his defeat at the Narmada River.

4. Monolithic Rathas of Mahabalipuram

c. 650 CE

Carved out of single massive granite boulders under Pallava King Narasimhavarman I (Mamalla). Named after the Pandavas (Dharmaraja, Bhima, Arjuna, Nakula-Sahadeva, and Draupadi), they represent the earliest rock-cut Dravidian temple architecture.
