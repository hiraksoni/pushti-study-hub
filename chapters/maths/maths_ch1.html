TOPIC 01 • PLACE VALUE & NOTATION

The Indian & International Numeration Systems & Powers of 10

Periods, comma separation rules, expanded notation using exponents ($10^n$), and place value charts.

Illustrations 1–5

The Indian System of Numeration

In the Indian system, digits are grouped into periods starting from the right:

  • Ones Period (3 digits): Ones ($10^0$), Tens ($10^1$), Hundreds ($10^2$).
  • Thousands Period (2 digits): Thousands ($10^3$), Ten Thousands ($10^4$).
  • Lakhs Period (2 digits): Lakhs ($10^5$), Ten Lakhs ($10^6$).
  • Crores Period (2 digits): Crores ($10^7$), Ten Crores ($10^8$).
  • Arabs Period (2 digits): Arabs ($10^9$), Ten Arabs ($10^{10}$).
Comma Rule: 3 digits, then every 2 digits → 9,87,65,43,210

The International System of Numeration

Used globally and in American scientific literature. Every single period consists of exactly 3 digits:

  • Ones Period (3 digits): Ones ($10^0$), Tens ($10^1$), Hundreds ($10^2$).
  • Thousands Period (3 digits): Thousands ($10^3$), Ten Thousands ($10^4$), Hundred Thousands ($10^5$).
  • Millions Period (3 digits): Millions ($10^6$), Ten Millions ($10^7$), Hundred Millions ($10^8$).
  • Billions Period (3 digits): Billions ($10^9$), Ten Billions ($10^{10}$), Hundred Billions ($10^{11}$).
Comma Rule: Every 3 digits from right → 9,876,543,210

Face Value of a Digit

The intrinsic value of the digit itself, completely independent of its position in the numeral. For example, in $75,82,419$, the face value of $8$ is simply $8$.

Place Value of a Digit

$\text{Place Value} = \text{Face Value} \times \text{Value of the Place (Power of 10)}$. In $75,82,419$, digit $8$ is at Ten Thousands place ($10^4$). Hence: $\text{Place Value} = 8 \times 10,000 = \mathbf{80,000}$.

TOPIC 02 • ORDERING & PERMUTATIONS

Comparing Numbers, Ascending/Descending Order & Digit Permutations

Positional comparison algorithms, forming greatest and smallest numbers under constrained positions.

Illustrations 6–9

Rule 1: Count the Number of Digits

The number with more digits is always greater than the number with fewer digits.
Example: $1,00,24,510$ ($8$ digits) is strictly greater than $98,76,543$ ($7$ digits), irrespective of the starting digits!

Rule 2: Left-to-Right Positional Comparison

If both numbers possess the exact same number of digits, compare digits starting from the extreme left (highest place value). The number with the larger digit at the first differing position is greater.

TOPIC 03 • APPROXIMATIONS

Exact vs. Approximate Values & Nearest Neighbours Rounding

Rounding rules (weak vs strong digits) to nearest 10, 100, 1000, 10,000, Lakh, and Million.

Illustrations 10–11

Exact Values

A value that is completely accurate and not rounded or estimated in any way. Used for discrete countable items (e.g. "There are exactly 42 students in class", "A cricket team has 11 players").

Approximate Values

A convenient number close to the actual value, obtained by rounding off. Used when exact numbers are either unknown, fluctuating, or practically unnecessary (e.g. "50,000 spectators attended the match").

The 3-Step Universal Rounding Off Algorithm
  1. Identify the Target Place: Highlight the digit at the place value you want to round to (e.g. Tens, Hundreds, Thousands).
  2. Inspect the Decision Digit (Immediate Right Neighbor):
    • If neighbor is $0, 1, 2, 3, 4$ (Weak Digits): Leave the target digit unchanged (Round Down).
    • If neighbor is $5, 6, 7, 8, 9$ (Strong Digits): Increase the target digit by $1$ (Round Up).
  3. Zero Out Remaining Places: Replace all digits to the right of the target place with zeros ($0$).

The Product Digit Count Theorem (Olympiad Core)

When an $m$-digit number is multiplied by an $n$-digit number, how many digits can the product have?

Number of Digits = Either $(m + n - 1)$ or $(m + n)$

Proof by Boundary Extremes:
Smallest $m$-digit number is $10^{m-1}$; smallest $n$-digit number is $10^{n-1}$.
$$\text{Minimum Product} = 10^{m-1} \times 10^{n-1} = 10^{(m+n-2)} \implies \text{has } (m + n - 1) \text{ digits}$$ Largest $m$-digit number is $< 10^m$; largest $n$-digit number is $< 10^n$.
$$\text{Maximum Product} < 10^m \times 10^n = 10^{m+n} \implies \text{has at most } (m + n) \text{ digits}$$

Palindromic Repunit Squares

$1 \times 1 = 1$
$11 \times 11 = 121$
$111 \times 111 = 12,321$
$1,111 \times 1,111 = 1,234,321$
$11,111 \times 11,111 = 123,454,321$
$111,111 \times 111,111 = 12,345,654,321$

Roman Numerals Master Key

Symbols: $\text{I}=1, \text{V}=5, \text{X}=10, \text{L}=50, \text{C}=100, \text{D}=500, \text{M}=1000$.

  • $\text{V}, \text{L}, \text{D}$ are never repeated, never subtracted.
  • $\text{I}$ can be subtracted from $\text{V}$ and $\text{X}$ only.
  • $\text{X}$ can be subtracted from $\text{L}$ and $\text{C}$ only.
  • $\text{C}$ can be subtracted from $\text{D}$ and $\text{M}$ only.
  • A bar over a symbol ($\overline{\text{V}}$) multiplies its value by $1,000$.

Problem: How many total 6-digit, 7-digit, and 8-digit natural numbers exist in the decimal number system? Derive the universal formula for the total number of $n$-digit numbers.

Self-Practice Mode: Solve in your notebook before revealing the solution.
General Theorem & Proof:

In base-10, any $n$-digit number ranges from the smallest $n$-digit number ($10^{n-1}$) to the greatest $n$-digit number ($10^n - 1$).
The total count $T_n$ of numbers in an inclusive integer range $[A, B]$ is given by: $$T_n = \text{Greatest } n\text{-digit} - \text{Smallest } n\text{-digit} + 1$$ $$T_n = (10^n - 1) - 10^{n-1} + 1 = 10^n - 10^{n-1} = 10^{n-1}(10 - 1) = \mathbf{9 \times 10^{n-1}}$$

HOTS 2: Product Digit Range Theorem Proof

Problem: Prove rigorously that the product of an $m$-digit number and an $n$-digit number can have only either $(m + n - 1)$ or $(m + n)$ digits, and cannot have any other number of digits.

Self-Practice Mode: Solve in your notebook before revealing the solution.
Mathematical Rigorous Proof:

Let $A$ be an $m$-digit integer and $B$ be an $n$-digit integer.
By definition of decimal place values: $$10^{m-1} \le A < 10^m$$ $$10^{n-1} \le B < 10^n$$ Multiplying both strict inequalities: $$10^{m-1} \times 10^{n-1} \le A \times B < 10^m \times 10^n$$ Applying the law of exponents $10^a \times 10^b = 10^{a+b}$: $$10^{(m+n-2)} \le A \times B < 10^{(m+n)}$$ Since $A \times B$ is an integer strictly less than $10^{m+n}$, the maximum possible value is $10^{m+n} - 1$, which has exactly $(m + n)$ digits.
Also, $A \times B \ge 10^{(m+n-2)}$, but more precisely, the smallest possible product is: $$\text{Min}(A) \times \text{Min}(B) = 10^{m-1} \times 10^{n-1} = 10^{m+n-2}$$ A power of $10$, namely $10^k$, has $(k + 1)$ digits. Therefore, $10^{m+n-2}$ has $(m + n - 2 + 1) = \mathbf{(m + n - 1)}$ digits.
Since the product is bounded between an $(m+n-1)$-digit minimum and an $(m+n)$-digit maximum, the number of digits in $A \times B$ is strictly either $(m + n - 1)$ or $(m + n)$. $\quad \blacksquare$

Trap 1: The Zero-Digit Place Value Omission

When writing numerals from words (e.g. "Nine crore five lakh forty-one"), students mistakenly write 9500041 (7 digits).

Trap 2: The "Distributive Product" Fallacy

In general rule product estimation, students often round to a single common place instead of rounding each factor to its own greatest place.
Secret: For $9250 \times 29$, round $9250 \rightarrow 9000$ (to thousands) and $29 \rightarrow 30$ (to tens) to yield $270,000$.

Trap 3: Subtraction Rules in Roman Numerals

Why is 99 written as XCIX and NOT IC?
Secret: The symbol I can only be subtracted from V and X. It can NEVER be subtracted from L, C, D, or M. To write 99, break it into $(90) + (9) = \text{XC} + \text{IX} = \text{XCIX}$.

Trap 4: The 1-Off Bound in Range Counting

How many natural numbers are between 100 and 999?
Secret: Subtracting $999 - 100 = 899$ forgets the boundary integer! The count of inclusive integers is $(B - A + 1) = 999 - 100 + 1 = \mathbf{900} = 9 \times 10^2$.

1. Number Line Geometry, Absolute Value & Directional Conventions

The set of integers $\mathbb{Z} = \{ \dots, -4, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots \}$ extends whole numbers by appending negative counterparts representing opposite directions, deficits, or reversals.

Zero: The Symmetrical Pivot

$0$ is an integer that is neither positive nor negative. It is the neutral origin: strictly greater than every negative integer and strictly smaller than every positive integer.

$\forall x \in \mathbb{Z}^+, \quad -x < 0 < x$

Absolute Value ($|x|$)

The absolute value represents geometric distance from the origin on the number line, independent of direction.

$|a| = a \text{ (if } a \ge 0), \quad |-a| = a \text{ (if } a < 0)$

Predecessor & Successor

Moving 1 step left gives the predecessor ($n - 1$). Moving 1 step right gives the successor ($n + 1$).

$\text{Pred}(-8) = -9, \quad \text{Succ}(-8) = -7$

Real-World Signed Coordinates

North ($+$) vs South ($-$), Above sea level ($+$) vs Below sea level ($-$), Profit ($+$) vs Loss ($-$), Deposit ($+$) vs Withdrawal ($-$).

$\Delta h = h_{\text{plane}} - h_{\text{sub}} = 4500 - (-1100) = 5600\text{ m}$

Number Line Ordering Principle

For any two integers on the horizontal axis: the number lying to the right is always greater than the number lying to its left. Consequently: $-12 < -5 < 0 < 3 < 15$. Notice that among negative integers, the number with the greater absolute value is actually smaller (e.g., $|-12| > |-5|$, but $-12 < -5$).

2. Addition & Subtraction Sign Dynamics, Additive Inverses & Algebraic Invariance

Integer addition and subtraction unify under the concept of the additive inverse: subtracting an integer is mathematically identical to adding its opposite.

Addition Algorithms

  • Like Signs: Add their absolute values and prefix the common sign.
    $(+15) + (+12) = +27$
    $(-18) + (-14) = -(18 + 14) = -32$
  • Unlike Signs: Find the absolute difference of their values and assign the sign of the integer with the larger absolute value.
    $(-25) + (+37) = +(37 - 25) = +12$
    $(+16) + (-42) = -(42 - 16) = -26$

Subtraction Algorithm

To subtract $b$ from $a$, invert the sign of $b$ and execute algebraic addition: $$a - b = a + (-b)$$ $$a - (-b) = a + b$$

Subtract $-63$ from $72$:
$72 - (-63) = 72 + 63 = 135$

Subtract $73$ from $-80$:
$-80 - 73 = -80 + (-73) = -153$

Algebraic Axioms of Addition & Subtraction

Property Addition ($\mathbb{Z}, +$) Subtraction ($\mathbb{Z}, -$) Proof / Concrete Counter-Example
1. Closure Closed ($a + b \in \mathbb{Z}$) Closed ($a - b \in \mathbb{Z}$) $(-7) + (-5) = -12 \in \mathbb{Z}$; $(-7) - (-5) = -2 \in \mathbb{Z}$
2. Commutativity $a + b = b + a$ $a - b \neq b - a$ $8 - 3 = 5$, but $3 - 8 = -5 \implies 5 \neq -5$
3. Associativity $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$ $(a - b) - c \neq a - (b - c)$ $(10 - 4) - 2 = 4$; $10 - (4 - 2) = 8 \implies 4 \neq 8$
4. Identity Element $a + 0 = 0 + a = a$ ($0$ is identity) Right-only ($a - 0 = a$, but $0 - a = -a$) $-15 + 0 = -15$; $0 - (-15) = +15$
5. Inverse Element $a + (-a) = 0$ (Additive Inverse) N/A Additive inverse of $-72$ is $+72$; inverse of $325$ is $-325$

3. Multiplication Sign Laws, Parity of Negative Factors & Distributive Engine

Multiplication of integers extends repeated addition to signed spaces. The signs follow systematic parity rules based on the count of negative multipliers.

Like Signs $\implies (+)$

Product of two positives or two negatives is always positive.

$(+4) \times (+6) = +24$
$(-5) \times (-4) = +20$

Unlike Signs $\implies (-)$

Product of one positive and one negative is always negative.

$(+4) \times (-9) = -36$
$(-8) \times (+7) = -56$

Parity of Negative Factors

Odd number of negatives $\to (-)$; Even number of negatives $\to (+)$.

$(-1)^{2k} = +1$
$(-1)^{2k+1} = -1$

Multiplicative Identity & Zero

$1$ is multiplicative identity; $0$ annihilates any product.

$a \times 1 = a$
$a \times 0 = 0$

The Distributive Property of Multiplication over Addition & Subtraction

For any three integers $a, b, c$: $$a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$$ $$a \times (b - c) = (a \times b) - (a \times c)$$ This master property allows high-speed mental factoring and decomposition:

Expansion Technique:
$49 \times 17 = (50 - 1) \times 17$
$= (50 \times 17) - (1 \times 17)$
$= 850 - 17 = 833$
Common Factor Extraction:
$625 \times (-35) + (-625) \times 65$
$= 625 \times [(-35) + (-65)]$
$= 625 \times (-100) = -62500$

4. Division Theorems, Zero Singularity & BODMAS Nested Bracket Hierarchy

Division is the inverse operation of multiplication: $a \div b = c \iff b \times c = a$.

Sign Quotient Rule

$(+) \div (+) = (+)$
$(-) \div (-) = (+)$
$(+) \div (-) = (-)$
$(-) \div (+) = (-)$

The Zero Singularity

$0 \div a = 0$ (for $a \neq 0$)
$a \div 0$ is NOT DEFINED.
Division by zero has no consistent quotient in real algebra.

Non-Closure of Division

Integers are not closed under division. $7 \div 2 = 3.5 \notin \mathbb{Z}$. Also, division is neither commutative nor associative.

Division by Identity

$a \div 1 = a$
$a \div (-1) = -a$
$1 \div a \neq a$ (unless $a = 1$).

Hierarchy of Bracket Removal (Innermost to Outermost)

1. $\overline{a - b}$
Vinculum / Bar

Highest precedence. Evaluate term under bar first.

2. $( \quad )$
Parentheses / Round

Second precedence. Inner circular brackets.

3. $\{ \quad \}$
Braces / Curly

Third precedence. Intermediate grouping braces.

4. $[ \quad ]$
Brackets / Square

Outermost grouping. Resolve last before full expression.

Standard Operator Evaluation Order: Brackets $\to$ Of ($X \text{ of } Y = X \times Y$) $\to$ Division $\to$ Multiplication $\to$ Addition $\to$ Subtraction.

Example 1: Evaluation of Unlike Sign Additions

Evaluate: (i) $13 + (-7)$    (ii) $(-17) + 6$    (iii) $(-7) + (-13)$

Solution:
(i) $13 + (-7) = 13 - 7 = 6$
(ii) $(-17) + 6 = -(17 - 6) = -11$
(iii) $(-7) + (-13) = -(7 + 13) = -20$

Example 2: Verification of Distributive Law

Verify that $17 \times [7 + (-3)] = (17 \times 7) + [17 \times (-3)]$.

Solution:
$\text{L.H.S.} = 17 \times [7 + (-3)] = 17 \times 4 = 68$
$\text{R.H.S.} = (17 \times 7) + [17 \times (-3)] = 119 + (-51) = 68$
$\because \text{L.H.S.} = \text{R.H.S.} = 68$, hence verified.

Example 3: Clever Grouping of Multiplication Factors

Evaluate using suitable properties: $15 \times (-25) \times (-4) \times (-10)$.

Solution:
Group factors yielding base-10 multiples:
$= [15 \times (-10)] \times [(-25) \times (-4)]$
$= (-150) \times (+100) = -15000$.

Example 4: Distributive Multiplicative Decomposition

Using distributive property, calculate: (i) $49 \times 17$    (ii) $(-26) \times (-41)$    (iii) $(-39) \times (-15) + (-1) \times 39$.

Solution:
(i) $49 \times 17 = (50 - 1) \times 17 = (50 \times 17) - (1 \times 17) = 850 - 17 = 833$
(ii) $(-26) \times (-41) = 26 \times 41 = 26 \times (40 + 1) = (26 \times 40) + (26 \times 1) = 1040 + 26 = 1066$
(iii) Rewrite second term: $(-1) \times 39 = 1 \times (-39)$.
Expression $= (-39) \times [(-15) + 1] = (-39) \times (-14) = 39 \times 14 = 39 \times (10 + 4) = 390 + 156 = 546$.

Example 5: Integer Division with Signs

Find the quotient: (i) $1078 \div (-11)$    (ii) $(-12096) \div (-21)$

Solution:
(i) $(+) \div (-) = (-) \implies -\left(\frac{1078}{11}\right) = -98$
(ii) $(-) \div (-) = (+) \implies +\left(\frac{12096}{21}\right) = 576$

Example 6: Multi-Bracket Sequential Simplification

Simplify: $[(-24) \div (-6)] + 5$ and $(-42) \div [(-8) + (-6)]$.

Solution:
1. Evaluate inner bracket first: $[(-24) \div (-6)] = 4$. Then $4 + 5 = 9$.
2. Evaluate divisor bracket first: $[(-8) + (-6)] = -14$. Then $(-42) \div (-14) = +3$.

Example 7: Unknown Integer from Division

What should be divided by $-8$ to get $36$?

Solution:
Let the integer be $x$.
$\frac{x}{-8} = 36 \implies x = 36 \times (-8) = -288$.
Hence, $-288$ should be divided by $-8$ to yield $36$.

The product of two integers is $-182$. If one of the integers is $13$, find the other integer.

Solution:

Solution:
Apples remaining for distribution $= 25 - 13 = 12$.

6 Real-World Exemplar Applications of Integers

1. Mine Shaft Elevator Descent

An elevator descends into a mine shaft at the rate of $6\text{ m/min}$. If the descent starts from $+20\text{ m}$ above ground level, how long will it take to reach $-340\text{ m}$?

Total displacement $= -340 - (+20) = -360\text{ m}$
Rate $= -6\text{ m/min}$
$\text{Time} = \frac{-360}{-6} = 60\text{ minutes} = 1\text{ hour}$.

2. Competitive Test Marking Scheme

Class test of $10$ questions: $+4$ for correct, $-2$ for incorrect. Naresh attempts all, gets $8$ correct. Umesh attempts all, gets $6$ correct.

Naresh: $8(4) + 2(-2) = 32 - 4 = 28\text{ marks}$
Umesh: $6(4) + 4(-2) = 24 - 8 = 16\text{ marks}$.

3. Bank Account Transaction Ledger

Rajan had ₹6000 balance. A week later, he deposited ₹1500. Next day, he withdrew $\frac{1}{3}\text{rd}$ of his total balance. Find final balance.

Balance before withdrawal $= 6000 + 1500 = \text{₹}7500$
Withdrawal $= \frac{1}{3} \times 7500 = \text{₹}2500$
Final Balance $= 7500 - 2500 = \text{₹}5000$.

4. Shunting Engine Vector Displacement

Engine starts at station ($0$), moves $1000\text{ m}$ North, reverses $1250\text{ m}$ South, moves $600\text{ m}$ North, then $800\text{ m}$ South.

North $= (+)$, South $= (-)$
Net pos $= +1000 - 1250 + 600 - 800$
$= (1600) - (2050) = -450\text{ m}$
$\implies 450\text{ m South of station}$.

5. Airplane & Submarine Altitude Delta

Plane flies at $+4500\text{ m}$ above sea level directly above a submarine floating at $-1100\text{ m}$ below sea level.

Vertical Distance $= h_1 - h_2$
$= 4500 - (-1100) = 4500 + 1100 = 5600\text{ m}$.

6. Net Profit/Loss Unit Accounting

Profit of ₹1 ($+100\text{ p}$) per pen, loss of $30\text{ p}$ ($-30\text{ p}$) per pencil. In a month, net loss is ₹5 ($-500\text{ p}$) and he sold $40$ pens. How many pencils sold?

Pen profit $= 40 \times 100\text{ p} = +4000\text{ p}$
Total profit/loss $= +4000 - 30x = -500\text{ p}$

A shopkeeper earns ₹1 profit per pen, incurs 30 paise loss per pencil. In a month, he incurs a total loss of ₹5 and sold 40 pens. How many pencils were sold?

Convert to paise: ₹1 = $100\text{ p}$; ₹5 = $500\text{ p}$.
Profit on 40 pens $= 40 \times 100 = 4000\text{ paise}$.
Net Loss $= -500\text{ paise}$.

$\text{Time} = \frac{-360}{-6} = 60\text{ minutes} = \mathbf{1\text{ hour}}$.

HOTS 1: Reciprocal Multiplicative Integer Equation

Find an integer which when multiplied by $-1$ and divided by $-9$ results into the reciprocal of that integer.

Algebraic Formulation:
Let the required integer be $x$ ($x \neq 0$).
According to the given condition: $$\frac{x \times (-1)}{-9} = \frac{1}{x}$$ $$\frac{-x}{-9} = \frac{1}{x} \implies \frac{x}{9} = \frac{1}{x}$$ Cross-multiplying both sides: $$x^2 = 9 \implies x = \pm \sqrt{9} = \pm 3$$ Since the problem specifies natural integral foundation: $\mathbf{x = 3}$.
Check: $3 \times (-1) = -3$; $(-3) \div (-9) = \frac{-3}{-9} = \frac{1}{3}$ (which is the reciprocal of $3$).

HOTS 2: Shunting Engine 4-Leg Vector Trajectory

A railway engine starts shunting wagons by first going $1000\text{ m}$ north of railway station. It reverses and travels $1250\text{ m}$ south, then moves $600\text{ m}$ north. Finally moving $800\text{ m}$ south, it comes to rest. How far is the engine from the station and in which direction?

Signed Vector Addition:
Let North be $+x$ and South be $-x$.
Station position $= 0\text{ m}$.
$\text{Displacement} = (+1000) + (-1250) + (+600) + (-800)$
$= (1000 + 600) - (1250 + 800) = 1600 - 2050 = -450\text{ m}$.
Since displacement is negative, the engine is $\mathbf{450\text{ m South of the railway station}}$.
