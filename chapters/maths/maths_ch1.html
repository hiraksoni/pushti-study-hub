The Indian System of Numeration

In the Indian system, digits are grouped into periods starting from the right:

  • Ones Period (3 digits): Ones ($10^0$), Tens ($10^1$), Hundreds ($10^2$).
  • Thousands Period (2 digits): Thousands ($10^3$), Ten Thousands ($10^4$).
  • Lakhs Period (2 digits): Lakhs ($10^5$), Ten Lakhs ($10^6$).
  • Crores Period (2 digits): Crores ($10^7$), Ten Crores ($10^8$).
  • Arabs Period (2 digits): Arabs ($10^9$), Ten Arabs ($10^{10}$).
Comma Rule: 3 digits from right, then every 2 digits → 1,54,36,59,801

The International System of Numeration

Used globally and in scientific literature. Every single period consists of exactly 3 digits:

  • Ones Period (3 digits): Ones ($10^0$), Tens ($10^1$), Hundreds ($10^2$).
  • Thousands Period (3 digits): Thousands ($10^3$), Ten Thousands ($10^4$), Hundred Thousands ($10^5$).
  • Millions Period (3 digits): Millions ($10^6$), Ten Millions ($10^7$), Hundred Millions ($10^8$).
  • Billions Period (3 digits): Billions ($10^9$), Ten Billions ($10^{10}$), Hundred Billions ($10^{11}$).
Comma Rule: Every 3 digits from right → 1,543,659,801

Face Value of a Digit

The Face Value of a digit is the numerical digit itself, regardless of where it appears in the number. In $5,72,84,310$, the face value of $7$ is simply $\mathbf{7}$.

Place Value of a Digit

$\text{Place Value} = \text{Face Value} \times \text{Place Weight (Power of 10)}$. In $5,72,84,310$, digit $7$ is at Ten Lakhs place ($10^6$). Hence: $\text{Place Value} = 7 \times 10,00,000 = \mathbf{70,00,000}$.

TOPIC 02 • COMPARISON & PERMUTATION

Comparing Numbers, Ascending/Descending Order & Digit Permutations

Deterministic comparison algorithms, forming greatest/smallest numbers under positional constraints.

Illustrations 6–9

Rule 1: Count the Number of Digits

If two numbers have different numbers of digits, the number with more digits is strictly greater.
E.g. $56,23,41,720$ (9 digits) $>$ $4,62,34,120$ (8 digits). No positional scanning is needed!

Rule 2: Left-to-Right Positional Comparison

If both numbers have the same number of digits, compare digits starting from the highest place (leftmost). The number with the greater digit at the first point of difference is greater.
E.g. $3,9\mathbf{8},12,465$ vs $3,9\mathbf{6},84,521 \implies 8 > 6 \implies 3,98,12,465 > 3,96,84,521$.

Order Definitions

• Ascending Order: Arrangement of numbers from the smallest to the largest: $n_1 < n_2 < n_3 < n_4$.
• Descending Order: Arrangement of numbers from the largest to the smallest: $n_1 > n_2 > n_3 > n_4$.

TOPIC 03 • ESTIMATION HEURISTICS

Exact vs. Approximate Values & Nearest Neighbours Rounding Off

When to round up vs. round down in real life, nearest neighbours table, and rounding algorithms.

Illustration 10 • Real Scenarios

Exact Values

An exact value is completely accurate, counted, and not estimated or rounded in any way.
Example: There are exactly $52$ students seated in the classroom, or a bicycle has exactly $2$ wheels.

Approximate Values & Real-Life Heuristics

An approximate value is a number that is close to the actual number, obtained by rounding up or rounding down.
• Rounding UP in real life: In a party, there are $18$ guests and chairs come in sets of $10$. You round up to $20$ chairs so everyone can sit.
• Rounding DOWN in real life: You have $\text{₹}58$ in pocket and each toy costs $\text{₹}5$. You round down to $\text{₹}55$ to buy $11$ toys.

The Nearest Neighbours Rounding Rules

To round a number to a target place (tens, hundreds, thousands, lakhs, crores):
1. Inspect the digit immediately to the right of the target place.
2. If that digit is $0, 1, 2, 3, \text{ or } 4$, ROUND DOWN (leave the target digit unchanged).
3. If that digit is $5, 6, 7, 8, \text{ or } 9$, ROUND UP (add $1$ to the target digit).
4. Replace all digits to the right of the target place with zeroes.

Estimating Sums, Differences & Products: The General Rule

Estimating answers quickly by rounding each number to its highest place value.

The General Rule of Estimation

To estimate the result of any arithmetic operation quickly without doing cumbersome paper-and-pencil computation:

The General Rule: Round off each number involved to its greatest (highest) place value, and then perform the required operation.

Find the estimated sum and difference of $7,89,13,245$ and $2,13,45,987$.

Round off the given numbers to their highest place value (nearest crore):
• In $7,89,13,245$, the ten-lakhs digit is $8 \ge 5 \implies$ rounds UP to $\mathbf{8,00,00,000}$.

Product & Quotient Estimation • General Rule Textbook Worked Examples

(i) Estimate the product: $6,784 \times 295$      (ii) Estimate the quotient: $8,745 \div 34$

(i) Product $6,784 \times 295$:
$6,784$ rounded to its highest place (Thousands) $\to 7,000$.
$295$ rounded to its highest place (Hundreds) $\to 300$.
$$\text{Estimated Product} = 7,000 \times 300 = \mathbf{21,00,000} \quad (2.1\text{ Million})$$
(ii) Quotient $8,745 \div 34$:
$8,745$ rounded to its highest place $\to 9,000$. Divisor $34$ rounded to nearest ten $\to 30$.
$$\text{Estimated Quotient} = 9,000 \div 30 = \mathbf{300}$$

Overestimation vs. Underestimation Traps

  • If both numbers are rounded up, the estimated sum/product will be strictly greater than the exact answer (Overestimation).
  • If both numbers are rounded down, the estimated sum/product will be strictly smaller than the exact answer (Underestimation).
  • If one rounds up and one rounds down, their rounding errors partially offset each other, yielding a closer estimation!

The Fundamental Product Digit Count Theorem (Illustrations 15 & 16)

When multiplying an $m$-digit number by an $n$-digit number, the number of digits in the product is ALWAYS either $(m + n - 1)$ or $(m + n)$ digits.

Proof / Bounds Demonstration:
• Smallest Product: Smallest $m$-digit number is $10^{m-1}$; smallest $n$-digit number is $10^{n-1}$.
$$\text{Minimum Product} = 10^{m-1} \times 10^{n-1} = 10^{(m+n-2)} \implies \mathbf{(m + n - 1) \text{ digits}}$$ • Greatest Product: Largest $m$-digit number is $< 10^m$; largest $n$-digit number is $< 10^n$.
$$\text{Maximum Product} < 10^m \times 10^n = 10^{(m+n)} \implies \text{has at most } \mathbf{(m + n) \text{ digits}}$$

Roman Numerals Master Rules

$\text{I}=1, \text{V}=5, \text{X}=10, \text{L}=50, \text{C}=100, \text{D}=500, \text{M}=1000$.

  • $\text{I, X, C, M}$ can repeat up to 3 times ($3 = \text{III}, 30 = \text{XXX}, 300 = \text{CCC}$).
  • $\text{V, L, D}$ are never repeated, never subtracted.
  • $\text{I}$ can be subtracted from $\text{V}$ and $\text{X}$ only ($\text{IV}=4, \text{IX}=9$).
  • $\text{X}$ can be subtracted from $\text{L}$ and $\text{C}$ only ($\text{XL}=40, \text{XC}=90$).
  • $\text{C}$ can be subtracted from $\text{D}$ and $\text{M}$ only ($\text{CD}=400, \text{CM}=900$).

SECTION 06 • LOGICAL JUSTIFICATIONS

Section 6: True or False Concepts

10 Foundational assertions with rigorous mathematical explanations.

10 Statements

1. The successor of a two-digit number is always a two-digit number.

FALSE: The successor of $99$ is $100$, which is a three-digit number!

2. 1 Million is equal to 100 Lakhs.

FALSE: 1 Million is equal to 10 Lakhs. 100 Lakhs is equal to 1 Crore.

3. The product of an $m$-digit number and an $n$-digit number can have at most $(m+n)$ digits.

TRUE: As proven by the product digit count theorem, the upper bound is strictly $< 10^{m+n}$, so it has at most $(m+n)$ digits.

4. In the Roman numeral system, the symbol V is never subtracted.

TRUE: V (5), L (50), and D (500) are never written to the left of a greater numeral (never subtracted).

5. Commas in the International system are placed after every two digits from the right.

FALSE: In the International system, commas are placed after every three digits from the right.

6. $450$ rounded off to the nearest hundred is $500$.

TRUE: The tens digit is $5 \ge 5$, so we round up to $500$.

7. The face value of a digit changes depending on its position in a numeral.

FALSE: The place value changes, but the face value is the intrinsic digit itself and remains completely constant.

8. $1\text{ Billion} = 100\text{ Crores}$.

TRUE: $10^9 \div 10^7 = 100$, so 1 Billion $= 100$ Crores ($1$ Arab).

9. The smallest 4-digit number is the successor of the greatest 3-digit number.

TRUE: $999 + 1 = 1000$.

10. In Roman numerals, the symbol X can be repeated up to four times.

FALSE: No Roman symbol can be repeated more than three times consecutively (40 is XL, not XXXX).

Self-Practice Mode: Solve in your notebook before revealing the solution.
General Theorem & Proof:

In base-10, any $n$-digit number ranges from the smallest $n$-digit number ($10^{n-1}$) to the greatest $n$-digit number ($10^n - 1$).
The total count $T_n$ of numbers in an inclusive integer range $[A, B]$ is given by: $$T_n = \text{Greatest } n\text{-digit} - \text{Smallest } n\text{-digit} + 1$$ $$T_n = (10^n - 1) - 10^{n-1} + 1 = 10^n - 10^{n-1} = 10^{n-1}(10 - 1) = \mathbf{9 \times 10^{n-1}}$$

HOTS 2: Product Digit Range Theorem Proof

Problem: Prove rigorously that the product of an $m$-digit number and an $n$-digit number can have only either $(m + n - 1)$ or $(m + n)$ digits, and cannot have any other number of digits.

Self-Practice Mode: Solve in your notebook before revealing the solution.
Mathematical Rigorous Proof:

Let $A$ be an $m$-digit integer and $B$ be an $n$-digit integer.
By definition of decimal place values: $$10^{m-1} \le A < 10^m$$ $$10^{n-1} \le B < 10^n$$ Multiplying both strict inequalities: $$10^{m-1} \times 10^{n-1} \le A \times B < 10^m \times 10^n$$ Applying the law of exponents $10^a \times 10^b = 10^{a+b}$: $$10^{(m+n-2)} \le A \times B < 10^{(m+n)}$$ Since $A \times B$ is an integer strictly less than $10^{m+n}$, the maximum possible value is $10^{m+n} - 1$, which has exactly $(m + n)$ digits.
Also, $A \times B \ge 10^{(m+n-2)}$, but more precisely, the smallest possible product is: $$\text{Min}(A) \times \text{Min}(B) = 10^{m-1} \times 10^{n-1} = 10^{m+n-2}$$ A power of $10$, namely $10^k$, has $(k + 1)$ digits. Therefore, $10^{m+n-2}$ has $(m + n - 2 + 1) = \mathbf{(m + n - 1)}$ digits.
Since the product is bounded between an $(m+n-1)$-digit minimum and an $(m+n)$-digit maximum, the number of digits in $A \times B$ is strictly either $(m + n - 1)$ or $(m + n)$. $\quad \blacksquare$
