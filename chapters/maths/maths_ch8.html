1.2 Master Vocabulary, Etymology & Sanskrit Roots

Deep mathematical clarity begins with knowing the exact meanings and origins of terms:

Fraction भिन्न (Bhinna)

Etymology: Latin fractio (broken, from frangere to break); Sanskrit root Bhid (to break, split into parts).
Meaning: A number that represents an equal part or proportion of a whole unit or a collection.

Numerator अंश (Aṁśa)

Etymology: Latin numerator (counter, one who numbers); Sanskrit Aṁśa (part, share, portion).
Meaning: The top number in $\frac{a}{b}$. It counts how many equal parts of the unit are being considered.

Denominator हर (Hara) / छेद (Cheda)

Etymology: Latin denominator (namer, classifier); Sanskrit Hara (divisor) or Cheda (the cutter).
Meaning: The bottom number in $\frac{a}{b}$. It names the size of each part by specifying how many equal parts the whole unit is cut into ($b \neq 0$).

Proper Fraction उचित भिन्न (Ucitha Bhinna)

Definition: A fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ where numerator is strictly smaller than denominator ($a < b$). Its numerical value is strictly between $0$ and $1$ ($0 < \frac{a}{b} < 1$). E.g., $\frac{2}{3}, \frac{5}{8}$.

Improper Fraction अनुचित भिन्न (Anucitha Bhinna)

Definition: A fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ where numerator is greater than or equal to denominator ($a \ge b$). Its numerical value is always $\ge 1$. Represents more than one whole unit. E.g., $\frac{7}{4}, \frac{11}{5}$.

Reciprocal व्युत्क्रम (Vyutkrama)

Etymology: Latin reciprocus (returning back, alternating); Sanskrit Vyutkrama (reversed order, inverted).
Definition: The multiplicative inverse. The reciprocal of $\frac{a}{b}$ is $\frac{b}{a}$. Their product is always $1$: $\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{b}{a} = 1$.

Simplest Form अपवर्तन (Apavartana)

Definition: A fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ is in lowest / simplest form when $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ (coprime). The operation of dividing both terms by their common factor was named Apavartana by ancient Indian mathematicians.

Fraction Division भागहार (Bhāgahāra)

Definition: Dividing by a fraction is identical to multiplying by its reciprocal. Formulated in Sanskrit by Brahmagupta in 628 CE: "Invert the numerator and denominator of the divisor, then multiply."

1.3 Anatomy & Classification Matrix of Fractions

Proper Fractions ($a < b$)

• Represents a part of a single whole unit.
• Value is strictly less than 1 ($0 < f < 1$).
• Examples: $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{4}, \frac{7}{10}$.
• Always located to the left of 1 on the number line.

Improper Fractions ($a \ge b$)

• Represents one or more complete wholes plus parts.
• Value is greater than or equal to 1 ($f \ge 1$).
• Examples: $\frac{5}{3}, \frac{9}{4}, \frac{4}{4} = 1$.
• Can always be converted into a Mixed Fraction.

Mixed Fractions ($W + \frac{a}{b}$)

• Explicit combination of a whole integer and proper fraction.
• $W\frac{a}{b} = W + \frac{a}{b} = \frac{W \times b + a}{b}$.
• Example: $2\frac{3}{4} = 2 + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{11}{4}$.
• Provides clear physical intuition for measurements!

2.2 Brahmagupta's Product Rule (628 CE)

In his landmark treatise Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE), the great Indian mathematician Brahmagupta codified the universal law for the multiplication of two fractions:

Brahmagupta's Theorem:

$$\text{Product of two fractions} = \frac{\text{Product of their Numerators}}{\text{Product of their Denominators}}$$ $$\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d} = \frac{ac}{bd}$$
Textbook Example (p. 190)

Example A: Calculate $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{4}{7}$

$$\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{4}{7} = \frac{1 \times 4}{2 \times 7} = \frac{4}{14} = \frac{2}{7}$$

Textbook Example (p. 190)

Example B: Calculate $\frac{5}{9} \times \frac{1}{3}$

$$\frac{5}{9} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{5 \times 1}{9 \times 3} = \frac{5}{27}$$

3.1 Apavartana: Simplification to Lowest Form (Textbook p. 192)

In ancient India, reducing fractions by cancelling common factors was designated Apavartana (अपवर्तन), which literally translates to "simplifying by shedding excess to reach the true essence".

Philosophical Context (Around 150 CE): The celebrated Jaina philosopher Umāsvāti incorporated Apavartana into his philosophical treatises (such as the Tattvārtha Sūtra commentary) as an analogy for spiritual purification: just as a fraction reaches its purest, irreducible clarity by dividing out common factors, the human soul attains clarity by removing karmic clutter!

Cross-Cancellation Example

Calculate $\frac{3}{7} \times \frac{2}{15}$ using Apavartana:

Notice the common factor $3$ between numerator $3$ and denominator $15$ ($15 = 3 \times 5$):

$$\frac{\cancel{3}^1}{7} \times \frac{2}{\cancel{15}_5} = \frac{1 \times 2}{7 \times 5} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{35}}$$

Simplifying before multiplying prevents unwieldy intermediate numbers!

3.2 Bhāskarāchārya's Līlāvatī: The Miser's Cowrie Dilemma (1150 CE)

In his 1150 CE masterpiece Līlāvatī, the great astronomer-mathematician Bhāskarāchārya (Bhāskara II) included a celebrated humorous verse illustrating chained fraction multiplication:

The Ancient Verse Problem (Textbook p. 197):

A miserly rich man encountered a beggar. Pretending to bestow a grand charitable gift, he solemnly announced that he was giving him:

$$\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{5} \times \frac{1}{16} \times \frac{1}{4} \text{ of a Dramma (silver coin)}$$

Evaluate the expression: what did the beggar actually receive?

Step-by-Step Telescoping Cancellation

Observe the cancellations across numerators and denominators:

$$\left(\frac{1}{\cancel{2}} \times \frac{\cancel{2}}{\cancel{3}} \times \frac{\cancel{3}}{4}\right) \times \frac{1}{5} \times \frac{1}{16} \times \frac{1}{4}$$ $$= \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{5} \times \frac{1}{16} \times \frac{1}{4}$$ $$= \frac{1}{20 \times 64} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{1280} \text{ Dramma}}$$

Ancient Indian Monetary System Breakdown:

  • $1\text{ Gold Dinar} = 16\text{ Silver Drammas}$
  • $1\text{ Silver Dramma} = 16\text{ Copper Panas}$
  • $1\text{ Copper Pana} = 80\text{ Cowrie Shells}$ (कपर्दिका / Kavadis)
  • Therefore: $1\text{ Dramma} = 16 \times 80 = \mathbf{1280\text{ Cowrie Shells}}$!

Conclusion: The miser made a dramatic show of charity, but gave the beggar exactly 1 single cowrie shell!

4.1 Three Cases of Product Magnitude: Why Multiplication Doesn't Always Increase!

The Great Primary-School Trap: "Multiplication Always Makes Things Bigger"

When young students learn multiplication with whole numbers ($3 \times 4 = 12 > 4$), they mistakenly form the rigid belief that multiplying always yields a larger product.
In fractions, this belief is FALSE! Multiplying by a number less than 1 scales the quantity DOWN.

Consider the product of two positive fractions $f_1 \times f_2 = P$. The size of $P$ compared to $f_1$ and $f_2$ depends entirely on whether the factors are proper or improper:

Case I: Both Proper ($< 1$)

When both fractions are proper ($f_1 < 1$ and $f_2 < 1$):
$$\mathbf{P < f_1 \quad \text{and} \quad P < f_2}$$ The product is strictly smaller than both individual numbers!
Example: $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$.
Notice: $\frac{1}{6} < \frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{1}{6} < \frac{1}{3}$. Taking half of a third shrinks it!

Case II: One Proper, One Improper

When one fraction is proper ($< 1$) and one is improper ($> 1$):
$$\mathbf{f_{\text{proper}} < P < f_{\text{improper}}}$$ The product is greater than the proper fraction, but smaller than the improper fraction!
Example: $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{5}{2} = \frac{10}{6} = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67$.
Notice: $\frac{2}{3} (0.67) < 1.67 < \frac{5}{2} (2.5)$.

Case III: Both Improper ($> 1$)

When both fractions are improper ($f_1 > 1$ and $f_2 > 1$):
$$\mathbf{P > f_1 \quad \text{and} \quad P > f_2}$$ The product is strictly greater than both individual numbers!
Example: $\frac{3}{2} (1.5) \times \frac{4}{3} (1.33) = 2$.
Notice: $2 > 1.5$ and $2 > 1.33$.

4.2 Debunking Riya's Misconception (Textbook Illustration 6, p. 194)

The Misconception Stated:

Riya says: "When I multiply two fractions, I always get a smaller number."

Is Riya correct? Explain your answer with concrete mathematical examples for each case.

Rigorous Textbook Evaluation

Soln: Riya is NOT always correct. Her statement is true only in specific conditions. Let us examine all three possibilities:

1. Where Riya is Correct (Case II):
When both fractions are proper (lying between $0$ and $1$).
$$\frac{1}{4} \times \frac{2}{5} = \frac{2}{20} = \frac{1}{10}$$ Here, $\frac{1}{10} < \frac{1}{4}$ and $\frac{1}{10} < \frac{2}{5}$. The product is indeed smaller than both factors.

2. Where Riya is Completely Wrong (Case I):
When both fractions are improper (greater than $1$).
$$\frac{5}{4} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{15}{8}$$ Notice $\frac{15}{8} = 1\frac{7}{8} = 1.875$, while $\frac{5}{4} = 1.25$ and $\frac{3}{2} = 1.5$.
$\frac{15}{8}$ is strictly larger than both factors! Riya's claim fails completely here.

3. Where Riya is Only Partly Correct (Case III):
When one fraction is less than $1$ and the other is greater than $1$.
$$\frac{1}{4} \times 4 = 1$$ The product $1$ is larger than $\frac{1}{4}$, but smaller than $4$. It is smaller than one factor, but larger than the other!

4.3 Commutative & Associative Laws for Fractions

Fraction multiplication strictly obeys the foundational laws of algebra:

Commutative Property:

$$\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c}{d} \times \frac{a}{b}$$

The order of multiplying two fractions does not alter the product.
$$\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{5}{7} = \frac{10}{21} = \frac{5}{7} \times \frac{2}{3}$$

Associative Property:

$$\left(\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}\right) \times \frac{e}{f} = \frac{a}{b} \times \left(\frac{c}{d} \times \frac{e}{f}\right)$$

When multiplying three or more fractions, grouping does not affect the final result.

4.4 Reciprocals of Fractions & The Zero Trap (Textbook p. 194)

Two non-zero numbers are called reciprocals (or multiplicative inverses) of each other if their product is strictly equal to $1$.

$$\text{If } \frac{a}{b} \text{ is a non-zero fraction, its reciprocal is } \frac{b}{a}$$ $$\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{b}{a} = \frac{ab}{ba} = 1$$
Given Number / Fraction Fraction Form Its Reciprocal Verification Product
$\frac{3}{2}$ (Improper) $\frac{3}{2}$ $\mathbf{\frac{2}{3}}$ (Proper) $\frac{3}{2} \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{6}{6} = 1$
$\frac{5}{9}$ (Proper) $\frac{5}{9}$ $\mathbf{\frac{9}{5} = 1\frac{4}{5}}$ (Improper) $\frac{5}{9} \times \frac{9}{5} = 1$

The Zero Reciprocal Trap:

Does the number $0$ have a reciprocal? NO! Any number multiplied by $0$ is $0$, never $1$ ($0 \times x = 0 \ne 1$). Therefore, division by $0$ is mathematically undefined, and zero has no reciprocal!

5.1 Why Do We Invert and Multiply? (The Deep Mathematical Proof)

Understanding Division as Measurement Grouping

In whole numbers, $12 \div 3 = 4$ means: "How many groups of 3 fit inside 12?"
The exact same logic applies to fractions!
• What does $3 \div \frac{1}{2}$ mean? It asks: "How many half-units fit inside 3 whole units?"
• In 1 whole unit, there are 2 halves.
• Therefore, in 3 whole units, there are $3 \times 2 = \mathbf{6}$ halves!
• Notice: $3 \div \frac{1}{2} = 3 \times 2 = 6$. Dividing by $\frac{1}{2}$ doubles the answer!

The Rigorous Algebraic Proof (Eliminating the Denominator)

Let us divide any fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ by another fraction $\frac{c}{d}$ (where $b, c, d \neq 0$):

Step Mathematical Expression Justification
1. Express as Compound Fraction $$\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{\frac{a}{b}}{\frac{c}{d}}$$ Definition of division as a fraction of fractions.
2. Scale by Multiplicative Inverse $$= \frac{\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c}}{\frac{c}{d} \times \frac{d}{c}}$$ Multiply numerator and denominator by $\frac{d}{c}$ (multiplying by $\frac{k}{k} = 1$ leaves value unchanged).
3. Simplify the Denominator $$\frac{c}{d} \times \frac{d}{c} = 1$$ Any fraction multiplied by its reciprocal equals $1$.
4. Final Result $$= \frac{\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c}}{1} = \mathbf{\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c}}$$ Dividing by 1 leaves the numerator. Q.E.D.
Brahmagupta's Rule (628 CE):

"By reversing the numerator and denominator of the divisor, the operation becomes multiplication."
$$\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times c}$$

5.2 Division of a Whole Number by a Fraction

Dividing a whole number by a fraction answers: "How many fractional pieces of size $\frac{a}{b}$ can be cut out from $W$ wholes?"

Textbook Illustration 7 (p. 195)

Problem: Solve $15 \div 3\frac{1}{2}$

Step 1: Convert mixed fraction to improper: $3\frac{1}{2} = \frac{(3 \times 2) + 1}{2} = \frac{7}{2}$.

Step 2: Invert divisor and multiply: $$15 \div \frac{7}{2} = 15 \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{30}{7} = \mathbf{4\frac{2}{7}}$$

Textbook Illustration 10 (p. 195) — Rope Cutting Model

Problem: Jay cuts a $20\text{ m}$ long rope into smaller pieces of length $3\frac{1}{3}\text{ m}$ each. How many pieces of rope did he get?

Total rope length $= 20\text{ m}$. Length of one piece $= 3\frac{1}{3}\text{ m} = \frac{10}{3}\text{ m}$.

$$\text{Number of pieces} = 20 \div \frac{10}{3} = \cancel{20}^2 \times \frac{3}{\cancel{10}_1} = 2 \times 3 = \mathbf{6\text{ pieces}}$$

Jay gets exactly 6 full pieces of rope!

5.3 Division of a Fraction by Another Fraction

When dividing two fractions, apply Brahmagupta's reciprocal rule directly:

Textbook Illustration 8 (p. 195)

Problem: Simplify $5\frac{1}{6} \div \frac{9}{2}$

Convert $5\frac{1}{6} = \frac{31}{6}$. Then invert divisor $\frac{9}{2} \to \frac{2}{9}$:

$$\frac{31}{6} \div \frac{9}{2} = \frac{31}{\cancel{6}_3} \times \frac{\cancel{2}^1}{9} = \frac{31 \times 1}{3 \times 9} = \frac{31}{27} = \mathbf{1\frac{4}{27}}$$

Problem: $25$ shirts of equal size are prepared from $30\frac{5}{9}\text{ m}$ of cloth. Find the length of cloth required for making one shirt.

Number of shirts $= 25$. Cloth per shirt:

$$\frac{275}{9} \div 25 = \frac{\cancel{275}^{11}}{9} \times \frac{1}{\cancel{25}_1} = \frac{11}{9}\text{ m} = \mathbf{1\frac{2}{9}\text{ m}}$$

1. Fraction Anatomy & The 8 Core Classifications

A fraction is a number of the form $\frac{a}{b}$, where $a$ and $b$ are whole numbers and $b \neq 0$. Here, $a$ is the numerator (representing the parts taken) and $b$ is the denominator (representing total equal parts into which the whole is divided).

1. Proper Fraction

Numerator strictly less than denominator ($a < b$). Value is strictly $< 1$.

$\frac{3}{5}, \quad \frac{7}{9}, \quad \frac{11}{17}$

2. Improper Fraction

Numerator greater than or equal to denominator ($a \ge b$). Value is $\ge 1$.

$\frac{5}{3}, \quad \frac{13}{7}, \quad \frac{8}{8} = 1$

3. Mixed Fraction

Combination of a non-zero whole number and a proper fraction ($q\frac{r}{d}$).

$3\frac{2}{5} = \frac{3 \times 5 + 2}{5} = \frac{17}{5}$

4. Equivalent Fractions

Fractions having identical simplest values formed by scaling numerator & denominator by $k \neq 0$.

$\frac{3}{7} = \frac{6}{14} = \frac{9}{21} = \frac{12}{28}$

5. Like Fractions

Fractions having identical denominators. Can be added/subtracted directly.

$\frac{2}{9}, \quad \frac{5}{9}, \quad \frac{8}{9}$

6. Unlike Fractions

Fractions having different denominators. Must convert via LCM before operations.

$\frac{3}{4}, \quad \frac{5}{6}, \quad \frac{7}{12}$

7. Unit Fractions

Fractions whose numerator is strictly $1$. Fundamental Egyptian fraction building blocks.

$\frac{1}{2}, \quad \frac{1}{5}, \quad \frac{1}{17}$

8. Decimal Fractions

Fractions whose denominators are powers of $10$ ($10, 100, 1000, \dots$).

$\frac{7}{10} = 0.7, \quad \frac{3}{100} = 0.03$

Reduction to Lowest Terms: The HCF Algorithm

A fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ is in lowest terms (irreducible) if and only if $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.

$$\text{To reduce } \frac{78}{338}: \quad \gcd(78, 338) = 26 \implies \frac{78 \div 26}{338 \div 26} = \mathbf{\frac{3}{13}}$$

2. Comparison Algorithms & Ordering

Method 1: The Cross-Multiplication Rule (2 Fractions)

For comparing any two fractions $\frac{a}{b}$ and $\frac{c}{d}$:

  • If $a \times d > b \times c \implies \mathbf{\frac{a}{b} > \frac{c}{d}}$
  • If $a \times d < b \times c \implies \mathbf{\frac{a}{b} < \frac{c}{d}}$
  • If $a \times d = b \times c \implies \mathbf{\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}}$
$$\text{Compare } \frac{4}{7} \text{ and } \frac{5}{9}: \quad 4 \times 9 = 36 < 7 \times 5 = 35 \implies \mathbf{\frac{4}{7} > \frac{5}{9}}$$

Method 2: LCM of Denominators (3+ Fractions)

For sorting $3$ or more fractions into ascending/descending order:

  1. Find $L = \text{LCM}(b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n)$.
  2. Multiply numerator & denominator of each fraction by $\frac{L}{b_i}$ to convert to like fractions.
  3. Order by resulting numerators.
$$\text{Order } \frac{5}{12}, \frac{3}{4}, \frac{7}{8}, \frac{13}{24}: \; \text{LCM}=24 \rightarrow \frac{10}{24}, \frac{18}{24}, \frac{21}{24}, \frac{13}{24}$$ $$\therefore \mathbf{\frac{5}{12} < \frac{13}{24} < \frac{3}{4} < \frac{7}{8}}$$

Rapid Heuristic: Same Numerator Theorem

When fractions have the same numerator, the fraction with the larger denominator is smaller:

$$\frac{7}{12} < \frac{7}{10} < \frac{7}{8} < \frac{7}{5}$$

3. Arithmetic Engines: Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication & Inverses

Add & Subtract Engine

For like fractions:

$$\frac{a}{c} \pm \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a \pm b}{c}$$

For mixed numbers, add whole parts and fractional parts separately or convert to improper fractions:

$$3\frac{1}{2} + 1\frac{5}{6} = (3 + 1) + \left(\frac{3}{6} + \frac{5}{6}\right) = 4 + \frac{8}{6} = 4 + 1\frac{1}{3} = \mathbf{5\frac{1}{3}}$$

Multiplication Engine

$$\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}$$

The "Of" Operator: Represents multiplication of a quantity by a fraction:

$$\frac{3}{4} \text{ of } 1\text{ hour} = \frac{3}{4} \times 60\text{ minutes} = \mathbf{45\text{ minutes}}$$ $$\frac{2}{5} \text{ of } \text{Rs. } 1 = \frac{2}{5} \times 100\text{ paise} = \mathbf{40\text{ paise}}$$

Multiplicative Inverse & Division

Two non-zero numbers are reciprocals if their product is $1$:

$$\text{Reciprocal of } \frac{a}{b} = \frac{b}{a}, \quad \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{b}{a} = 1$$

Division Protocol: Multiply dividend by reciprocal of divisor:

$$\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times c}$$

4. Product Inequality Theorems & Olympiad Speed Traps

Case I: Proper $\times$ Proper

The product of two proper fractions is strictly less than each of the two fractions:

$$\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5} = \frac{8}{15} \implies \frac{8}{15} < \frac{2}{3} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{8}{15} < \frac{4}{5}$$

Case II: Improper $\times$ Improper

The product of two improper fractions ($> 1$) is strictly greater than each of the two fractions:

$$\frac{3}{2} \times \frac{5}{4} = \frac{15}{8} \implies \frac{15}{8} > \frac{3}{2} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{15}{8} > \frac{5}{4}$$

Case III: Proper $\times$ Improper

The product lies between the two factors (greater than proper, less than improper):

$$\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{5}{2} = \frac{5}{3} \implies \frac{2}{3} < \mathbf{\frac{5}{3}} < \frac{5}{2}$$

HOTS Problem Deconstruction: The Equal Midpoint Fraction

Problem: Find a fraction which is as much greater than $\frac{5}{6}$ as it is less than $\frac{8}{9}$.

$$\text{Let fraction be } x. \quad x - \frac{5}{6} = \frac{8}{9} - x \implies 2x = \frac{5}{6} + \frac{8}{9} = \frac{15 + 16}{18} = \frac{31}{18}$$ $$x = \frac{31}{18 \times 2} = \mathbf{\frac{31}{36}}$$

Thinking Cap • Challenge 3

3. Tushar says that for any given denominator, the number of like fractions that are also proper fractions will always be one less than the value of the denominator. Is he correct? Justify your answer.

View Complete Mathematical Proof

✅ Yes, Tushar is completely correct!

Formal Mathematical Justification:

  1. Definition of Proper Fraction:
    A fraction $\frac{n}{d}$ is defined as a proper fraction if its numerator is strictly less than its denominator, with positive integer numerator ($1 \le n < d$).
  2. Definition of Like Fractions:
    Like fractions are fractions sharing the exact same denominator $d$.
  3. Counting the valid numerators:
    For a given positive integer denominator $d$ ($d \ge 2$), the possible positive whole number numerators that form a proper fraction are: $$\{1, 2, 3, \dots, d - 1\}$$ The cardinality (count) of this set is strictly: $$\mathbf{(d - 1)}$$ which is exactly one less than the value of the denominator.

Concrete Examples:
- If denominator $d = 4$, the like proper fractions are $\frac{1}{4}, \frac{2}{4}, \frac{3}{4} \implies 4 - 1 = \mathbf{3\text{ fractions}}$.
- If denominator $d = 7$, the like proper fractions are $\frac{1}{7}, \frac{2}{7}, \frac{3}{7}, \frac{4}{7}, \frac{5}{7}, \frac{6}{7} \implies 7 - 1 = \mathbf{6\text{ fractions}}$.

✅ Conclusion: Tushar's statement is mathematically sound and universally true for all natural number denominators $d \ge 2$.

True or False Conceptual Analysis

Page 9 • Questions 11 to 15
11. If there are two rational numbers with a common denominator, the one with the smaller numerator is greater than the other.
FALSE

Justification: For positive common denominators, the rational number with the greater numerator is greater. For example, between $\frac{3}{7}$ and $\frac{5}{7}$, $5 > 3$, so $\frac{5}{7} > \frac{3}{7}$. The one with the smaller numerator is smaller, not greater.

12. Every rational number is a natural number but every natural number need not be a rational number.
FALSE

Justification: The exact reverse is true! Every natural number $n$ can be written as $\frac{n}{1}$, making it a rational number ($\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Q}$). However, rational numbers like $\frac{1}{2}$ or $-5$ are NOT natural numbers.

13. There are countless rational numbers between two successive integers.
TRUE

Justification: This is the density property of rational numbers. Between any two integers $a$ and $a+1$, there exist infinitely many rational numbers (e.g. between 1 and 2: $1.1, 1.01, 1.001, \dots$).

14. Zero is the smallest rational number.
FALSE

Justification: There is no smallest rational number! All negative rational numbers (such as $-1, -100, -\frac{1}{2}$) are strictly less than $0$.

15. The rational numbers $\frac{-21}{5}$ and $\frac{7}{-31}$ are on opposite sides of $0$ on the number line.
FALSE

Justification: Both rational numbers are negative! $\frac{-21}{5} = -4.2 < 0$, and $\frac{7}{-31} = -\frac{7}{31} < 0$. Since both are negative, they both lie on the same side of $0$ (the left side).

1. Two positive improper fractions are multiplied. Is the product sometimes, always, or never less than $1$? Give examples to support your answer.
View Detailed Mathematical Proof & Analysis

Core Conclusion: The product is NEVER LESS THAN 1.

Mathematical Proof:
• By definition, a positive improper fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ has numerator greater than or equal to denominator: $a \ge b > 0$.
• Therefore, any positive improper fraction is $\ge 1$.
• Let the two fractions be $f_1 \ge 1$ and $f_2 \ge 1$.
• When multiplying two numbers that are both $\ge 1$: $$f_1 \times f_2 \ge 1 \times 1 = 1$$
• The minimum possible value of the product occurs when both fractions equal $\frac{1}{1} = 1$, yielding a product of $1$. In all other cases where at least one fraction is strictly greater than $1$, the product is strictly greater than $1$ ($> 1$).
• It is mathematically impossible for the product to be strictly less than $1$.

Examples:
• Example 1: $\frac{3}{2} \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{12}{6} = 2 > 1$.
• Example 2: $\frac{5}{4} \times \frac{7}{5} = \frac{7}{4} = 1.75 > 1$.
• Boundary Example: $\frac{1}{1} \times \frac{1}{1} = 1$, which is equal to $1$ (not less than $1$).

2. A string is cut in halves. One of the halves is kept aside. One-fifth of the remaining half is cut away and the piece left is $8\text{ feet}$ long. How long was the string initially?
View Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Method 1: Algebraic Formulation

  1. Let the initial length of the string be $L\text{ feet}$.
  2. The string is cut into two equal halves, each measuring $\frac{1}{2}L\text{ feet}$.
  3. One half is kept aside. The remaining half has length $\frac{1}{2}L$.
  4. One-fifth ($\frac{1}{5}$) of this remaining half is cut away.
    Fraction of this half that remains: $$1 - \frac{1}{5} = \frac{4}{5}$$
  5. Length of the final piece left: $$\text{Length left} = \frac{4}{5} \times \left(\frac{1}{2}L\right) = \frac{4}{10}L = \frac{2}{5}L$$
  6. We are given that this final piece is $8\text{ feet}$: $$\frac{2}{5}L = 8 \implies L = 8 \times \frac{5}{2} = \frac{40}{2} = \mathbf{20\text{ feet}}$$

Method 2: Reverse Reasoning (Work Backwards)

  • The piece left is $8\text{ ft}$, which represents $\frac{4}{5}$ of the half.
  • Therefore, full length of that half $= 8 \div \frac{4}{5} = 8 \times \frac{5}{4} = 10\text{ feet}$.
  • Since this was half of the original string, the initial total length $= 10 \times 2 = \mathbf{20\text{ feet}}$.
✅ Final Answer: The original length of the string was $\mathbf{20\text{ feet}}$.
