1.2 Anatomy & Types of Fractions

A fraction is written in the form $\frac{a}{b}$, where $a$ is the numerator (number of parts taken), $b$ is the denominator (total equal parts into which the whole is divided), and $b \ne 0$.

Fraction Type Mathematical Definition Key Characteristic Concrete Examples
Proper Fraction $\text{Numerator} < \text{Denominator}$ ($a < b$) Value is strictly less than $1$ ($0 < \frac{a}{b} < 1$) $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{4}, \frac{5}{9}, \frac{7}{12}$
Improper Fraction $\text{Numerator} \ge \text{Denominator}$ ($a \ge b$) Value is greater than or equal to $1$ ($\frac{a}{b} \ge 1$) $\frac{5}{4}, \frac{7}{3}, \frac{11}{2}, \frac{4}{4}$
Mixed Fraction $\text{Whole Number} + \text{Proper Fraction}$ Combination of an integer and a fractional part $1\frac{1}{4} = \frac{5}{4}$, $3\frac{1}{2} = \frac{7}{2}$, $5\frac{1}{6} = \frac{31}{6}$
Unit Fraction $\text{Numerator} = 1$ Fundamental building block of all fractional arithmetic $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{10}, \frac{1}{1280}$
Like vs Unlike Like: same $b$; Unlike: different $b$ Like fractions can be directly added/subtracted Like: $(\frac{2}{7}, \frac{5}{7})$; Unlike: $(\frac{2}{3}, \frac{3}{4})$
Converting Mixed to Improper (Standard Step): $$W\frac{a}{b} = \frac{(W \times b) + a}{b}$$ Example: $3\frac{1}{2} = \frac{(3 \times 2) + 1}{2} = \frac{7}{2}$. Always perform this conversion before multiplying or dividing!

2.1 Multiplication of a Fraction by a Whole Number (Textbook p. 189)

Multiplication by a positive whole number is repeated addition. When multiplying a whole number by a fraction, multiply the whole number by the numerator while leaving the denominator unchanged:

$$\text{Whole Number} \times \frac{\text{Numerator}}{\text{Denominator}} = \frac{\text{Whole Number} \times \text{Numerator}}{\text{Denominator}}$$ $$k \times \frac{a}{b} = \frac{k \times a}{b}$$
Figure 2.1 (Textbook p. 189): Repeated addition model for $5 \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{5 \times 1}{4} = \frac{5}{4} = 1\frac{1}{4}$.

2.2 Brahmagupta's Product Rule (628 CE)

In his landmark treatise Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE), the great Indian mathematician Brahmagupta codified the universal law for the multiplication of two fractions:

Brahmagupta's Theorem:

$$\text{Product of two fractions} = \frac{\text{Product of their Numerators}}{\text{Product of their Denominators}}$$ $$\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d} = \frac{ac}{bd}$$
Textbook Example (p. 190)

Example A: Calculate $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{4}{7}$

$$\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{4}{7} = \frac{1 \times 4}{2 \times 7} = \frac{4}{14} = \frac{2}{7}$$

Textbook Example (p. 190)

Example B: Calculate $\frac{5}{9} \times \frac{1}{3}$

$$\frac{5}{9} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{5 \times 1}{9 \times 3} = \frac{5}{27}$$

2.4 Fraction as an Operator "of" & Real-World Surveys

The word "of" in mathematics signifies multiplication: $\frac{a}{b} \text{ of } X = \frac{a}{b} \times X$.

Textbook Illustration 1 (p. 190)

Find $\frac{1}{3} \text{ of } 4$ and show diagrammatically:

$$\frac{1}{3} \text{ of } 4 = \frac{1}{3} \times 4 = \frac{4}{3} = 1\frac{1}{3}$$

Visual explanation: 4 separate circles, each having $\frac{1}{3}$ portion taken. Grouping 3 thirds forms $1$ full circle, leaving $\frac{1}{3}$ of the remaining circle, yielding exactly $1\frac{1}{3}$ circles!

  • (iii) Mathematics: $60 - (12 + 24) = 60 - 36 = \mathbf{24\text{ students}}$
  • (iv) Fraction liking Mathematics: $\frac{24}{60} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{5}}$

3.1 Apavartana: Simplification to Lowest Form (Textbook p. 192)

In ancient India, reducing fractions by cancelling common factors was designated Apavartana (अपवर्तन), which literally translates to "simplifying by shedding excess to reach the true essence".

Philosophical Context (Around 150 CE): The celebrated Jaina philosopher Umāsvāti incorporated Apavartana into his philosophical treatises (such as the Tattvārtha Sūtra commentary) as an analogy for spiritual purification: just as a fraction reaches its purest, irreducible clarity by dividing out common factors, the human soul attains clarity by removing karmic clutter!

Cross-Cancellation Example

Calculate $\frac{3}{7} \times \frac{2}{15}$ using Apavartana:

Notice the common factor $3$ between numerator $3$ and denominator $15$ ($15 = 3 \times 5$):

$$\frac{\cancel{3}^1}{7} \times \frac{2}{\cancel{15}_5} = \frac{1 \times 2}{7 \times 5} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{35}}$$

Simplifying before multiplying prevents unwieldy intermediate numbers!

3.2 Bhāskarāchārya's Līlāvatī: The Miser's Cowrie Dilemma (1150 CE)

In his 1150 CE masterpiece Līlāvatī, the great astronomer-mathematician Bhāskarāchārya (Bhāskara II) included a celebrated humorous verse illustrating chained fraction multiplication:

The Ancient Verse Problem (Textbook p. 197):

A miserly rich man encountered a beggar. Pretending to bestow a grand charitable gift, he solemnly announced that he was giving him:

$$\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{5} \times \frac{1}{16} \times \frac{1}{4} \text{ of a Dramma (silver coin)}$$

Evaluate the expression: what did the beggar actually receive?

Step-by-Step Telescoping Cancellation

Observe the cancellations across numerators and denominators:

$$\left(\frac{1}{\cancel{2}} \times \frac{\cancel{2}}{\cancel{3}} \times \frac{\cancel{3}}{4}\right) \times \frac{1}{5} \times \frac{1}{16} \times \frac{1}{4}$$ $$= \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{5} \times \frac{1}{16} \times \frac{1}{4}$$ $$= \frac{1}{20 \times 64} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{1280} \text{ Dramma}}$$

Ancient Indian Monetary System Breakdown:

  • $1\text{ Gold Dinar} = 16\text{ Silver Drammas}$
  • $1\text{ Silver Dramma} = 16\text{ Copper Panas}$
  • $1\text{ Copper Pana} = 80\text{ Cowrie Shells}$ (कपर्दिका / Kavadis)
  • Therefore: $1\text{ Dramma} = 16 \times 80 = \mathbf{1280\text{ Cowrie Shells}}$!

Conclusion: The miser made a dramatic show of charity, but gave the beggar exactly 1 single cowrie shell!

3.3 Sridharacharya's Pātīgaṇita (9th Century CE)

The 9th-century Indian master Śrīdharāchārya authored the Pātīgaṇita, one of the ancient world's most sophisticated treatises on arithmetic algorithms.

Textbook p. 196: Figure It Out Q4

Śrīdhara's Puzzle: "Friend, after thinking, what sum will be obtained by adding together $1+\frac{1}{6}$, $1+\frac{1}{10}$, $1+\frac{1}{13}$, $1+\frac{1}{9}$, and $1+\frac{1}{2}$?"

Mathematical Evaluation:

$$\text{Sum} = \left(1 + \frac{1}{6}\right) + \left(1 + \frac{1}{10}\right) + \left(1 + \frac{1}{13}\right) + \left(1 + \frac{1}{9}\right) + \left(1 + \frac{1}{2}\right)$$ $$= (1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1) + \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{13}\right)$$ $$= 5 + \left(\frac{3+1}{6} + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{13}\right) = 5 + \left(\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{13}\right)$$ $$= 5 + \left(\frac{7}{9} + \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{13}\right) = 5 + \left(\frac{79}{90} + \frac{1}{13}\right) = 5 + \frac{1027 + 90}{1170} = 5 + \frac{1117}{1170} = \mathbf{5\frac{1117}{1170}}$$

4.1 Three Cases of Product Magnitude (Textbook p. 193)

Does multiplication always make numbers larger? In whole numbers, $3 \times 4 = 12 > 4$. But in fractions, the relationship between the product and its factors depends entirely on whether the factors are greater than, equal to, or less than $1$!

Case Category Mathematical Condition Relationship of Product to Factors Textbook Example
Case I: Both Numbers > 1 Both factors are improper fractions or whole numbers $> 1$ Product is GREATER than both factors $\frac{5}{4} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{15}{8}$
$\frac{15}{8} > \frac{5}{4}$ and $\frac{15}{8} > \frac{3}{2}$ ($1.875 > 1.25$ and $1.5$)
Case II: Both Numbers between 0 and 1 Both factors are proper fractions ($0 < x < 1$) Product is SMALLER than both factors $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$
$\frac{1}{6} < \frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{1}{6} < \frac{1}{3}$
Case III: One < 1 and One > 1 One proper fraction ($< 1$) and one number $> 1$ Product lies STRICTLY BETWEEN the two factors $\frac{2}{5} \times 5 = 2$
$\frac{2}{5} < 2 < 5$
Core Multiplicative Principle: Multiplying by a number less than $1$ scales down; multiplying by a number greater than $1$ scales up!

4.2 Debunking Riya's Misconception (Textbook Illustration 6, p. 194)

The Misconception Stated:

Riya says: "When I multiply two fractions, I always get a smaller number."

Is Riya correct? Explain your answer with concrete mathematical examples for each case.

Rigorous Textbook Evaluation

Soln: Riya is NOT always correct. Her statement is true only in specific conditions. Let us examine all three possibilities:

1. Where Riya is Correct (Case II):
When both fractions are proper (lying between $0$ and $1$).
$$\frac{1}{4} \times \frac{2}{5} = \frac{2}{20} = \frac{1}{10}$$ Here, $\frac{1}{10} < \frac{1}{4}$ and $\frac{1}{10} < \frac{2}{5}$. The product is indeed smaller than both factors.

2. Where Riya is Completely Wrong (Case I):
When both fractions are improper (greater than $1$).
$$\frac{5}{4} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{15}{8}$$ Notice $\frac{15}{8} = 1\frac{7}{8} = 1.875$, while $\frac{5}{4} = 1.25$ and $\frac{3}{2} = 1.5$.
$\frac{15}{8}$ is strictly larger than both factors! Riya's claim fails completely here.

3. Where Riya is Only Partly Correct (Case III):
When one fraction is less than $1$ and the other is greater than $1$.
$$\frac{1}{4} \times 4 = 1$$ The product $1$ is larger than $\frac{1}{4}$, but smaller than $4$. It is smaller than one factor, but larger than the other!

4.3 Commutative & Associative Laws for Fractions

Fraction multiplication strictly obeys the foundational laws of algebra:

Commutative Property:

$$\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c}{d} \times \frac{a}{b}$$

The order of multiplying two fractions does not alter the product.
$$\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{5}{7} = \frac{10}{21} = \frac{5}{7} \times \frac{2}{3}$$

Associative Property:

$$\left(\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}\right) \times \frac{e}{f} = \frac{a}{b} \times \left(\frac{c}{d} \times \frac{e}{f}\right)$$

When multiplying three or more fractions, grouping does not affect the final result.

4.4 Reciprocals of Fractions & The Zero Trap (Textbook p. 194)

Two non-zero numbers are called reciprocals (or multiplicative inverses) of each other if their product is strictly equal to $1$.

$$\text{If } \frac{a}{b} \text{ is a non-zero fraction, its reciprocal is } \frac{b}{a}$$ $$\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{b}{a} = \frac{ab}{ba} = 1$$
Given Number / Fraction Fraction Form Its Reciprocal Verification Product
$\frac{3}{2}$ (Improper) $\frac{3}{2}$ $\mathbf{\frac{2}{3}}$ (Proper) $\frac{3}{2} \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{6}{6} = 1$
$\frac{5}{9}$ (Proper) $\frac{5}{9}$ $\mathbf{\frac{9}{5} = 1\frac{4}{5}}$ (Improper) $\frac{5}{9} \times \frac{9}{5} = 1$
$7$ (Whole number) $\frac{7}{1}$ $\mathbf{\frac{1}{7}}$ (Unit fraction) $7 \times \frac{1}{7} = 1$
$1$ (Unity) $\frac{1}{1}$ $\mathbf{1}$ (Self-reciprocal) $1 \times 1 = 1$
$3\frac{1}{2}$ (Mixed number) $\frac{7}{2}$ $\mathbf{\frac{2}{7}}$ $\frac{7}{2} \times \frac{2}{7} = 1$

The Zero Reciprocal Trap:

Does the number $0$ have a reciprocal? NO! Any number multiplied by $0$ is $0$, never $1$ ($0 \times x = 0 \ne 1$). Therefore, division by $0$ is mathematically undefined, and zero has no reciprocal!

5.1 Brahmagupta & Bhāskara II Division Theorem (Textbook p. 195)

Division is the inverse operation of multiplication. In Indian mathematics, this operation was unified into multiplication by reciprocal:

Brahmagupta's Universal Statement (628 CE):

"The division of fractions is performed by interchanging the numerator and denominator of the divisor; the numerator of the dividend is then multiplied by the new numerator, and the denominator by the new denominator."

In 1150 CE in Līlāvatī, Bhāskara II termed this rule Bhāgahāra (division by multiplying by the inverted fraction):

$$\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \left(\text{Reciprocal of } \frac{c}{d}\right) = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c} = \frac{ad}{bc}$$

Three Golden Steps for Fraction Division:

  1. Keep the first fraction (the dividend) exactly as it is.
  2. Change the division sign ($\div$) into a multiplication sign ($\times$).
  3. Invert the second fraction (the divisor) to write its reciprocal. Then multiply and simplify using Apavartana!

5.2 Division of a Whole Number by a Fraction

Dividing a whole number by a fraction answers: "How many fractional pieces of size $\frac{a}{b}$ can be cut out from $W$ wholes?"

Textbook Illustration 7 (p. 195)

Problem: Solve $15 \div 3\frac{1}{2}$

Step 1: Convert mixed fraction to improper: $3\frac{1}{2} = \frac{(3 \times 2) + 1}{2} = \frac{7}{2}$.

Step 2: Invert divisor and multiply: $$15 \div \frac{7}{2} = 15 \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{30}{7} = \mathbf{4\frac{2}{7}}$$

Textbook Illustration 10 (p. 195) — Rope Cutting Model

Problem: Jay cuts a $20\text{ m}$ long rope into smaller pieces of length $3\frac{1}{3}\text{ m}$ each. How many pieces of rope did he get?

Total rope length $= 20\text{ m}$. Length of one piece $= 3\frac{1}{3}\text{ m} = \frac{10}{3}\text{ m}$.

$$\text{Number of pieces} = 20 \div \frac{10}{3} = \cancel{20}^2 \times \frac{3}{\cancel{10}_1} = 2 \times 3 = \mathbf{6\text{ pieces}}$$

Jay gets exactly 6 full pieces of rope!

5.3 Division of a Fraction by Another Fraction

When dividing two fractions, apply Brahmagupta's reciprocal rule directly:

Textbook Illustration 8 (p. 195)

Problem: Simplify $5\frac{1}{6} \div \frac{9}{2}$

Convert $5\frac{1}{6} = \frac{31}{6}$. Then invert divisor $\frac{9}{2} \to \frac{2}{9}$:

$$\frac{31}{6} \div \frac{9}{2} = \frac{31}{\cancel{6}_3} \times \frac{\cancel{2}^1}{9} = \frac{31 \times 1}{3 \times 9} = \frac{31}{27} = \mathbf{1\frac{4}{27}}$$
Textbook Illustration 9 (p. 195) — Tailoring Problem

Problem: $25$ shirts of equal size are prepared from $30\frac{5}{9}\text{ m}$ of cloth. Find the length of cloth required for making one shirt.

Total cloth $= 30\frac{5}{9}\text{ m} = \frac{(30 \times 9) + 5}{9} = \frac{275}{9}\text{ m}$.

Number of shirts $= 25$. Cloth per shirt:

$$\frac{275}{9} \div 25 = \frac{\cancel{275}^{11}}{9} \times \frac{1}{\cancel{25}_1} = \frac{11}{9}\text{ m} = \mathbf{1\frac{2}{9}\text{ m}}$$

5.4 Comparing Quotients in Fraction Division

Just as in multiplication, the behavior of quotients in division reveals fundamental scaling properties:

Divisor Nature Operation Behavior of Quotient Concrete Demonstration
Divisor is Proper (< 1) $A \div \frac{a}{b}$ where $\frac{a}{b} < 1$ Quotient is GREATER than the dividend! $6 \div \frac{1}{2} = 6 \times 2 = \mathbf{12 > 6}$
Cutting into halves doubles the count!
Divisor is Improper (> 1) $A \div \frac{a}{b}$ where $\frac{a}{b} > 1$ Quotient is SMALLER than the dividend! $6 \div \frac{3}{2} = 6 \times \frac{2}{3} = \mathbf{4 < 6}$

Ex 3: Area of Fractional Rectangle

6.2 Multi-Stage Sharing Problems (Textbook pp. 193 & 201)

Textbook Example 12 (p. 193)

Ex 12: Avinash's Multi-Tier Family Inheritance

Problem: Avinash had ₹55,000. He kept $\frac{1}{11}$ of it for himself, gave half of the remaining money to his wife, and divided the rest equally among his four children. How much money did each child get?

  1. Total Amount: ₹$55,000$
  2. Avinash's share: $\frac{1}{11} \times 55,000 = \text{₹}5,000$
  3. Remaining amount: $55,000 - 5,000 = \text{₹}50,000$
  4. Wife's share: $\frac{1}{2} \times 50,000 = \text{₹}25,000$
  5. Amount left for 4 children: $50,000 - 25,000 = \text{₹}25,000$
  6. Each child's share: $\frac{25,000}{4} = \mathbf{\text{₹}6,250}$
Textbook Example 13 (p. 201)

Ex 13: The Dynamic Mango Seller Problem

Problem: Out of 140 mangoes, a fruit seller sells half of existing mangoes plus $1$ to the 1st customer, then sells $\frac{1}{3}$ of remainder plus $1$ to the 2nd customer, $\frac{1}{5}$ of remainder plus $1$ to the 3rd customer, and $\frac{1}{7}$ of remainder plus $1$ to the 4th customer. How many mangoes remain unsold?

Customer Starting Stock Mangoes Sold: (Fraction $\times$ Stock) + 1 Remaining Stock
1st Customer $140$ $\frac{1}{2} \times 140 + 1 = 70 + 1 = \mathbf{71}$ $140 - 71 = \mathbf{69}$
2nd Customer $69$ $\frac{1}{3} \times 69 + 1 = 23 + 1 = \mathbf{24}$ $69 - 24 = \mathbf{45}$
3rd Customer $45$ $\frac{1}{5} \times 45 + 1 = 9 + 1 = \mathbf{10}$ $45 - 10 = \mathbf{35}$
4th Customer $35$ $\frac{1}{7} \times 35 + 1 = 5 + 1 = \mathbf{6}$ $35 - 6 = \mathbf{29}$

Final Result: Exactly 29 mangoes remain unsold!

Textbook p. 196: Figure It Out Q4 (The Fencepost Law)

Tsewang's Sapling Spacing Problem

Problem: Tsewang plants $4$ saplings in a row in his garden. The distance between any two adjacent saplings is $\frac{3}{4}\text{ m}$. Find the distance between the first and the last sapling.

Common Trap Alert: Do not multiply by 4! 4 saplings in a row have exactly 3 intervals between them:

[Tree 1] —— $\frac{3}{4}\text{ m}$ —— [Tree 2] —— $\frac{3}{4}\text{ m}$ —— [Tree 3] —— $\frac{3}{4}\text{ m}$ —— [Tree 4]

$$\text{Total distance} = 3 \times \frac{3}{4}\text{ m} = \frac{9}{4}\text{ m} = \mathbf{2\frac{1}{4}\text{ m}}$$

7.3 Telescoping Product Series (Textbook p. 202 Q12)

Evaluate each product and discover the magnificent cancellation law:

$$\text{Step 1: } 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}}$$ $$\text{Step 2: } \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{\cancel{2}} \times \frac{\cancel{2}}{3} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{3}}$$ $$\text{Step 3: } \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{3}\right) \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{4}\right) \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{5}\right) = \frac{1}{\cancel{2}} \times \frac{\cancel{2}}{\cancel{3}} \times \frac{\cancel{3}}{\cancel{4}} \times \frac{\cancel{4}}{5} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{5}}$$ $$\text{Step 4: } \prod_{k=2}^{10} \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right) = \frac{1}{\cancel{2}} \times \frac{\cancel{2}}{\cancel{3}} \times \dots \times \frac{\cancel{9}}{10} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{10}}$$

General Mathematical Law:

$$\prod_{k=2}^{n} \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{n}}$$ Proof: Each intermediate numerator cancels out the preceding denominator, leaving only the very first numerator ($1$) and the very last denominator ($n$)!

7.4 Geometric Region Deduction (Textbook p. 207 Q50)

In rectangle $ABCD$, regions $P, Q, R, S$ partition the space. $R$ is thrice the size of $P$ ($R = 3P$) and half the size of $S$ ($S = 2R = 6P$). $Q$ is twice the size of $P$ ($Q = 2P$).

Figure 7.2 (Textbook p. 207 Q50): Rectangle $ABCD$ divided into sub-regions $P, Q, R, S$.

Deduction of Fraction $\frac{Q + S}{\text{Total}}$:

Let $P = x$. Then:
$Q = 2x$
$R = 3x$
$S = 2R = 2(3x) = 6x$
$$\text{Total Area} = P + Q + R + S = x + 2x + 3x + 6x = \mathbf{12x}$$ $$Q + S = 2x + 6x = \mathbf{8x}$$ $$\text{Fraction} = \frac{8x}{12x} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}}$$
