Primary Auxiliary Verbs (Be, Have, Do)

Textbook Definition: Auxiliary verbs that assist principal verbs to formulate continuous, perfect, or passive constructions, as well as questions and negative sentences. Unlike modals, primary auxiliaries can also function autonomously as main verbs.
Layman Terms: Multi-tasker verbs! When alone, they state facts, conditions, or possession. When paired with another verb, they act as helpers to indicate time, ask questions, or say 'no'.
The 'Be' Family (am, is, are, was, were, been, being) The 'Have' Family (has, have, had, having) The 'Do' Family (do, does, did)
1. She is writing an exciting adventure novel.
2. They were playing badminton in the courtyard yesterday.
3. The antique vase was broken by accident.
4. I am preparing my science presentation.
5. The highway is being paved by construction crews.
6. We have been waiting here since sunrise.
7. She is a brilliant neuroscientist at the hospital.
8. The students were exceptionally quiet during the exam. 		1. She has completed her master's degree with honors.
2. We have visited five different continents so far.
3. The train had departed before we reached the platform.
4. They have been living in this cottage for a decade.
5. I have three antique pocket watches in my collection.
6. She has a slight fever and needs to rest.
7. We had a wonderful family dinner last night.
8. Do you have an extra pencil I could borrow? 		1. Do you recognize the woman in that portrait?
2. He does not enjoy spicy street food.

4. I do hope you recover from your illness swiftly!

7. He does yoga every morning at 6 AM.
8. Who did this magnificent charcoal sketch?

Modal Auxiliaries: Characteristics & Core Rules

Textbook Definition: Defective auxiliary verbs that express modal meanings (ability, permission, possibility, necessity, etc.). Unlike primary auxiliaries, modals do not inflect for person or number (no -s, -ed, or -ing forms) and are invariably followed by a bare infinitive.
Layman Terms: The stubborn rule-followers! They never add '-s' even with he/she/it, they can never wear '-ing' or '-ed', they always need a plain partner verb (base form), and they never stand alone as complete actions.
Core Grammatical Rule Correct Usage Examples Incorrect Common Pitfalls
No 3rd Person '-s' He can solve complex equations.
She must leave right away.		 *He cans solve* (Incorrect)
*She musts leave* (Incorrect)
Followed by Bare Infinitive You should go home now.
They might come later.		 *You should to go* (Incorrect)
*They might coming* (Incorrect)
No Auxiliary 'Do' in Questions May I enter the hall?
Can you speak French?		 *Do I may enter?* (Incorrect)
*Do you can speak?* (Incorrect)
Direct Negative with 'Not' You must not touch the wire.
We cannot stay here.		 *You don't must touch* (Incorrect)
*We don't can stay* (Incorrect)
No Non-Finite Forms She wants to be able to drive.
He had to go early.		 *She wants to can drive* (Incorrect)
*He had to must go* (Incorrect)
No Double Modals I might be able to help.
He will have to leave.		 *I might can help* (Incorrect)
*He will must leave* (Incorrect)
Invariant Past Modals She could run fast as a child.
I would read all night.		 *She can-ed run* (Incorrect)
*I will-ed read* (Incorrect)
Semi-Modal Exception We ought to obey the law.
You used to live here.		 *We ought obey* (Needs 'to')
*You used live* (Needs 'to')

Modals of Ability & Capacity (Can, Could, Be Able To)

Textbook Definition: Modal auxiliaries denoting physical, intellectual, or acquired capability. 'Can' indicates present or general ability; 'Could' denotes past ability or theoretical capacity; 'Be able to' expresses ability across all tenses, especially specific single-event achievements.
Layman Terms: Saying "I have the superpower or skill to do this!" 'Can' is your present skill, 'Could' is what you used to do back in the day, and 'Was able to' means you managed to succeed in a tough situation.
Present Ability (Can / Cannot) Past General Ability (Could / Couldn't) Specific Achievements (Was / Were Able To)

7. We can finish the entire syllabus ahead of schedule.
8. He cannot swim without wearing flotation armbands. 		1. In his twenties, my grandfather could run a marathon in three hours.

3. Before the injury, the gymnast could perform double backflips.

5. We could hear the wolves howling in the distant valley all night.
6. I couldn't understand what the foreign tourist was saying.
7. Beethoven could still compose masterpieces after losing his hearing.

Modals of Permission, Requests & Prohibitions (May, Can, Could, Would)

Textbook Definition: Modals indicating formal or informal authorization, granting and denying consent, or formulating requests graded by politeness, social distance, and deference.
Layman Terms: The good manners verbs! Use 'Can' with friends, 'Could' or 'Would' to be super polite, and 'May' when speaking to teachers, bosses, or examiners.
Formal & Official Permission (May / May Not) Informal & Friendly Requests (Can / Cannot) Polite Requests & Inquiries (Could / Would)

Modals of Possibility, Probability & Logical Deduction (May, Might, Could, Must, Can't)

Textbook Definition: Modals used by speakers to calibrate degrees of epistemic certainty—ranging from weak, remote possibility (might, could) to strong, factual possibility (may) and unavoidable logical deduction (must, can't).
Layman Terms: The detective scale! From "a wild guess" (might/could), to "a fair chance" (may), all the way to "Sherlock Holmes certainty" (must = definitely yes, can't = definitely no).
Possibility & Uncertainty (May, Might, Could) Strong Logical Deduction (Must) Negative Deduction / Impossibility (Can't / Cannot)

3. If you leave right now, you could catch the express shuttle.
4. She hasn't replied to my text; she might be driving right now.
5. The sudden drop in temperature may cause frost on the crops.
6. We could encounter heavy tourist traffic near the hill station.
7. Scientists believe the newly discovered exoplanet might harbor water.
8. The museum may stay open late during the weekend cultural festival. 		1. The streets are soaking wet and puddles are everywhere; it must have rained.
2. She studied sixteen hours daily for six months; she must be exhausted.
3. His car is parked outside and the TV is on; he must be inside the house.
4. The engine of the motorcycle is burning hot; someone must have ridden it recently.
5. Wearing an Olympic gold medal and smiling broadly, she must be overjoyed.
6. Having lived in Tokyo for thirty years, Mr. Tanaka must know Japanese culture deeply.
7. There is an endless queue outside that bakery; the sourdough must be extraordinary.
8. You have walked thirty kilometers in the blazing heat; you must be ravenously hungry. 		1. That cannot be Mr. Harrison; he emigrated to Australia two years ago!

4. She can't have forgotten her twin sister's wedding anniversary today.
5. The front door was bolted from the inside; the thief couldn't have escaped through it.
6. He can't be eighty years old; he runs ten kilometers every single morning!
7. That timid puppy cannot be vicious; it is gently licking the baby's fingers.
8. You can't have finished a five-hundred-page textbook in twenty minutes!

Modals of Duty, Necessity & Advice (Must, Ought to, Should, Have to)

Textbook Definition: Auxiliaries conveying prescriptive norms, ranging from subjective or moral duty (ought to), general recommendation and advisability (should), to absolute external or legal compulsion (must, have to).
Layman Terms: The "do your duty!" verbs. 'Should' is friendly advice, 'Ought to' is what your moral compass says is right, and 'Must' / 'Have to' is the law—break it and you face penalties!
Advice & Recommendation (Should / Shouldn't) Moral Duty & Social Expectation (Ought to) Compulsory Law & Strict Rule (Must / Have to)

5. If your dry cough lingers for over a week, you should visit a doctor.

Modals of Future, Determination & Habits (Will, Would, Shall)

Textbook Definition: Modals indicating future intention, spontaneous willingness, firm promises or threats, past habitual actions, hypothetical scenarios, and formal suggestions or offers.
Layman Terms: Looking forward or looking back! 'Will' is for iron promises and immediate decisions, 'Shall' is for suggestions ("Shall we?"), and 'Would' is for things you did routinely in the past.
Determination & Promises (Will / Won't) Past Habits & Hypotheticals (Would) Formal Offers & Suggestions (Shall)

2. We will never forget the tremendous sacrifice made by our martyrs.
3. I will help you assemble that wooden wardrobe this weekend.
4. The stubborn mule will not move an inch forward.
5. I will call you the instant my flight lands in Chicago.
6. We will overcome this financial hardship together as a team.
7. I won't disclose your confidential secret to anyone.
8. The government will build three new solar power plants this year. 		1. When we were children, my grandfather would take us fishing every Saturday.
2. She would spend hours sketching portraits beside the window.
3. Every evening after dinner, the village elders would gather beneath the banyan tree.
4. If I inherited a million dollars, I would establish a free animal shelter.
5. Would you mind lowering your voice while in the reading room?
6. He said he would join the trekking expedition next month.
7. I would rather starve than accept a bribe from corrupt officials.
8. When autumn arrived, migratory flocks would fly toward warmer southern shores. 		1. Shall I carry that heavy luggage bag to the car for you?
2. Shall we take a pleasant stroll along the beach before sunset?
3. Shall we start the meeting now or wait for the manager?
4. The tenant shall pay the monthly rent on or before the fifth day.
5. You shall receive your prize at the annual sports ceremony.
6. Shall I prepare a hot pot of chamomile tea for everyone?
7. Where shall we go for dinner to celebrate your victory?
8. No student shall enter the examination hall after the second bell.
