Personal Pronouns

Textbook Definition: Personal pronouns replace names of persons, animals, and things. They are classified into First Person (speaker: I, we), Second Person (spoken to: you), and Third Person (spoken about: he, she, it, they). Subjective pronouns act as the subject of a verb, while objective pronouns act as the object.
Layman Terms: Words used instead of people's or things' names so you don't repeat them. Subjective forms do the action; objective forms receive the action.
Subjective (Performs Action)Objective (Receives Action)
• I enjoy learning modern world history.
• We won first prize in the national debate.
• You speak three international languages fluently.
• He completed the grueling marathon yesterday.
• She plays the violin with great elegance.
• It rained heavily throughout the entire night.
• They constructed a beautiful treehouse together.
• You all need to assemble inside the hall. 		• The school principal congratulated me warmly.
• Grandma baked delicious chocolate cookies for us.
• I will send you all the study materials tonight.
• The barking watchdog chased him down the lane.
• The committee presented her with a gold medal.
• The hungry kitten devoured it in seconds.
• The coach encouraged them before the match started.
• The tour guide led you all safely along the trail.

Possessive & Demonstrative Pronouns

Textbook Definition: Possessive pronouns denote ownership or possession (mine, ours, yours, his, hers, its, theirs). Demonstrative pronouns indicate or point out specific persons or things based on distance (this, that, these, those).
Layman Terms: Possessive words show whose property something is without needing another noun. Demonstrative words point an imaginary finger at nearby or faraway objects.
Possessive (Ownership)Demonstrative (Pointing Out)
• That shiny red bicycle by the porch is mine.
• The hard-fought championship trophy is ours.

• The warm winter jacket hanging on the wall is his.
• The beautifully decorated vanilla cake is hers.
• The puppy guarded the bone because it was its.
• The grand victory in the tournament was theirs.

• Those are snow-capped peaks of the distant Himalayas.
• Is this the parcel you were expecting earlier today?
• These taste far sweeter than the fruits we bought last week.
• That looks like a rare eagle flying over the ridge.
• Those were truly the most memorable days of childhood.

Interrogative & Relative Pronouns

Textbook Definition: Interrogative pronouns are used to ask questions (who, whom, whose, which, what). Relative pronouns refer back to an antecedent noun or pronoun and connect clauses (who, whom, whose, which, that).
Layman Terms: Interrogative pronouns ask the questions (Who? What? Which?). Relative pronouns act as connectors that bridge extra details back to a person or thing.
Interrogative (Asking Questions)Relative (Connecting Clauses)

• Whose are these muddy boots left in the corridor?
• Which of these flavor options do you prefer?

Reflexive & Emphatic Pronouns

Textbook Definition: Reflexive pronouns reflect the action of the verb back upon the subject and follow verbs or prepositions. Emphatic pronouns are placed after nouns or pronouns to emphasize that the subject performed the action personally.
Layman Terms: Both end in -self or -selves. Reflexive is a boomerang action (you do it to yourself). Emphatic is adding extra drama/insistence that YOU did it with your own hands.
Reflexive (Action Reflects on Subject)Emphatic (Added Personal Emphasis)
• I accidentally nicked myself while peeling an apple.
• You should always believe in yourself and stay calm.
• He prepared himself mentally for the grueling exam.
• She bought herself a pair of warm woolen gloves.
• The tiny kitten groomed itself quietly on the rug.
• We enjoyed ourselves thoroughly at the amusement park.
• You all must protect yourselves against the heatwave.
• The frightened villagers hid themselves in the shelter. 		• I myself inspected every single line of the document.
• You yourself signed the contract and agreed to all terms.
• The Prime Minister himself inaugurated the new terminal.
• The head chef herself cooked every dish for the banquet.
• The fortress itself is over seven centuries old.
• We ourselves planted fifty fruit saplings in the orchard.
• You yourselves witnessed the miraculous recovery firsthand.
• The directors themselves conceded that mistakes had been made.

Distributive, Reciprocal & Indefinite Pronouns

Textbook Definition: Distributive pronouns refer to individuals of a group considered separately (each, either, neither). Reciprocal pronouns indicate mutual actions (each other, one another). Indefinite pronouns refer to persons or things in an unspecified manner (someone, anyone, nobody, everything, few, all).
Layman Terms: Distributive treats group members one by one. Reciprocal describes a two-way street / ping-pong action. Indefinite refers to unspecified folks or general quantities.

Exercise A: Subjective vs Objective Pronouns
Identify and write the pronouns in these sentences as subjective or objective.
1. We will join you at the party tomorrow. → We (subjective), you (objective)
2. Can you help us with our homework? → you (subjective), us (objective)
3. I saw him at the park yesterday. → I (subjective), him (objective)
4. He looked at me and smiled. → He (subjective), me (objective)
5. The teacher asked if he had completed his assignment. → he (subjective) [his is possessive adj]
6. You are an intelligent person. → You (subjective)
7. It was a beautiful day. → It (subjective)
8. They enjoyed at the party. → They (subjective)

✨ Beyond the Textbook

E1. She gave them the instructions clearly. → She (subjective), them (objective)
E2. They invited her to the concert. → They (subjective), her (objective)
E3. I told him the truth about the incident. → I (subjective), him (objective)
E4. We rely on you for dependable advice. → We (subjective), you (objective)
E5. He asked us to join the sports club. → He (subjective), us (objective)
