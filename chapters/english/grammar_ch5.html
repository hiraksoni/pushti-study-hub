Attributive vs. Predicative Adjectives

Textbook Definition: Attributive adjectives are used before the noun to qualify it directly. Predicative adjectives are used in the predicate after a linking verb (such as be, feel, become, seem, look).
Layman Terms: Attributive sits right in front of the thing it describes. Predicative comes after a linking verb to describe the subject across the sentence.

Kinds of Adjectives

Textbook Definition: Adjectives are classified into distinct categories: Quality, Quantity, Number, Demonstrative, Possessive, Interrogative, Distributive, Emphasising, Proper, and Compound.
Layman Terms: Different ways we describe things—by their appearance, amount, count, pointing them out, stating ownership, asking about them, highlighting individuals, or emphasizing facts.
KindExamples (At least 8 per category)
Quality (shape, size, colour, state)brave, intelligent, blue, round, heavy, smooth, crunchy, tired, energetic, graceful
Quantity (how much - uncountable)some, enough, whole, little, much, all, any, half, sufficient, abundant
Number (how many - countable)five, several, many, few, first, second, numerous, dozen, twenty, double
Demonstrative (pointing out)this, that, these, those, such, certain, yonder, same
Possessive (ownership)my, your, his, her, its, our, their, whose
Interrogative (asking questions)which book, what time, whose turn, which street, what color, whose idea, which answer, what question
Distributive (individual members)each child, every student, either side, neither team, each day, every person, either way, neither candidate
Emphasising (laying stress)very, actual, own, pure, absolute, total, complete, utter
Proper (from proper nouns)Indian, Italian, American, Shakespearean, Victorian, Japanese, Chinese, British
Compound (combined words)ice-cold, brightly-lit, well-known, open-minded, part-time, world-famous, good-looking, middle-aged

Possessive Adjectives vs. Possessive Pronouns

Textbook Definition: Possessive adjectives qualify a noun by coming immediately before it to indicate ownership. Possessive pronouns replace the noun completely and stand alone without a subsequent noun.
Layman Terms: Possessive adjectives always need a noun partner directly after them (my book). Possessive pronouns stand completely independently (mine).

Degrees of Comparison

Textbook Definition: Adjectives change in form to show degrees of comparison: Positive (the base form expressing a quality), Comparative (compares two entities), Superlative (compares three or more entities).
Layman Terms: The base descriptor (tall), the two-way comparison with "-er" or "more" (taller), and the top-of-the-group champion with "-est" or "most" (tallest).

Order of Adjectives (OSASCOMP)

Textbook Definition: When multiple adjectives precede a single noun, they typically follow the conventional hierarchical sequence: Opinion → Size → Age → Shape → Colour → Origin → Material → Purpose.
Layman Terms: A mnemonic rule (OSASCOMP) describing the natural order English speakers instinctively use when piling up descriptive words before a thing.
