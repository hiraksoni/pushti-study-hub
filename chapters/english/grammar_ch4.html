Nominative (Subjective) Case

Textbook Definition: The nominative case is used when a noun or pronoun functions as the subject of a finite verb in a sentence, performing the action or being the primary focus of description.
Layman Terms: The "do-er" or main character of the sentence! Simply ask "Who?" or "What?" before the main verb to uncover the nominative subject.

Objective (Accusative) Case

Textbook Definition: The objective (accusative) case is used when a noun or pronoun functions as the direct object of a transitive verb or as the object following a preposition, receiving the direct impact of the verb's action.
Layman Terms: The direct "receiver" of the action! Ask "Whom?" or "What?" directly after the verb, or locate the noun/pronoun sitting right after a preposition like at, in, to, through, or with.

Dative Case (Indirect Object)

Textbook Definition: The dative case denotes the indirect object of a verb—the person, animal, or entity to whom or for whom an action is performed, positioned between the transitive verb and the direct object.
Layman Terms: The beneficiary who gets the gift or favor! Whenever someone gives, buys, shows, sends, or tells something to someone, that fortunate receiver is in the dative case.

Possessive (Genitive) Case

Textbook Definition: The possessive (genitive) case denotes ownership, possession, origin, or authorship. In nouns it is marked by an apostrophe with 's' (-'s or -'), while pronouns employ dedicated possessive determiners and independent possessive pronouns.
Layman Terms: Words showing who owns what! For people or animals, add 's (e.g. John's dog). For pronouns, use possessive adjectives like my, your, our, or standalone words like mine, yours, theirs.

Special Case Rules & Pitfalls (Stay Ahead)

Textbook Definition: Advanced grammatical rules govern case selection in elliptical comparisons with 'than' or 'as', compound constructions, vocative direct address, and inanimate possessives.
Layman Terms: Tricky grammar traps where even adults stumble! Learn the master rules for "between you and me", "taller than I", and setting off names in direct address.
Grammar Rule & ConceptHigh-Level Practical Examples
Between you and me (Preposition governs Objective; never "between you and I").
Compound Subjects (Use Nominative: "Rohan and I", not "Rohan and me").
Compound Objects (Use Objective: "invited Rohan and me", not "and I").
Comparisons with 'Than' / 'As' (Elliptical clause uses Nominative: "taller than I [am]").
Predicate Nominative with 'To Be' (Formal: "It is I", "This was he").
Vocative Case (Direct Address) (Set off with commas: "Come here, John").
Inanimate Possessives (Use 'of the...', not "'s": "leg of the table").
Case with Gerunds (Possessive case precedes gerund: "appreciated your helping").		 Please keep this confidential discussion strictly between you and me.
