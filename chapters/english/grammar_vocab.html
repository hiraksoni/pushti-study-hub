1. Homophones
Pages 149–150
Definition: Homophones are words that have similar or identical pronunciations, but different meanings and different spellings.
Memory Hook: Homo (same) + Phone (sound) = Same sound, different spelling!

Look at this classic example from your book:

❌ "Anshu is deer to me. I saw a dear in the zoo." (Illogical due to phonetic confusion)
✔ "Anshu is dear to me. I saw many deer in the zoo." (Correctly used homophones!)
Homophone Pair Part of Speech & Meaning Contextual Example Sentence
advice vs advise advice noun: idea or suggestion given to help decide
advise verb: to offer suggestions or counsel		 • He did not listen to his father's advice.
• My mother advised me to work hard.
bear vs bare bear noun/verb: a large wild mammal / to endure
bare adjective: uncovered or naked		 • I saw a bear in the zoo.
• Do not walk on hot pavement with bare feet.

site noun: location or place where something is built		 • Pramod cited many examples from the book.
• This is the construction site for our new house.

compliment noun/verb: praise, admiration or respect		 • The dishes on the menu complement each other perfectly.
• Everyone complimented him on his achievement.
check vs cheque check verb: to examine or verify
cheque noun: written bank order to pay money		 • Please check your answers before submission.
• He gave me a cheque for ₹10,000.
die vs dye die verb: to cease living
dye verb/noun: to colour fabric or hair		 • At least six people died in the road accident.
• She will dye her hair black tomorrow.
brake vs break brake noun: device to stop a vehicle
break verb: to separate into pieces / split		 • My bicycle has no rear brakes.
• Please don't break my favourite toy!

lose vs loose lose verb: to be unable to find something / fail to win
loose adjective: not firmly fitting or fastened		 • Be careful not to lose your house keys.
• He was wearing a loose cotton shirt.
not vs knot not adverb: word expressing denial or refusal
knot noun: fastening made by tying cord/rope		 • Ghosts are not real.

stationary vs stationery stationary adjective: fixed in place, not moving
stationery noun: writing materials (paper, pens, notebooks)		 • The train remained stationary on track two.
• I need to buy school stationery (Tip: stationery has e for envelopes!).

Pages 151–152
Definition: Homonyms are words that are spelt and pronounced in the exact same manner, but have different meanings depending on the context in which they are used.
Key Distinction: Homophones sound alike (may look different). Homonyms look AND sound alike, but mean completely different things!

3. Suffixes & The 5 Rules of Spelling
Pages 152–154
Definition: Suffixes are letters or groups of letters added to the end of a root word to modify its meaning or change its grammatical function.
Examples: comfort + -able = comfortable • pain + -less = painless • bad + -ly = badly
Suffix Grammatical Role Textbook Examples
-able Capable of, worthy of admirable, agreeable, approachable, avoidable, believable, breakable, comfortable
-en To become or cause to be (verb) blacken, brighten, cheapen, dampen, darken, fatten, flatten, freshen, gladden
-ful Full of, having quantity of armful, careful, cheerful, colourful, delightful, fearful, forgetful, helpful, hopeful
-ion Action, process, state (noun) action, attraction, celebration, collection, collision, communication, confusion
-ly In a manner of (adverb) badly, bravely, brightly, cleverly, fairly, foolishly, freely, gladly, greatly, happily
-less Without, lacking fruitless, priceless, speechless, sleepless, hapless, senseless, limitless, cordless
-er One who performs an action catcher, commander, dancer, defender, designer, dodger, driver, dryer, explorer

The 5 Golden Rules of Adding Suffixes

Rule 1: Suffixes -ness and -ly

The spelling of the root word generally does NOT change:

kind + -ness = kindness • love + -ly = lovely
Stay Ahead Exception: If the root word ends in a consonant + -y, change -y to -i before adding -ness or -ly:
ready + -ly = readily • giddy + -ness = giddiness • happy + -ness = happiness
Rule 2: Words Ending in Vowel + -y

When a word ends with -y preceded by a vowel (a, e, i, o, u), keep the -y when adding any suffix:

enjoy + -ed = enjoyed • play + -ing = playing • buy + -er = buyer • joy + -ful = joyful
Rule 3: Words Ending in Silent -e

When adding a suffix starting with a vowel, drop the silent final -e:

care + -ing = caring • noise + -y = noisy • make + -er = maker
Crucial Exception: For words ending in -ce or -ge, keep the silent -e if the suffix begins with a or o (to keep the soft sound of c and g):
courage + -ous = courageous • peace + -able = peaceable • notice + -able = noticeable
Rule 4: Consonant Doubling with Short Vowels (CVC Rule)

When a single-syllable word ends in a single consonant preceded by a single short vowel, double the final consonant before a vowel suffix:

flip + -ed = flipped • mud + -y = muddy • run + -ing = running • big + -est = biggest
Rule 5: Doubling Final l in Multi-Syllable Words

When a word has more than one syllable and ends with the letter l, double the l when adding suffixes:

control + -ed = controlled • compel + -ing = compelling • travel + -er = traveller
*Note: This rule does not apply to words ending in -w, -x, or -y.
4. Common Incorrect Phrases
Pages 154–155
Many popular English phrases are misused because of acoustic resemblance or spelling confusion. Here are the 10 most common mistakes you must fix for your exam:
Incorrect Phrase ❌ Correct Standard Phrase ✔ Meaning & Contextual Explanation
all and all all in all Considering all parts or factors of the situation together.
day in age day and age Modern times or current era, as opposed to an earlier historical period.
all for not all for naught All efforts were in vain; amounted to completely nothing (naught = zero).
one in the same one and the same Referring to the identical entity, person, or concept.
peaked my interest piqued my interest To stimulate, rouse, or awaken curiosity (from French piquer, to prick).
case and point case in point A specific instance or exemplary case that illustrates what is being argued.
another think coming another thing coming You are mistaken and will need to reconsider your assumption or plan.
nerve wrecking nerve racking Extremely stressful, causing acute anxiety or agony.
I could care less I couldn't care less I do not care at all (saying "could care less" implies you actually do care a bit!).
deep seeded deep-seated Firmly established, entrenched; deeply rooted and hard to dislodge.

6. Common Indian English Expressions to Avoid
Pages 155–156
These phrases arise from literal word-for-word translation from Hindi and Indian regional languages into English. In formal exams and international communication, standard alternatives should always be used:
"The meeting was preponed."
"The meeting was rescheduled / brought forward."
The antonym of postpone in standard English is not 'prepone'. Use brought forward or rescheduled to an earlier date.
"What is your good name?"
"What is your name?" / "May I know your name?"
Direct translation of the polite Hindi phrase 'शुभ नाम'. In English, names don't have adjectives like 'good'. Simply ask their name.
"I am just taking my lunch."
"I am having / eating my lunch."
'Taking lunch' implies physically carrying a lunchbox to another place. For consumption, use having lunch.
"I am on my way, only."
"I am on my way." / "I'm already en route."
The unnecessary addition of 'only' comes from the Hindi emphatic particle 'ही' ("मैं रास्ते में ही हूँ"). Omit 'only'.
"He passed out of college in 2020."
"He graduated from college in 2020."
In standard English, to pass out means to faint or lose consciousness! For completing an educational degree, use graduated.
"The list is given below."
"Below is the list." / "The following is the list."
Direct translation of 'नीचे दिया गया है'. Simply writing below or as follows is more natural and concise.
7. Unnecessary Repetition (Redundancies / Tautology)
Pages 156–158
Tautology or pleonasm is the needless repetition of words that mean the same thing. Because the modifier is already contained within the core word, using both makes your writing wordy and unpolished.
Wrongfully Used Term ❌ Why It Is Redundant & What To Use Instead ✔
actual fact A fact by definition is something proved true. Just use fact.
added bonus A bonus is already an addition beyond expectation. Just use bonus.
ATM machine The letter 'M' in ATM already stands for Machine (Automated Teller Machine). Saying "ATM machine" says "machine" twice! Just say ATM.
blatantly obvious Things that are blatant are already conspicuous and obvious. Use either blatant or obvious.
empty space Space by definition is an unoccupied area. Just say space.
false pretence A pretence is by nature deceptive and false. Just say pretence.
repay back Repay already means to pay back. Use either repay or pay back.
repeat again Repeat means to say or do again. Just say repeat.
return back Return means to come or go back. Use either return or go back.
revert back Revert means returning to a previous condition or replying. Do not use 'back'. Say revert or reply.
brief moment A moment is by definition brief. Say moment or brief period.
circulate around To circulate means to move around. Drop 'around'; just say circulate.
close proximity Proximity means nearness in space. Just say proximity or close to.
collaborate together The prefix col- already implies together. Say collaborate or work together.
exact same Same means identical and not different. Just use the same.
two equal halves A half is by mathematical definition one of two equal parts. Just say two halves.
cancel out Cancel means voided or annulled. Drop 'out'; just say cancel.
free gift A gift is given freely without payment. Say gift.
unexpected surprise A surprise is something unexpected. Say surprise.
using a.m. with morning a.m. stands for ante meridiem (before noon/morning). Say 9 a.m. OR nine in the morning, not both together.
total destruction Destruction is the process of being destroyed. Saying 'total' adds no extra meaning.
temper tantrum A tantrum is always an outburst of bad temper. Just say tantrum.
general public Public means the ordinary people of a community. Just say the public.
first and foremost Foremost already denotes the very first in rank or order. Just use foremost or first.

Exercise D & E: Spot & Eliminate Redundancies
Textbook Pages 155–158
Identify the redundant word and rewrite each sentence cleanly:
1. "He went to the ATM machine to withdraw cash."
What is redundant? Redundancy: 'machine'. Correct: "He went to the ATM to withdraw cash."
2. "The school will revert back to in-person classes next Monday."
What is redundant? Redundancy: 'back'. Correct: "The school will revert to in-person classes next Monday."
3. "The article peaked my interest in marine biology."
What is wrong? Wrong phrase: 'peaked'. Correct: "The article piqued my interest in marine biology."
4. "I will return back your library books tomorrow morning at 9 a.m."
Spot the two errors! Errors: 'return back' + 'morning at 9 a.m.'
Correct: "I will return your library books tomorrow at 9 a.m."
5. "My older sister passed out from Delhi University last summer."
What is the blunder? Blunder: 'passed out' means fainted!
Correct: "My older sister graduated from Delhi University last summer."
