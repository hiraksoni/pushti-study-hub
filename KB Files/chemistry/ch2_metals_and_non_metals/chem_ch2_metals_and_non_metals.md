### Classification of Elements & Physical Properties of Metals (Pages 35-37)

1. Classification of Elements:
   - Presently, 118 chemical elements are known and organized in the periodic table. Elements are broadly classified into metals, non-metals, and metalloids.
   - Metals: Elements that readily lose electrons to form positive ions (cations) and are electropositive in nature. They typically have 1, 2, or 3 valence electrons.
   - Occurrence: Unreactive noble metals (gold, silver, platinum) occur in the free or native elemental state. Reactive metals occur combined as minerals and ores in the Earth's crust.
   - Metallurgy: The technological process of extracting pure metals profitably from their ores.

2. Physical Properties of Metals:
   - Physical State: Almost all metals are solids at room temperature, with the notable exceptions of mercury (Hg) and gallium (Ga), which are liquids.
   - Melting and Boiling Points: Extremely high due to strong metallic bonds (exceptions: mercury and gallium have low melting points).
   - Hardness: Generally hard and strong (exceptions: lithium, sodium, and potassium are soft alkali metals that can be cut easily with a butter knife).
   - Metallic Lustre: Freshly cut metal surfaces have a characteristic shine and can be polished.
   - Malleability: The mechanical property by which metals can be hammered into thin sheets without breaking. Gold (Au) and silver (Ag) are the most malleable metals.
   - Ductility: The property by which metals can be drawn into thin, flexible wires. Gold is the most ductile metal (1 gram of gold can be drawn into a wire ~2 km long).
   - Electrical and Thermal Conductivity: Metals are excellent conductors of heat and electricity due to free mobile valence electrons. Silver (Ag) is the best conductor, followed closely by copper (Cu) and aluminium (Al).
   - Tensile Strength: High capacity to withstand longitudinal pull and heavy loads without snapping.
   - Sonorosity: Metals emit a resonant, ringing sound when struck with a hard object.
   - Density: Metals generally have high densities (exceptions: lithium, sodium, potassium have low densities and float on water).

3. 'Do You Know?' Callout Facts:
   - Gold is exceptionally ductile: one gram of pure gold can be drawn into a wire approximately 2 kilometres in length.
   - The most expensive metal is rhodium, followed by platinum and gold.
   - Recycling aluminium beverage cans saves 95% of the energy required to produce virgin aluminium from bauxite ore; recycling a single aluminium can saves enough electricity to run a television for 3 hours.

### Chemical Properties of Metals & Reactivity Series (Pages 37-41)

1. Reaction with Oxygen (Combustion):
   - Metals react with atmospheric oxygen to form basic metal oxides.
   - Sodium and Potassium react violently at room temperature, catching fire when exposed to moist air; therefore, they are stored submerged in kerosene oil:
     4Na(s) + O₂(g) → 2Na₂O(s)
     4K(s) + O₂(g) → 2K₂O(s)
   - Magnesium burns with a dazzling white light to form a white powder of magnesium oxide:
     2Mg(s) + O₂(g) --Heat--> 2MgO(s)
   - Aluminium, Zinc, and Lead form a thin, impervious protective surface oxide layer:
     4Al(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2Al₂O₃(s)
   - Iron reacts slowly with oxygen in the presence of moisture to form reddish-brown rust:
     4Fe(s) + 3O₂(g) + xH₂O(l) → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O(s)
   - Copper does not burn, but upon prolonged heating in air it forms a black coating of copper(II) oxide:
     2Cu(s) + O₂(g) --Prolonged heat--> 2CuO(s) [Black cupric oxide]
   - Noble metals (Ag, Au, Pt) do not combine with oxygen even at elevated temperatures.

2. Reaction with Water and Steam:
   - Sodium and Potassium react vigorously with cold water, producing alkaline hydroxides and hydrogen gas that ignites explosively:
     2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)↑ + Heat
   - Calcium reacts gently with cold water; bubbles of H₂ adhere to the metal surface, causing it to float:
     Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑
   - Magnesium does not react with cold water; it reacts with hot/boiling water to form magnesium hydroxide, or with steam to form magnesium oxide:
     Mg(s) + 2H₂O(l) --Hot water--> Mg(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑
     Mg(s) + H₂O(g) --Steam--> MgO(s) + H₂(g)↑
   - Aluminium, Zinc, and Iron do not react with cold or hot water; they react exclusively with steam to form oxides:
     2Al(s) + 3H₂O(g) → Al₂O₃(s) + 3H₂(g)↑
     Zn(s) + H₂O(g) → ZnO(s) + H₂(g)↑
     3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) [Magnetic iron oxide] + 4H₂(g)↑
   - Lead, Copper, Silver, and Gold do not react with water or steam under any conditions.

3. The Metal Reactivity (Activity) Series:
   - Decreasing order of chemical reactivity:
     K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Ni > Sn > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au
   - Metals above hydrogen displace hydrogen gas from dilute non-oxidising acids (HCl, H₂SO₄):
     Zn(s) + 2HCl(dil.) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑ (burns with a characteristic pop sound)
     Mg(s) + H₂SO₄(dil.) → MgSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)↑
   - Metals below hydrogen (Cu, Ag, Au) cannot displace hydrogen from dilute acids.

4. Displacement Reactions in Salt Solutions:
   - A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its aqueous salt solution:
     Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) [Blue] → FeSO₄(aq) [Light green] + Cu(s) [Reddish-brown deposit]
     Zn(s) + FeSO₄(aq) [Green] → ZnSO₄(aq) [Colourless] + Fe(s)
     Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) [Colourless] → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) [Blue] + 2Ag(s) [Silver crystals]
     Cu(s) + FeSO₄(aq) → No reaction (Copper is less reactive than iron)

### Corrosion, Prevention Methods & Commercial Alloys (Pages 42-45)

1. Corrosion of Metals:
   - The slow chemical destruction or eating away of a metal by atmospheric gases (oxygen, moisture, CO₂, H₂S) is called corrosion.
   - Rusting of Iron: Requires both oxygen and water. Forms hydrated ferric oxide:
     4Fe + 3O₂ + xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (Rust, non-adherent crumbly brown scale)
   - Corrosion of Copper: Exposed to moist air containing CO₂, copper develops a protective green patina of basic copper carbonate:
     2Cu + H₂O + CO₂ + O₂ → CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂ [Basic copper carbonate / Malachite green]
   - Tarnishing of Silver: Silver reacts with trace hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) gas in the air to form a black coating of silver sulphide:
     2Ag + H₂S → Ag₂S [Black] + H₂
   - Corrosion of Aluminium: Forms an impervious, tenacious surface film of aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) that shields underlying metal from further attack.

2. Prevention of Corrosion:
   - Barrier Protection: Applying paint, grease, oil, varnish, or enamel to exclude air and moisture.
   - Galvanization: Depositing a thin protective coating of metallic zinc on iron or steel by dipping into molten zinc or via electrolytic deposition. Zinc acts sacrificial: even if scratched, zinc corrodes preferentially before iron.
   - Electroplating: Coating a base metal with a less reactive or decorative metal using electrolysis:
     * Copper plating: Anode Cu, Cathode Object, Electrolyte CuSO₄ + dilute H₂SO₄
     * Silver plating: Anode Ag, Cathode Object, Electrolyte K[Ag(CN)₂]
     * Nickel plating: Anode Ni, Cathode Object, Electrolyte Nickel ammonium sulphate
     * Gold plating: Anode Au, Cathode Object, Electrolyte K[Au(CN)₂]
     * Zinc plating: Anode Zn, Cathode Object, Electrolyte ZnSO₄
     * Tin plating: Anode Sn, Cathode Object, Electrolyte SnSO₄

3. Alloys and Alloying:
   - An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal, in definite proportions, prepared by melting the components together.
   - 24-carat Gold is 100% pure gold and is very soft; 22-carat Gold contains 22 parts gold and 2 parts copper/silver to impart hardness and durability for jewellery.
   - Amalgam: An alloy in which mercury (Hg) is one of the constituent metals (e.g., sodium amalgam Na-Hg, dental silver amalgam).
   - Table of Commercial Alloys, Constituents and Uses:
     1. Brass: Cu (60-80%) and Zn (40-20%) -> Utensils, ammunition cartridges, musical instruments.
     2. Bronze: Cu (80%) and Sn (20%) -> Statues, medals, coins, heavy ship propellers.
     3. Solder: Sn (50%) and Pb (50%) -> Soldering electrical wires and electronic components (low melting point).
     4. Duralumin: Al (95%), Cu (4%), Mn (0.5%), Mg (0.5%) -> Lightweight aircraft bodies, auto parts, kitchenware.
     5. German Silver: Cu (50%), Zn (30%), Ni (20%) -> Tableware, decorative ornaments (contains NO actual silver).
     6. Gun Metal: Cu (88%), Sn (10%), Zn (2%) -> Heavy gears, industrial castings, gun barrels.
     7. Bell Metal: Cu (78%), Sn (22%) -> Acoustic church bells, temple gongs.
     8. Magnalium: Al (95%), Mg (5%) -> Analytical balance beams, precision optical instruments.
     9. Type Metal: Pb, Sb, Sn -> Printing press type casting (expands on solidification).
     10. Stainless Steel: Fe (73%), Cr (18%), Ni (8%), C (1%) -> Cutlery, cooking vessels, surgical instruments (resists rusting).

### Properties, Reactions and Uses of Non-metals (Pages 44-47)

1. Nature of Non-metals:
   - Non-metals are elements that tend to gain or share electrons to form negative ions (anions); they are electronegative and generally possess 5, 6, or 7 valence electrons.
   - Physical States: Found in all three physical states at room temperature:
     * Solids: Carbon (C), Sulphur (S), Phosphorus (P), Iodine (I₂).
     * Liquid: Bromine (Br₂) is the only liquid non-metal at standard conditions.
     * Gases: Hydrogen (H₂), Nitrogen (N₂), Oxygen (O₂), Chlorine (Cl₂), Fluorine (F₂), and Noble gases.
   - Mechanical Properties: Non-metals are non-malleable, non-ductile, and brittle; when hammered, solid non-metals shatter into powders.
   - Hardness: Generally soft, with the singular exception of diamond (an allotrope of carbon), which is the hardest naturally occurring substance known.
   - Electrical and Thermal Conductivity: Poor conductors of heat and electricity. Exception: Graphite (an allotropic form of carbon) is an excellent electrical conductor due to delocalized pi electrons.
   - Lustre: Dull in appearance, lacking metallic shine. Exception: Iodine crystals exhibit a shiny metallic lustre.
   - Sonorosity: Non-sonorous (do not produce a ringing sound when struck).

2. Chemical Properties of Non-metals:
   - Reaction with Oxygen: Form acidic or neutral oxides.
     * Carbon: C + O₂ → CO₂; CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (Carbonic acid)
     * Sulphur: S + O₂ → SO₂; SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (Sulphurous acid)
     * Neutral Oxides: Carbon monoxide (CO), Nitric oxide (NO), Nitrous oxide (N₂O), and Water (H₂O) are neutral to litmus.
   - Reaction with Water and Acids: Non-metals do not react with water or dilute acids because they cannot furnish electrons to reduce H⁺ ions into hydrogen gas (H₂).
   - Phosphorus is highly reactive with atmospheric oxygen (spontaneously catches fire) and is stored immersed in water.
   - Reactions with Chlorine:
     4P + 6Cl₂ → 4PCl₃ (Phosphorus trichloride)
     H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (Hydrogen chloride gas)
     C + 2Cl₂ → CCl₄ (Carbon tetrachloride)
   - Reactions with Hydrogen:
     S + H₂ → H₂S (Hydrogen sulphide gas, rotten-egg smell)
     N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ (Ammonia gas)

3. Industrial and Everyday Uses of Non-metals:
   - Oxygen: Respiration in all aerobic organisms; fuel combustion; oxy-acetylene welding torches.
   - Nitrogen: Synthesis of ammonia fertilisers (urea, ammonium nitrate); inert packaging gas for potato chips to prevent oxidation; liquid nitrogen as a cryogenic coolant.
   - Chlorine: Water purification/disinfection to kill pathogenic bacteria; bleaching agent; manufacture of PVC and disinfectants.
   - Iodine: Antiseptic (tincture of iodine for wounds); dietary nutrient in iodised salt to prevent goitre.
   - Sulphur: Vulcanization of natural rubber; manufacture of sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄); fungicides, skin ointments, and gunpowder.
   - Phosphorus: Match industry (safety matches and fireworks); phosphate fertilisers.
   - Carbon: Graphite for electrodes, dry cell batteries, and pencil leads; Diamond for glass cutting, rock drilling, and jewellery; Charcoal for fuel and water filters.
   - Hydrogen: Oxy-hydrogen welding; rocket fuel propellant; hydrogenation of vegetable oils into vanaspati ghee.
   - Silicon: Semiconductor microchips for computer electronics and solar photovoltaic panels.

### Comprehensive Comparison Between Metals and Non-metals (Pages 46-47)

1. Comparison of Physical Properties:
   | Property | Metals | Non-metals |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1. Physical State | Solids at room temperature (except liquid mercury and gallium) | Solids, liquids (bromine), and gases |
   | 2. Lustre | Possess metallic lustre; can be polished | Dull appearance (except lustrous iodine and graphite) |
   | 3. Hardness | Generally hard and strong (except soft Li, Na, K) | Soft and brittle (except diamond, the hardest known substance) |
   | 4. Malleability & Ductility | Highly malleable and ductile (gold and silver highest) | Brittle; non-malleable and non-ductile |
   | 5. Electrical & Thermal Conductivity | Good conductors (silver best, then Cu, Al) | Poor conductors/insulators (except conducting graphite) |
   | 6. Melting & Boiling Points | High melting and boiling points (except Hg, Ga) | Low melting and boiling points (except carbon, silicon, boron) |
   | 7. Density | High density (except Li, Na, K) | Low density |
   | 8. Tensile Strength | High tensile strength | Low tensile strength (break easily) |
   | 9. Sonority | Sonorous (produce ringing sound) | Non-sonorous |

2. Comparison of Chemical Properties:
   | Property | Metals | Non-metals |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1. Nature of Ions | Form positive ions (cations) by losing electrons: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻, Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻ | Form negative ions (anions) by gaining electrons: Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻, O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻ |
   | 2. Nature of Oxides | Form basic oxides (turn red litmus blue): 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O; Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH | Form acidic or neutral oxides: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (Acidic); CO, NO, H₂O (Neutral) |
   | 3. Reaction with Water | Displace hydrogen from water/steam (K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe) | Generally do not react with water (except fluorine: 2F₂ + 2H₂O → 4HF + O₂) |
   | 4. Reaction with Dilute Acids | Metals above H displace H₂ gas: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑ | Do not react with dilute acids; cannot displace H₂ gas |
   | 5. Redox Behavior | Act as reducing agents (readily donate electrons) | Act as oxidising agents (readily accept electrons) |

#### [chem_ch2_ncert_009] You are provided with three iron nails, each dipped in oil, water and vinegar. Which iron nail will not rust, and why?

**Answer:** The iron nail dipped in oil will not rust.
Why: Rusting requires both moisture (water) and oxygen (air). The layer of oil forms an impenetrable barrier that cuts off exposure to atmospheric oxygen and water.
- The nail in water rusts because both water and dissolved oxygen are present.
- The nail in vinegar reacts vigorously with acetic acid, corroding rapidly.

**Explanation:** Oil excludes both air and water, preventing electrochemical rust formation.

#### [chem_ch2_ncert_011] One of the methods of protecting iron from getting rusted is to put a thin coating of zinc metal over it. Since sulphur does not react with water, can it be used for this purpose? Justify your answer.

**Answer:** No, sulphur cannot be used to protect iron from rusting.
Justification:
1. Sulphur is a brittle non-metal that lacks malleability and ductility; it cannot form a flexible, adherent, uniform metallic coating on iron and would easily crumble or peel off.
2. In the presence of air, sulphur slowly oxidises to sulphur dioxide (SO₂), which reacts with moisture to form sulphurous/sulphuric acid, accelerating the corrosion of iron.
3. Zinc is malleable, strongly adheres to iron, and provides sacrificial cathodic protection.

**Explanation:** Coatings must be adherent, durable, and chemically stable against atmospheric oxidation.

*Topic:* `Sulphur Coating vs Galvanization Justification` | *Difficulty:* `medium` | *Marks:* `2`

#### [chem_ch2_ncert_012] An ironsmith heats iron before making tools. Why is heating necessary in this process?

**Explanation:** Thermal activation increases metal ductility and malleability, enabling plastic deformation.

#### [chem_ch2_se_003] A solution of copper sulphate was stored in an iron container. After a few days, some holes were seen in the iron container. Explain the observation.

#### [chem_ch2_la_004] Define displacement reaction. An iron knife kept in blue copper sulphate solution turns the blue solution into light green. Explain.

2. Explanation:

- When an iron knife is placed in an aqueous solution of blue copper sulphate (CuSO₄), iron atoms lose electrons to form ferrous ions (Fe²⁺), entering the solution:

- The original blue color of the solution (due to Cu²⁺ ions) gradually fades and transforms into light green due to the accumulation of newly formed ferrous sulphate (FeSO₄) in solution.

**Explanation:** Redox displacement: Fe(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).
