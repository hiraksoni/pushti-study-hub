## Theoretical Foundations & Grammatical Architecture

### 1. Attributive vs Predicative Positioning
Adjectives occupy two fundamental syntactic positions:
• Attributive Position: Sits directly before the noun it modifies (e.g., 'a hardworking police officer', 'the blue sky', 'an interesting novel').
• Predicative Position: Sits in the predicate following a linking/copular verb (be, seem, look, taste, feel, become, sound) and modifies the subject (e.g., 'The police officer is hardworking'; 'The soup tasted delicious'; 'The exam seemed difficult').

### 2. Comprehensive Kinds of Adjectives
English adjectives are classified into 10 categories:
1. Adjectives of Quality (Descriptive): brave, intelligent, blue, round, heavy, delicious.
2. Adjectives of Quantity (Uncountable): some, much, little, enough, whole, sufficient.
3. Adjectives of Number (Countable): five, several, many, few, first, second, numerous.
4. Demonstrative Adjectives: this, that, these, those, such, certain.
5. Possessive Adjectives: my, our, your, his, her, its, their, whose.
6. Interrogative Adjectives: which book, what time, whose turn.
7. Distributive Adjectives: each child, every student, either side, neither team.
8. Emphasising Adjectives: very, actual, own, pure, utter, absolute.
9. Proper Adjectives: Indian, Victorian, Shakespearean, Italian, Japanese.
10. Compound Adjectives: well-known, ice-cold, open-minded, world-famous.

### 3. Possessive Adjectives vs Possessive Pronouns
• Possessive Adjectives: Always precede and qualify an explicit following noun (my laptop, her jacket, our home, their car).
• Possessive Pronouns: Stand completely independently, replacing the entire noun phrase without an accompanying noun (mine, hers, ours, theirs).
Example comparison: 'This is my book' (my = Possessive Adjective) vs 'This book is mine' (mine = Possessive Pronoun).

### 4. Degrees of Comparison & Irregular Systems
Adjectives exhibit three degrees of comparison:
1. Positive Degree: Denotes simple existence of quality without comparison (tall, easy, beautiful).
2. Comparative Degree: Compares two persons, things, or sets using '-er' or 'more' followed by 'than' (taller, easier, more beautiful).
3. Superlative Degree: Expresses the highest intensity among three or more entities, preceded by 'the' and taking '-est' or 'most' (the tallest, the easiest, the most beautiful).
• Irregular Comparisons: good/well -> better -> best; bad/ill -> worse -> worst; little -> less -> least; much/many -> more -> most; far -> farther/further -> farthest/furthest; old -> older/elder -> oldest/eldest.

### 5. Order of Adjectives (OSASCOMP Mnemonic)
When multiple descriptive adjectives precede a single noun, standard English adheres to a conventional order:
1. Opinion / Observation: beautiful, lovely, ugly, brilliant, wonderful.
2. Size: large, tiny, enormous, small, gigantic.
3. Age: ancient, antique, young, new, vintage, modern.
4. Shape: round, square, oval, flat, spherical.
5. Colour: red, black, golden, blue, black-and-white.
6. Origin: Italian, Indian, Greek, French, Asian.
7. Material: wooden, leather, silk, metal, ceramic.
8. Purpose: dining (table), sleeping (bag), running (shoes).
Example: 'A lovely (1) small (2) ancient (3) round (4) black (5) Italian (6) leather (7) travel (8) bag'.

- **Answer**: The formation of comparative and superlative degrees in English is morphophonologically determined by syllable count and historical stem evolution:
1. Monosyllabic (One-Syllable) Adjectives:

2. Disyllabic Adjectives Ending in '-y':
• The terminal 'y' mutates to 'i', appending '-er' and '-est' (e.g., heavy -> heavier -> heaviest; happy -> happier -> happiest).

3. Poly-syllabic (Two or More Syllables) Adjectives:
• Prefix periphrastic 'more' for comparative and 'most' for superlative without altering the base adjective (e.g., intelligent -> more intelligent -> most intelligent; remarkable -> more remarkable -> most remarkable).

4. Irregular Mutation Comparisons:
• Root vowel changes or suppletive stems preserved from Old English:
  - good/well -> better -> best
  - bad/ill -> worse -> worst
  - far -> farther/further -> farthest/furthest
  - little -> less -> least
